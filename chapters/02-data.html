3  The Market

The question. What does this market look like, and which of its properties can you actually rely on?

Every later chapter in this book takes a return series and does something to it: estimates a moment, imposes a constraint, holds a portfolio through a crisis. None of that is interpretable without knowing what the series are. This chapter is the reference the rest of the book points back to.

The market is a simulated one. Five regions — the United States, Europe, the United Kingdom, Switzerland and Japan — each with equities, government bonds, credit and a money market, plus a global commodity block. Weekly and monthly from December 1991 to December 2022, everything in US dollars and unhedged. It is licensed to nobody, so it ships with the package, and every number printed below can be reproduced on your machine without an account anywhere.

3.1 What you need

Three tables carry the whole book. Their shape is the shape everything else expects.

returns  <- sim_class_returns_monthly     # date, class, return, index
bill     <- sim_riskfree_monthly          # date, rate_annual, return
assets   <- sim_assets                    # one row per instrument

dplyr::glimpse(returns, width = 72)
#> Rows: 7,812
#> Columns: 4
#> $ date   <date> 1992-01-31, 1992-01-31, 1992-01-31, 1992-01-31, 1992-0…
#> $ class  <chr> "CH_credit", "CH_equity", "CH_government", "CH_money_ma…
#> $ return <dbl> -0.031184, 0.035114, -0.032313, -0.049120, -0.029620, 0…
#> $ index  <dbl> 96.88, 103.51, 96.77, 95.09, 97.04, 109.21, 97.60, 96.3…

Twenty-one class series, 372 complete months, no gaps. Underneath them sit 181 individual instruments in sim_asset_returns_monthly, which is the same market at a finer grain and the universe the chapters about estimation error use.

PortfolioAnalytics, which occupies most of this book, wants none of this shape. It wants a wide xts matrix, and it will not convert for you.

R  <- as_xts_pm(returns, date, class, return)
rf <- bill$return
dim(R)
#> [1] 372  21

That is the boundary, and this book crosses it explicitly every time rather than letting a helper do it invisibly.

3.1.1 The moments

moments <- tibble::tibble(
  series   = colnames(R),
  ret      = apply(R, 2, ann_ret),
  vol      = apply(R, 2, ann_vol),
  sharpe   = (apply(R, 2, ann_ret) - ann_ret(rf)) / apply(R, 2, ann_vol),
  skew     = as.numeric(PerformanceAnalytics::skewness(R)),
  exkurt   = as.numeric(PerformanceAnalytics::kurtosis(R, method = "excess")),
  maxdd    = apply(R, 2, function(x) { i <- cumprod(1 + x); min(i / cummax(i)) - 1 })
) |>
  dplyr::arrange(dplyr::desc(sharpe))

Three things in that table are worth stopping on.

The return ranking is not the Sharpe ranking. US_credit earns 5.7% against US_equity’s 7.8%, and yet it ranks above it on Sharpe — 0.47 against 0.30 — because its volatility is a third of the equity number. Whether that makes it the better holding is the subject of sec-02-data-why, and the answer is not obvious.

The money market is not cash. US_money_market has a Sharpe of -0.66. A negative Sharpe ratio for an instrument whose name says money market is not a rounding artefact:

mm <- as.numeric(R[, "US_money_market"])
c(negative_months = mean(mm < 0),
  bill_negative   = mean(rf < 0),
  mm_drawdown     = moments$maxdd[moments$series == "US_money_market"])
#>             negative_months               bill_negative 
#>                    0.384409                    0.002688 
#> mm_drawdown.US_money_market 
#>                   -0.104657

It is a three-month instrument marked to market off a fitted curve, and it loses money in 38% of months against the bill’s 0.3%. Treat it as a short-duration asset and use sim_riskfree_monthly as the risk-free rate. Every risk-free figure in this book comes from there.

Currency is a first-order driver. The foreign money markets have volatilities near ten percent. A three-month instrument has almost no duration, so unhedged in dollars it is very nearly a pure currency position — sec-02-data-why takes that apart.

3.2 How to actually do it

The moments table above is what every description of a return series looks like. It is also where the trouble starts, because two of those six columns are routinely used as if they were properties of the asset, and they are not.

3.2.1 Test the distribution rather than assuming it

Almost every risk number in finance is a quantile, and almost every quantile is computed as though returns were Gaussian. Whether that is defensible is a question with an answer, and the answer is different for different assets here.

jb <- vapply(colnames(R), function(s) {
  x <- as.numeric(R[, s]); n <- length(x)
  s3 <- mean((x - mean(x))^3) / stats::sd(x)^3
  s4 <- mean((x - mean(x))^4) / stats::sd(x)^4 - 3
  stat <- n / 6 * (s3^2 + s4^2 / 4)
  stats::pchisq(stat, df = 2, lower.tail = FALSE)
}, numeric(1))

sum(jb < 0.05)                      # of 21
#> [1] 20
names(jb)[jb >= 0.05]               # the survivors
#> [1] "JP_equity"

20 of 21 series reject normality at the five percent level. The single survivor is the one nobody would guess: JP_equity, at 22.8% volatility the most violent risky series in the set, is also the closest to Gaussian — its skewness is 0.04 and its excess kurtosis 0.62. Volatility and non-normality are different things, and this table is the proof.

The non-normality lives in credit, not in equity:

For US_credit the two disagree by a factor of 2.2. For US_equity they disagree by a factor of 1.15. A Gaussian risk model is roughly right for equities and roughly half right for credit — and credit is the series that looked best in Table tbl-02-moments.

Note which argument does the work. PerformanceAnalytics::ES() defaults to method = "modified", so unless you say otherwise you are already getting the Cornish-Fisher figure rather than the historical one. That is the better default and almost nobody knows it is the default.

3.2.2 Moments are not equally estimable

The second habit worth breaking is treating all four moments as though they were the same kind of number. They are not, and the difference is measurable on a rolling window.

win <- 60L
risky <- colnames(R)[!grepl("money_market", colnames(R))]

roll_stat <- function(x, f) zoo::rollapply(x, win, f, align = "right")

instab <- lapply(risky, function(s) {
  x <- as.numeric(R[, s])
  full <- c(mean = mean(x), vol = stats::sd(x),
            skew = mean((x - mean(x))^3) / stats::sd(x)^3)
  rw <- list(mean = roll_stat(x, mean), vol = roll_stat(x, stats::sd),
             skew = roll_stat(x, function(z)
               mean((z - mean(z))^3) / stats::sd(z)^3))
  tibble::tibble(
    series = s, moment = names(full),
    rel_iqr   = vapply(names(full), function(m)
      as.numeric(stats::IQR(rw[[m]]) / abs(full[[m]])), numeric(1)),
    sign_flip = vapply(names(full), function(m)
      mean(sign(rw[[m]]) != sign(full[[m]])), numeric(1))
  )
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

instab |>
  dplyr::group_by(moment) |>
  dplyr::summarise(median_rel_iqr = median(rel_iqr),
                   median_sign_flip = median(sign_flip))
#> # A tibble: 3 × 3
#>   moment median_rel_iqr median_sign_flip
#>   <chr>           <dbl>            <dbl>
#> 1 mean            1.41             0.196
#> 2 skew            1.53             0.342
#> 3 vol             0.301            0

Read the second column first. Across 16 risky series and 313 overlapping sixty-month windows, the rolling volatility never once disagrees in sign with its full-sample value — it cannot, being a positive quantity — and its interquartile range is about a third of its own level. The rolling mean wanders by more than its own level. And the rolling skewness disagrees in sign with the full-sample skewness in roughly a third of all windows.

That is the practical statement this book will lean on repeatedly:

Volatility is estimable. The mean is not. Skewness will regularly tell you the opposite of the truth.

3.3 What else is in there

Maximum drawdown is the number everyone quotes. PerformanceAnalytics will give it to you in one call, and it is the wrong call.

PerformanceAnalytics::table.Drawdowns(R[, "US_government"], top = 3)
#> # A tibble: 3 × 7
#>   From       Trough     To           Depth Length `To Trough` Recovery
#>   <date>     <date>     <date>       <dbl>  <dbl>       <dbl>    <dbl>
#> 1 2020-08-31 2022-10-31 NA         -0.156      30          27       NA
#> 2 1993-10-31 1994-11-30 1995-05-31 -0.0767     20          14        6
#> 3 2009-01-31 2009-06-30 2010-06-30 -0.0569     18           6       12

table.Drawdowns() returns the individual episodes: when each began, when it bottomed, when it ended, and — the column that matters — how long recovery took. A NA in Recovery means the series never got back.

still_down <- vapply(colnames(R), function(s) {
  d <- PerformanceAnalytics::table.Drawdowns(R[, s], top = 1)
  is.na(d$Recovery[1])
}, logical(1))
names(still_down)[still_down]
#>  [1] "EU_government"    "EU_money_market"  "GLOBAL_commodity" "JP_credit"       
#>  [5] "JP_government"    "JP_money_market"  "UK_government"    "UK_money_market" 
#>  [9] "US_government"    "US_money_market"

10 of the 21 series end the sample inside their deepest drawdown — they had not recovered by December 2022. A single maxDrawdown() call reports the same depth for a series that recovered in eight months and one that never recovered at all. The episode table distinguishes them, and it costs one extra argument.

3.4 Why it works

Two properties of this market explain most of what happens in the rest of the book. Neither is visible in a table of moments.

3.4.1 Fat tails are events, not a distribution

Table tbl-02-moments reports an excess kurtosis for each series separately, which invites the reading that each series independently has a fat tail. That reading is wrong, and the error matters enormously for a portfolio, because independent fat tails diversify and shared ones do not.

Rr  <- R[, risky]
z   <- scale(Rr)
hit <- rowSums(abs(z) > 2.5)
p   <- mean(abs(z) > 2.5)

obs <- table(factor(hit, levels = 0:ncol(Rr)))
exp <- stats::dbinom(0:ncol(Rr), ncol(Rr), p) * nrow(Rr)

tibble::tibble(k = 0:ncol(Rr), observed = as.integer(obs), expected = round(exp, 2)) |>
  dplyr::filter(k >= 3, observed > 0)
#> # A tibble: 6 × 3
#>       k observed expected
#>   <int>    <int>    <dbl>
#> 1     3        6     1.7 
#> 2     4        4     0.13
#> 3     5        2     0.01
#> 4     8        1     0   
#> 5    10        1     0   
#> 6    13        1     0

Months in which five or more of the 16 risky series moved beyond two and a half standard deviations: 5 observed, against 0.01 expected if the series were independent. The excess is not marginal; it is a different phenomenon.

worst <- names(sort(hit, decreasing = TRUE))[1]
w <- zoo::index(Rr)[which.max(hit)]
c(month = format(w), series_beyond_2.5sd = max(hit), of = ncol(Rr))
#>               month series_beyond_2.5sd                  of 
#>        "2008-10-31"                "13"                "16"

In Oktober 2008, 13 of the 16 risky series moved beyond two and a half standard deviations at once. The fat tail in this market is not twenty-one fat tails. It is three or four events, and on those dates almost everything moves together.

3.4.2 The crises are not one crisis

The book will keep three episodes apart, because they are economically different, and a lesson learned from one of them is false in another.

ep <- list(
  GFC   = c("2008-09-01", "2009-03-31"),
  COVID = c("2020-02-01", "2020-04-30"),
  rates = c("2022-01-01", "2022-12-31")
)
cum <- vapply(ep, function(w) {
  sel <- zoo::index(Rr) >= as.Date(w[1]) & zoo::index(Rr) <= as.Date(w[2])
  apply(Rr[sel, ], 2, function(x) prod(1 + x) - 1)
}, numeric(ncol(Rr)))
round(100 * cum[c("US_equity", "US_government", "JP_government",
                  "US_credit", "EU_credit", "GLOBAL_commodity"), ], 1)
#>                    GFC COVID rates
#> US_equity        -45.6 -12.5 -17.7
#> US_government      7.5   6.2 -10.8
#> JP_government     14.2   2.2 -16.1
#> US_credit         -9.1  -2.1 -15.2
#> EU_credit        -17.7 -11.5 -23.0
#> GLOBAL_commodity -19.6  -1.9  11.8

Through the global financial crisis, government bonds did what the textbook says they do: US_government gained 7.5% while US_equity lost 45.6%. Through 2022 the same bond lost 10.8% — and the only series in the risky set that gained was the one which had lost most in the other two episodes.

names(which(cum[, "rates"] > 0))
#> [1] "GLOBAL_commodity"

A book that teaches diversification from 2008 alone teaches a rule that reverses in a third of the sample. Which is why every backtest in sec-22-backtesting-why reports these episodes separately rather than averaging over them.

The correlation matrix says the same thing, and says it in the way an optimiser will feel it:

mean_cor <- function(idx) {
  C <- stats::cor(Rr[idx, ]); mean(C[upper.tri(C)])
}
calm <- rep(TRUE, nrow(Rr))
for (w in ep) calm[zoo::index(Rr) >= as.Date(w[1]) &
                   zoo::index(Rr) <= as.Date(w[2])] <- FALSE

c(calm = mean_cor(calm),
  GFC  = mean_cor(zoo::index(Rr) >= as.Date(ep$GFC[1]) &
                  zoo::index(Rr) <= as.Date(ep$GFC[2])))
#>   calm    GFC 
#> 0.3021 0.5109

Average pairwise correlation rises from 0.30 in calm months to 0.51 through the crisis. The diversification you optimised for in the calm sample is not the diversification you have when you need it. Note also that the crisis window is seven months: a correlation matrix estimated on seven observations is a description, not an estimate, and the cumulative returns above carry far more weight than the correlations do.

3.4.3 Currency is not one decision

Everything here is unhedged in dollars, so a foreign holding carries its local return and its currency’s move together. How much of it is currency depends on the asset, not on the country — and that is the non-obvious part.

fx <- sim_fx_monthly
share <- function(series, region) {
  u <- as.numeric(R[, series])
  f <- fx$fx_return[fx$region == region][
        match(zoo::index(R), fx$date[fx$region == region])]
  c(unhedged  = ann_vol(u),
    hedged    = ann_vol(u - f),
    fx_share  = 1 - stats::var(u - f) / stats::var(u),
    if_indep  = stats::var(f) / stats::var(u))
}
round(rbind(
  JP_government = share("JP_government", "JP"),
  JP_equity     = share("JP_equity", "JP"),
  CH_equity     = share("CH_equity", "CH"),
  EU_credit     = share("EU_credit", "EU")
), 3)
#>               unhedged hedged fx_share if_indep
#> JP_government    0.107  0.031    0.918    0.885
#> JP_equity        0.228  0.227    0.009    0.196
#> CH_equity        0.206  0.196    0.089    0.250
#> EU_credit        0.135  0.077    0.676    0.498

Hedging JP_government takes its volatility from 10.7% to 3.1%. Hedging JP_equity takes it from 22.8% to 22.7% — which is to say, it does nothing at all.

The if_indep column explains why. If the local return and the currency were unrelated, the currency’s share of variance would be its own variance over the total. For JP_equity that benchmark is 20% and the measured share is 1%. The gap is a negative correlation between the local market and its own currency: the Japanese market rises when the yen falls. The hedge is already there, supplied by the economics rather than by a forward contract. For EU_credit the correlation runs the other way and hedging removes more than the currency’s own share.

So “should I hedge?” is not one question with one answer for a currency. It is a different question for the bond sleeve and the equity sleeve of the same country, and sec-13-sleeves-hands-on treats it as such.

Exercises

  1. sim_class_returns_weekly holds the same market at the model’s native frequency. Recompute Table tbl-02-moments on it, annualising by \(\sqrt{52}\) instead of \(\sqrt{12}\). Which of the six columns change materially, and which do not? Explain the pattern in terms of what aggregation does to a sum of returns.
  2. The exceedance count above used a threshold of 2.5 standard deviations. Repeat it at 2, 3 and 4. At which threshold does the excess over independence first appear, and what does that tell you about where in the distribution the dependence lives?
  3. Pick any two series with similar volatility but opposite skewness — for example US_credit and JP_government. Construct the equally weighted combination and compute its skewness. Is it the average of the two? Explain.
  4. Using sim_fx_monthly, build the fully hedged version of all sixteen risky series and recompute the average pairwise correlation in calm and crisis windows. Does hedging change the diversification of the set, or only its volatility?
