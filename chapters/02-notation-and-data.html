2  Notation and Data

Draft|Code: script · exercises · solutions · all chapters|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

What do the symbols mean, and where do the numbers come from? Every chapter of this book manipulates the same handful of objects, a matrix of returns, a vector of weights, a mean vector and a covariance matrix, and Markowitz (1952) needed all four before he could state the problem the rest of the book solves. Following Coqueret and Guida (2020) and Scheuch et al. (2023), whose books open the same way, we fix the notation once here so that no later chapter redefines a symbol, and we describe the two sources of data once so that no later chapter has to explain what it is computing on.

We first fix the notation and the conventions for annualizing, then describe the modeled market on which every chapter runs, its calendar and the choices that went into building it, then the real-data layer the book reaches for when a claim has to be about real markets, and finally the vocabulary that maps the names in the data onto the names the industry uses. This chapter sits on neither side of the strategic–tactical distinction that Section 3.1 defines; it is the ground both stand on.

library(xts)
library(PortfolioAnalytics)
library(PerformanceAnalytics)

2.1 Notation

Vectors are bold lower case, matrices are bold capitals, and a prime denotes the transpose. There are \(N\) assets observed over \(T\) periods. The object everything else is computed from is the \(T \times N\) matrix of simple returns \(\boldsymbol{R}\), whose element \(r_{t,i}\) is the return on asset \(i\) over period \(t\),

\[ r_{t,i} = \frac{P_{t,i} + D_{t,i}}{P_{t-1,i}} - 1, \tag{2.1}\]

with \(P\) the price and \(D\) whatever the asset paid out during the period, so that every return in this book is a total return. Simple returns rather than log returns, because a portfolio’s simple return is the weighted average of its constituents’ simple returns and that linearity is what makes Equation 2.2 below true; log returns aggregate over time and not across assets, and the book aggregates across assets far more often.

The risk-free rate over period \(t\) is \(r_{f,t}\), the return on a Treasury bill bought at the start of the period and held to its end, and it is the only return in the book that is known at the start of the period it applies to. Excess returns, \(r_{t,i} - r_{f,t}\), are the default basis throughout. Every mean, covariance, Sharpe ratio and optimizer input is computed on excess returns unless a chapter says otherwise, and the risk-free asset enters an optimization as the origin of the return axis rather than as a column of the return matrix. Section 3.1 argues why: a constant column makes the covariance matrix singular, a tangency portfolio exists only relative to a risk-free rate in the sense of Tobin (1958), and only the excess formulation stays coherent when weights are allowed to sum to less than one. Here we only state the convention.

The portfolio is the vector \(\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N\) of weights, one per asset, and \(\boldsymbol{\iota}\) is the vector of \(N\) ones, so that \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\iota} = 1\) says the portfolio is fully invested. The vector of expected excess returns is \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and the covariance matrix of excess returns is \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). Both are population quantities; an estimate of either carries a hat, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\), and the distinction matters more in this book than in most, because Part III is about the gap between the two. The portfolio’s expected excess return and variance are

\[ \mu_{\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu}, \qquad \sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}, \tag{2.2}\]

and their ratio \(\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}} / \sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}}\) is the Sharpe ratio of Sharpe (1966), which on the excess basis needs no rate subtracted from it. Section 9.1 takes Equation 2.2 as its starting point.

2.1.1 Annualizing

Returns arrive monthly and are reported annually, and the book uses two conventions for the conversion, each in its place. Under the first, a monthly mean is multiplied by twelve and a monthly standard deviation by \(\sqrt{12}\), so that a monthly Sharpe ratio is multiplied by \(\sqrt{12}\); this is exact for the mean and for the variance when returns are uncorrelated over time, and Lo (2002) gives the correction when they are not. It is the convention for anything that enters or leaves an optimizer, because \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) are arithmetic objects. Under the second, the annual return over a sample of \(T\) months is the compounded one,

\[ \bar{r}_{\text{ann}} = \Big(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1 + r_t)\Big)^{12/T} - 1, \tag{2.3}\]

which is what an investor who held the series actually earned per year. It is the convention for any table describing what a series did over the sample, and for cumulative returns over an episode. Per period the compounded mean falls short of the arithmetic one by roughly half the variance, and on a volatile series that gap is not a rounding artifact: it is the variance drag, and Section 9.2 returns to it. We say which convention a number follows wherever it could matter.

The right-hand column of Table 2.1 is a convention too. Every chapter’s first code block builds the same three objects and nothing else, because universe decisions are taken once, at import, and the excess-return basis is argued once, in chapter 3. A reader who has seen the block below has seen every data-preparation step the book performs:

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)   # total returns
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return                                  # the bill
Re <- R - rf                                                       # excess returns
stopifnot(ncol(R) == 16L, nrow(R) == length(rf), !anyNA(R))

as_xts_pm() is the boundary between the tidy world, where the data are stored one row per asset per date in the sense of Wickham et al. (2023), and the wide xts matrix PortfolioAnalytics requires; Section 1.2 explains the crossing and the three ways it fails. The stopifnot() is the book’s habit rather than decoration: wherever two objects must line up and nothing in the language enforces it, the invariant is stated in code, so that a mismatch fails the render rather than producing a plausible number.

As code, the two conventions are two lines, and every table in the book uses them:

ann_ret <- function(x, p = 12) prod(1 + x)^(p / length(x)) - 1   # compounded
ann_vol <- function(x, p = 12) stats::sd(x) * sqrt(p)             # sqrt-of-time
NoteWorked: annualizing one series three ways

Take US_equity. Its monthly sample mean and standard deviation are

x   <- as.numeric(R[, "US_equity"])
m   <- mean(x)
s   <- stats::sd(x)
g   <- prod(1 + x)^(1 / length(x)) - 1          # compounded monthly mean
round(c(monthly_mean = m, monthly_sd = s, months = length(x)), 5)
#> monthly_mean   monthly_sd       months 
#>      0.01128      0.04514    372.00000

so the arithmetic annual mean is \(12 \times 0.01128 = 0.1354\), the annual volatility is \(\sqrt{12} \times 0.04514 = 0.1564\), and the compounded return of Equation 2.3 is

\[ \bar{r}_{\text{ann}} = \Big(\prod_{t=1}^{372}(1 + r_t)\Big)^{12/372} - 1 = 44.6152^{\,0.03226} - 1 = 0.1303 . \]

Per month, the arithmetic mean exceeds the compounded mean, \(44.6152^{\,1/372} - 1 = 0.01026\), by 0.00102, which is half the monthly variance, \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.04514^2 = 0.00102\). That is the variance drag. Between the two annual figures the gap is smaller, 0.0051 against half the annual variance of 0.0122, because the compounded figure also earns return on return within the year, 0.0072, and twelve times a monthly mean does not. The same arithmetic in code:

round(c(arithmetic = 12 * m, compounded = ann_ret(x), volatility = ann_vol(x),
        monthly_gap = m - g, half_monthly_variance = 0.5 * s^2,
        annual_gap = 12 * m - ann_ret(x), half_annual_variance = 0.5 * ann_vol(x)^2,
        return_on_return = (1 + g)^12 - 1 - 12 * g), 5)
#>            arithmetic            compounded            volatility 
#>               0.13541               0.13034               0.15638 
#>           monthly_gap half_monthly_variance            annual_gap 
#>               0.00102               0.00102               0.00507 
#>  half_annual_variance      return_on_return 
#>               0.01223               0.00719

2.2 The Modeled Market

The default data of this book is a modeled market, and it contains no real data. Factor models for government bonds, credit, equities and commodities were estimated by historical simulation on market data from 1992 to 2022; the factors were replayed, the idiosyncratic components drawn afresh, and the asset paths reassembled, so that no series is the history of a real security. The market therefore runs on its own calendar, model years 1 to 31. Its stress episodes are named for what happens in them and ship as the table sim_episodes. And because every series is the output of a factor model, the market can be re-run with its factors shocked, which a history cannot, and Appendix H does so.

Because it contains no real data, the whole market ships inside tidypm and every number in this book reproduces offline, for every reader. The risk-free rate, a three-month Treasury bill, and the exchange rates enter the model as inputs. What the estimation on market data buys is realism of structure: the stress episodes arrive in the right places and with the right relative magnitudes, which Section 4.3 relies on.

2.2.1 The model calendar

Because the data are modeled rather than recorded, a date in this market is a position on the model’s own calendar, not a point in history. The dates are stored as ordinary Date objects, so that xts needs no special cases, but their years are not calendar years. Four tools translate them, the same four in every chapter: model_year() and in_episode() from tidypm, and fmt_model() and scale_x_model() from the book’s setup script, R/_common.R.

model_year() turns a date into its model year, and fmt_model() writes a date the way the prose does:

d <- zoo::index(R)                                   # the month ends of the sample
range(d)                                             # as stored
#> [1] "2101-01-31" "2131-12-31"
range(model_year(d))                                 # as read
#> [1]  1 31
fmt_model(range(d))                                  # as written
#> [1] "January of model year 1"   "December of model year 31"
worst <- d[which.min(as.numeric(R[, "US_equity"]))]  # a position is derived, never typed
fmt_model(worst)
#> [1] "March of model year 29"

The sample is thus 372 months, model years 1 to 31, and a point in it is named the way the worst month of US_equity is: March of model year 29, or simply model year 29. The stress episodes are the rows of sim_episodes, shown in Table 2.2, and the text calls each by its label.

in_episode() returns a logical vector that is TRUE on the dates of one episode, so that no chapter types a window:

crisis <- in_episode(d, "credit_crisis")
in_crisis <- c(US_equity     = prod(1 + R[crisis, "US_equity"]) - 1,
               US_government = prod(1 + R[crisis, "US_government"]) - 1)
stopifnot(in_crisis[["US_equity"]] < 0, in_crisis[["US_government"]] > 0)
c(months = sum(crisis), round(in_crisis, 3))
#>        months     US_equity US_government 
#>         7.000        -0.279         0.082

Over the 7 months of the credit crisis US_equity loses 28% and US_government gains 8%. On a time axis, scale_x_model() takes the place of the date scale and labels model years, as in Figure 2.1.

sim_class_returns_monthly |>
  dplyr::filter(class == "US_equity") |>
  ggplot2::ggplot(ggplot2::aes(date, index)) +
  ggplot2::geom_rect(data = sim_episodes, ggplot2::aes(xmin = start, xmax = end),
                     ymin = -Inf, ymax = Inf, inherit.aes = FALSE,

The convention applies to the modeled market only. The literature keeps its dates, and so do the real-data sleeves of Section 2.3.

2.2.2 The class universe

The allocation spine of the book is the table sim_class_returns_monthly: sixteen risky class series, five currencies (US, UK, EU, CH, JP) times three classes (government, credit, equity) plus one global commodity index, over 372 complete months, the 31 model years, everything in US dollars and unhedged. A weekly counterpart, sim_class_returns_weekly, holds the same market at the model’s native frequency, on 1618 weekly dates.

colnames(R)
#>  [1] "CH_credit"        "CH_equity"        "CH_government"    "EU_credit"       
#>  [5] "EU_equity"        "EU_government"    "GLOBAL_commodity" "JP_credit"       
#>  [9] "JP_equity"        "JP_government"    "UK_credit"        "UK_equity"       
#> [13] "UK_government"    "US_credit"        "US_equity"        "US_government"

Five money-market series, one per region, ship beside the universe as sim_money_market_monthly and are deliberately not in it. Each is the 0.25-year point on a curve fitted to that region’s two, five and ten-year yields, marked to market: a short-duration bond rather than a bill held to maturity, and so not the risk-free rate either. One calculation settles the point:

M  <- as_xts_pm(sim_money_market_monthly, date, class, return)
stopifnot(all(as.Date(zoo::index(M)) == as.Date(zoo::index(R))))
mm <- as.numeric(M[, "US_money_market"])

round(c(negative_months_money_market = mean(mm < 0),
        negative_months_bill         = mean(rf < 0),
        correlation                  = stats::cor(mm, rf)), 3)
#> negative_months_money_market         negative_months_bill 
#>                        0.188                        0.003 
#>                  correlation 
#>                        0.930

US_money_market tracks the bill at a correlation of 0.93, and is negative in 19% of months against the bill’s 0.3%. A risk-free rate has to be riskless over the holding period. The four foreign money markets have almost no duration and are, unhedged in dollars, nearly pure currency positions. The money markets are there for the chapters that need a short-duration asset or a currency exposure; sim_riskfree_monthly is the risk-free rate everywhere.

Table 2.3 gives the three numbers a reader needs to place each series. The return is the compounded figure of Equation 2.3, the Sharpe ratio divides its excess over the bill’s compounded return by the volatility, and Section 4.2 carries the full table with skewness, kurtosis and drawdowns.

2.2.3 How the class series are built

Each equity class is the value-weighted return of its constituent securities, weighted on their market capitalization at the previous weekly observation, never the contemporaneous one, so no portfolio is formed on information it could not have had; the capitalizations are sim_marketcap_weekly, which reaches back before model year 1 so that a prior week exists even for the first return date. Government, credit and commodity classes are equally weighted, because no capitalization exists for a constant-maturity bond or a commodity index. The weekly frequency is the model’s own, and a monthly class return is the compounded weekly return within the month: every monthly date is a month end, every week lies in the month it compounds into, and the monthly series is the weekly-rebalanced portfolio observed at month ends, not a monthly-rebalanced one. That can be checked rather than believed:

weekly_compounded <- sim_class_returns_weekly |>
  dplyr::mutate(month = lubridate::floor_date(date, "month")) |>
  dplyr::group_by(class, month) |>
  dplyr::summarise(compounded = prod(1 + return) - 1, .groups = "drop")

both <- sim_class_returns_monthly |>
  dplyr::mutate(month = lubridate::floor_date(date, "month")) |>
  dplyr::inner_join(weekly_compounded, by = c("class", "month"))

stopifnot(nrow(both) == nrow(sim_class_returns_monthly),
          all(both$date == lubridate::ceiling_date(both$month, "month") - 1),   # month ends
          min(sim_marketcap_weekly$date) < min(sim_class_returns_weekly$date)) # a prior week
max(abs(both$return - both$compounded))
#> [1] 0.0000000000000007945

2.2.4 The instrument universe

Underneath the sixteen classes sit 181 individual instruments, described one per row in sim_assets and carried at both frequencies in sim_asset_returns_monthly and sim_asset_returns_weekly. This is the same market at a finer grain and the universe of the sleeve chapters of Part VI: Section 21.1 selects within the equity block and Section 22.1 within the bonds.

n_by_class <- table(sim_assets$asset_class)
c(sectors = dplyr::n_distinct(stats::na.omit(sim_assets$sector)),
  grades  = dplyr::n_distinct(stats::na.omit(sim_assets$credit_grade)))
#> sectors  grades 
#>       8       2

The 123 equities are individual securities, each tagged with one of 8 sectors; the 30 credit instruments are three maturities in two grades per region, IG and HY; the 15 government bonds are zero-coupon instruments held at constant two, five and ten-year maturities; the 8 commodities are a broad index and seven sub-indices; and the 5 money-market instruments are the class series themselves, one per region. Identifiers are anonymized, US_EQU_01 and EU_CRE_03 and so on: a region, an asset class and a running number.

2.2.5 Exchange rates, curves, capitalizations and rating changes

Three further tables let a chapter show what moved a series rather than only its return, and a fourth carries sustainability information. sim_fx_monthly and sim_fx_weekly hold the exchange rate of each of the four non-dollar currencies, quoted as dollars per unit of the local currency, so that a positive fx_return means the local currency appreciated and an unhedged dollar investor gained. Because every series is unhedged in dollars, a foreign holding carries its local return and its currency’s move together, and the hedged return is one subtraction away:

\[ r^{\text{hedged}}_{t} = r^{\text{unhedged}}_{t} - f_{t}, \tag{2.4}\]

with \(f_t\) the matching fx_return. That is the whole of currency hedging as this book implements it; whether to hedge, and how much, is the question of Perold and Schulman (1988) and Campbell et al. (2010), which Section 4.3 opens and Section 15.2 settles for the strategic portfolio.

fx <- sim_fx_monthly
hedged_vol <- function(series, region) {
  f <- fx[fx$region == region, ]
  stopifnot(all(f$date == as.Date(zoo::index(R))))      # same grid, same order
  u <- as.numeric(R[, series])
  c(unhedged = ann_vol(u), hedged = ann_vol(u - f$fx_return))
}
regions <- c("UK", "EU", "CH", "JP")
gov <- t(sapply(regions, function(r) hedged_vol(paste0(r, "_government"), r)))
rownames(gov) <- paste0(regions, "_government")
jp_equity <- hedged_vol("JP_equity", "JP")
round(rbind(gov, JP_equity = jp_equity), 3)
#>               unhedged hedged
#> UK_government    0.095  0.048
#> EU_government    0.098  0.039
#> CH_government    0.109  0.033
#> JP_government    0.107  0.031
#> JP_equity        0.196  0.212

Hedging takes JP_government from 10.7% to 3.1% and does not lower JP_equity at all, which moves from 19.6% to 21.2%; Section 4.3 explains why the two differ.

sim_curve_weekly holds the drivers of the bond block: for each region the level, slope and curvature of a Nelson and Siegel (1987) yield curve, in the dynamic form of Diebold and Li (2006) which Section 22.2 takes up, plus an investment-grade and a high-yield credit spread per region, ten spreads in all.

sort(unique(sim_curve_weekly$series))
#>  [1] "CH_Curvature" "CH_HY_Spread" "CH_IG_Spread" "CH_Level"     "CH_Slope"    
#>  [6] "EU_Curvature" "EU_HY_Spread" "EU_IG_Spread" "EU_Level"     "EU_Slope"    
#> [11] "JP_Curvature" "JP_HY_Spread" "JP_IG_Spread" "JP_Level"     "JP_Slope"    
#> [16] "UK_Curvature" "UK_HY_Spread" "UK_IG_Spread" "UK_Level"     "UK_Slope"    
#> [21] "US_Curvature" "US_HY_Spread" "US_IG_Spread" "US_Level"     "US_Slope"

sim_marketcap_weekly holds the weekly market capitalization of the 123 equities, which is what a capitalization-weighted benchmark needs and what Section 21.2 builds one from.

sim_esg_changes_quarterly records changes in an ESG rating of the equities, one row per change, dated at the quarter end: 1113 integer changes on 122 of the 123 equities, from model year 11 on. The table carries changes only and no starting level, so cumulating them gives a rating up to an unknown constant, and the changes have no effect on the returns; Section 20.2 works with them.

2.2.6 Three caveats, one departure, and a frozen version

Three properties of the market are consequences of how it is built, and a reader should know them before modeling on it.

Currency dominates foreign bonds. Hedging removes on average 63% of a foreign government bond’s dollar volatility, as the calculation above shows, and a foreign money market is almost entirely currency. Only US and GLOBAL series are free of it.

The bond block is nearly rank-deficient at instrument level. Three maturities per region are priced off a three-factor curve, and the two credit grades at each maturity share one credit factor, so some bond combinations are near-exact replicas of others. The covariance matrix of the 181 instruments is technically invertible and practically not:

A <- as_xts_pm(sim_asset_returns_monthly, date, asset, return)
stopifnot(ncol(A) == nrow(sim_assets), setequal(colnames(A), sim_assets$asset_id))

log10_condition <- function(X) {
  e <- eigen(stats::cov(X), symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
  log10(max(e) / min(e))
}
round(c(sixteen_classes = log10_condition(R), instruments = log10_condition(A)), 1)
#> sixteen_classes     instruments 
#>             4.1            12.2

A condition number of \(10^{12.2}\) means that unconstrained mean-variance at instrument level produces nonsense, which is worth demonstrating rather than preventing, and it is why the sleeve chapters shrink the matrix in the manner of Ledoit and Wolf (2004) or constrain the weights. At class level the number is \(10^{4.1}\) and the problem does not exist; Section 7.1 is about the difference.

Equity series are single securities. The median annualized volatility of the 123 names is 31%, which is realistic for one security, but a portfolio of these names diversifies more readily than a real index would, because the names are few and their idiosyncratic components are drawn independently of one another.

One departure from the estimated model is deliberate and is stated as a parameter rather than hidden. Every euro-area equity carries an expected-return overlay of three percentage points a year, added as a constant drift. It is a capital-market assumption, not a measurement, and the sample says why it was needed:

overlay <- 0.03
eq      <- classes[classes$kind == "equity", ]
vol     <- setNames(eq$vol, eq$class)
without <- setNames(eq$ret - ifelse(eq$class == "EU_equity", overlay, 0), eq$class)
sharpe_without <- (without - ann_ret(rf)) / vol
eu      <- as.numeric(R[, "EU_equity"])
se_mean <- ann_vol(eu) / sqrt(nrow(R) / 12)     # sigma / sqrt(years)
stopifnot(overlay < se_mean,
          names(which.max(vol)) == "EU_equity",
          all(sharpe_without[c("US_equity", "UK_equity", "CH_equity")] >
                sharpe_without[["EU_equity"]]))

# The long-only tangency portfolio of chapter 9, with the overlay and without.
spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only") |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "StdDev")
tangency <- function(X) {
  extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(X, spec, optimize_method = "ROI", maxSR = TRUE))
}
Re_without <- Re
Re_without[, "EU_equity"] <- Re[, "EU_equity"] - overlay / 12
w_eu <- c(with    = tangency(Re)[["EU_equity"]],
          without = tangency(Re_without)[["EU_equity"]])
stopifnot(w_eu[["with"]] > 0.01, w_eu[["without"]] < 0.001)

round(c(return_without    = without[["EU_equity"]],
        sharpe_without    = sharpe_without[["EU_equity"]],
        cor_with_UK       = stats::cor(eu, as.numeric(R[, "UK_equity"])),
        cor_with_CH       = stats::cor(eu, as.numeric(R[, "CH_equity"])),
        tangency_with     = w_eu[["with"]],
        tangency_without  = w_eu[["without"]],
        se_of_annual_mean = se_mean,
        overlay_in_se     = overlay / se_mean), 2)
#>    return_without    sharpe_without       cor_with_UK       cor_with_CH 
#>              0.11              0.42              0.75              0.72 
#>     tangency_with  tangency_without se_of_annual_mean     overlay_in_se 
#>              0.09              0.00              0.04              0.79

Without the overlay, EU_equity earns 11.2% a year at the highest volatility of the five equity markets, a Sharpe ratio of 0.42 where the United States, Britain and Switzerland have 0.69, 0.50 and 0.46, and it correlates at 0.75 with Britain and 0.72 with Switzerland. The long-only tangency portfolio of Section 9.1 then holds none of it, against 9% with the overlay, and every chapter that trades return against risk would have carried a dead column. The overlay is 0.8 standard errors of the sample mean, so within one; by the argument of Merton (1980) that standard error depends only on the span of the sample in years, and 31 model years cannot distinguish a three-point assumption from the measured mean. No other series carries an overlay, and Appendix A records this one beside every other modeling choice.

Finally, the dataset is frozen at version 6.2.0. The idiosyncratic components are one draw, so a regenerated export is a different realized path rather than an update, and the drift is uneven across moments. Regenerating it is an event, not a refresh: every printed number in this book is pinned to 6.2.0, and the book’s repository carries a script that recomputes the load-bearing ones and names those a new export has moved. The full description of every series, its construction, its units and its known limitations is Appendix A.

2.3 The Real Data Sleeves

The modeled market is the default because every number reproduces offline for every reader. It cannot, by construction, support a claim about real markets, and some chapters have to make one: Section 21.2 measures factor premia on Kenneth French’s portfolios, and Section 25.3 notes that every asset in the modeled market survives to the end of the sample, a survivorship bias by construction that the real sleeves do not share. For those, tidypm carries a small data layer built the way Scheuch et al. (2023) build theirs, from free public sources, with two additions: a pinned cutoff date and a manifest.

The layer begins with a registry. pm_sources() is a list with one entry per sleeve, naming its source and carrying the function that fetches it. The registry is data rather than state; calling it downloads nothing.

8 of the 12 sleeves are built. Three equity sleeves and the cash sleeve come from Kenneth French’s data library, the market returns and risk-free rate behind Fama and French (1993); two government sleeves and an investment-grade credit sleeve are constructed from FRED yields by pricing a constant-maturity bond, which is the calculation Section 22.1 sets out; and high yield is a long-running mutual fund, because every free high-yield index history has been withdrawn behind a rolling window. Two caveats on that last sleeve belong here rather than in a footnote: a managed fund holding higher-quality high yield understates drawdowns, the wrong direction for the one sleeve whose reason to exist is its left tail, and its source is undocumented and could disappear, so the cache protects a reader who has already built the panel and not one arriving in ten years.

pm_init() walks the registry, downloads each implemented sleeve, joins them on the month-end date and writes the result, together with a manifest, to a single file in pm_cache_dir(), a directory in the user’s R cache outside any project and overridable through the environment variable TIDYPM_CACHE. It stops at a pinned cutoff, exported as a constant so that a chapter can name it rather than repeat a literal:

PM_CUTOFF
#> [1] "2025-12-31"

The cutoff is a calendar year end so that every annual table is a complete year and no chapter carries a stub year in which the sources, which publish at different speeds, have not all arrived. Run once, and read forever after:

pm_init()                       # downloads once, writes the panel and its manifest
panel <- pm_data()              # a disk read: no network, no recomputation
pm_data(sleeves = c("us_equity", "gov_long"), from = "1990-07-01")
pm_check()                      # your build against the manifest the book was built on

pm_data() never reaches the network. If no panel has been cached it stops and says to run pm_init(), because a function that silently downloads behaves differently on a plane. The panel it returns is deliberately ragged: a sleeve whose source starts later than the others is NA until it begins, and nothing pads or fills those rows, because a filled gap is an estimate dressed as an observation, and a covariance matrix estimated across one silently changes its effective sample, which is the failure as_xts_pm() warns about in Section 1.2.

The manifest is the honest version of reproducibility for data that is not ours to redistribute. French and FRED both revise history, so a panel rebuilt today need not equal the one the book’s numbers were computed on. pm_init() records an observation count and a hash per sleeve; pm_check() compares a reader’s manifest against the book’s and names the sleeves that moved, rather than leaving the difference to surface as an unexplained mismatch three chapters later. No downloaded file is committed to the book’s repository, and Appendix D records the version of every package the pipeline depends on.

2.4 Asset Class Vocabulary

The data uses one set of names and the industry another, and both appear in this book. Table 2.6 maps one onto the other. The rule is that code uses the data’s names, because a column is called what it is called, and prose uses whichever is clearer, which is usually the industry’s.

One word in the table deserves a sentence. Alternatives usually covers real estate, hedge funds, private equity and commodities together; this market carries only the last, so the word appears only where the industry’s usage is being quoted.

The class level of Table 2.3 is where Parts III to V of the book work, and it is the level at which the allocation literature since Brinson et al. (1986) locates most of the variation in a diversified portfolio’s return; the instrument level of Table 2.4 belongs to Part VI. Which of the two an allocation is set at is a question of granularity. Whether it is set from long-run assumptions or from conditioning information is a different question, that of horizon, and the words for its two answers, strategic and tactical, are defined in Section 3.1 rather than here.

Key Takeaways

  • Vectors are bold lower case, matrices bold capitals, estimates carry hats. \(\boldsymbol{R}\) is the \(T \times N\) matrix of simple total returns, \(\boldsymbol{w}\) the weights, \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) the moments of excess returns, and Equation 2.2 gives the portfolio’s mean and variance. Excess over the bill is the default basis everywhere; chapter 3 says why.
  • Arithmetic annualizing, mean \(\times 12\) and volatility \(\times\sqrt{12}\), is for anything that enters an optimizer. The compounded return of Equation 2.3 is for what a series earned. Per period, the gap is half the variance.
  • The modeled market contains no real data, so no series is the history of a real security, and it runs on model years 1 to
    1. model_year(), in_episode(), fmt_model() and scale_x_model() are how every chapter reads a date, and the four stress episodes are the rows of sim_episodes.
  • The market is sixteen risky class series over 372 months, five currencies times government, credit and equity plus a global commodity index, all in dollars and unhedged, with 181 instruments beneath them. The five money markets ship beside the universe and are neither cash nor the risk-free rate; the bill is.
  • Currency dominates foreign bonds, the bond block is nearly rank-deficient at instrument level, and equities are single names. Euro-area equity carries a deliberate three-point overlay, within one standard error of its sample mean. The dataset is frozen at version 6.2.0 because regenerating it redraws the idiosyncratic components.
  • Real data enters through pm_sources(), pm_init(), pm_data() and pm_check(): a registry, one download to a cache with a pinned cutoff, an offline read, and a manifest that names the sleeves that have moved since the book was built. It is used where a claim has to be about real markets.
