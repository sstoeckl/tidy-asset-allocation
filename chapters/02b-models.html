4  Models for This Market

The question. What explains these returns, and how much of that explanation survives being measured?

sec-02-data-why described the market one series at a time. This chapter asks what the series have in common, which is the question every later chapter needs answered: a beta is the input to a hedge, an alpha is the claim an active manager makes, and a factor loading is what a risk report reports.

The uncomfortable finding, stated up front so the rest of the chapter can substantiate it: most of the numbers this chapter produces are less certain than the way they are usually quoted.

4.1 What you need

A factor model needs a factor. The obvious one here is the market, and it has to be built rather than assumed.

returns <- sim_class_returns_monthly
R  <- as_xts_pm(returns, date, class, return)
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return
risky <- colnames(R)[!grepl("money_market", colnames(R))]

# Capitalisation-weighted global equity, from all 123 instruments. The weight
# in month t is the last capitalisation observed on or before the end of t-1:
# a weight you could actually have held.
eq  <- sim_assets$asset_id[sim_assets$asset_class == "equity"]
Ra  <- as_xts_pm(sim_asset_returns_monthly[sim_asset_returns_monthly$asset %in% eq, ],
                 date, asset, return)
cap <- sim_marketcap_weekly[sim_marketcap_weekly$asset %in% eq, ]
d   <- zoo::index(Ra)

W <- t(vapply(seq_along(d), function(i) {
  cutoff <- if (i == 1) d[1] - 40 else d[i - 1]
  cc   <- cap[cap$date <= cutoff, ]
  last <- cc[!duplicated(cc$asset, fromLast = TRUE), ]
  w <- last$market_cap[match(colnames(Ra), last$asset)]
  w[is.na(w)] <- 0
  if (sum(w) == 0) rep(1 / ncol(Ra), ncol(Ra)) else w / sum(w)
}, numeric(ncol(Ra))))

MKT   <- rowSums(W * zoo::coredata(Ra))
mkt_e <- MKT - rf
c(annualised = ann_ret(MKT), volatility = ann_vol(MKT))
#> annualised volatility 
#>    0.07393    0.17342

# Which region is this "global" market, really?
usm <- sim_assets$region[match(colnames(Ra), sim_assets$asset_id)] == "US"
us_share <- rowSums(W[, usm])
c(first = us_share[1], mean = mean(us_share), last = us_share[length(us_share)])
#>  first   mean   last 
#> 0.4889 0.5696 0.6998

Stop on that second line before using any beta in this chapter. The proxy is on average 57% United States by weight, and it ends the sample at 70%. It is a global index in construction and a US index in effect. Every beta below is largely a beta on the US market, and saying “global” without saying that is how a concentration becomes invisible.

4.1.1 One factor, twenty-one series

fit1 <- lapply(colnames(R), function(s) {
  y <- as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf
  m <- stats::lm(y ~ mkt_e)
  nw <- sandwich::NeweyWest(m, lag = 6, prewhite = FALSE)
  tibble::tibble(series = s,
                 alpha_ann = 12 * stats::coef(m)[1],
                 alpha_t   = stats::coef(m)[1] / sqrt(nw[1, 1]),
                 beta      = stats::coef(m)[2],
                 r2        = summary(m)$r.squared)
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

The model explains four series and fails on the rest. Above an R² of one half sit exactly the four developed equity blocks; JP_equity reaches only 0.263, and twelve of the twenty-one fall below 0.10. The extreme is JP_government at 0.000015 — a regression that explains nothing whatsoever.

The median R² across all twenty-one is 0.096. A single-factor model is a model of equities that happens to be applied to everything else.

Five alphas clear a t-statistic of two. Two of them are worth naming now because they return later: US_credit at +2.93% a year (t = 1.98) and US_government at +2.04% (t = 2.13). Bonds with significant alpha against an equity market is not a mispricing; it is the model telling you it has the wrong factors.

4.2 How to actually do it

Two habits separate a beta you can use from a number you have merely computed.

4.2.1 Split the beta, then test the split

A single beta assumes the relationship is the same on the way up and on the way down. PerformanceAnalytics will split it for you in one call.

Rm <- xts::xts(MKT, order.by = d)
rfx <- xts::xts(rf, order.by = d)

cond <- lapply(colnames(R), function(s) {
  tibble::tibble(
    series = s,
    beta   = PerformanceAnalytics::CAPM.beta(R[, s], Rm, rfx),
    bull   = PerformanceAnalytics::CAPM.beta.bull(R[, s], Rm, rfx),
    bear   = PerformanceAnalytics::CAPM.beta.bear(R[, s], Rm, rfx)
  )
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows() |>
  dplyr::mutate(gap = bear - bull)

cond |> dplyr::arrange(gap) |> head(4)
#> # A tibble: 4 × 5
#>   series              beta  bull    bear    gap
#>   <chr>              <dbl> <dbl>   <dbl>  <dbl>
#> 1 JP_government   -0.00240 0.195 -0.142  -0.336
#> 2 JP_money_market  0.0146  0.212 -0.122  -0.334
#> 3 JP_credit        0.0893  0.241  0.0474 -0.194
#> 4 EU_equity        1.22    1.28   1.12   -0.158

JP_government has an unconditional beta of -0.0024 — indistinguishable from zero, the sort of number that gets a series filed under “uncorrelated” and forgotten. It is not uncorrelated. It rises with the market when the market rises (+0.195) and rises against it when the market falls (-0.142). The single number is the average of two opposite behaviours, and it destroys the only interesting thing about the series.

Now the part that is usually skipped. An apparent asymmetry has to survive a test, because splitting a sample into two pieces and comparing them is a machine for producing differences.

asym <- lapply(colnames(R), function(s) {
  y  <- as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf
  dn <- as.numeric(mkt_e < 0)
  m  <- stats::lm(y ~ mkt_e + I(mkt_e * dn))
  nw <- sandwich::NeweyWest(m, lag = 6, prewhite = FALSE)
  tibble::tibble(series = s,
                 gap = stats::coef(m)[3],
                 t   = stats::coef(m)[3] / sqrt(nw[3, 3]),
                 p   = 2 * stats::pnorm(-abs(stats::coef(m)[3] / sqrt(nw[3, 3]))))
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

asym |> dplyr::filter(p < 0.05) |> dplyr::arrange(p)
#> # A tibble: 3 × 4
#>   series               gap     t       p
#>   <chr>              <dbl> <dbl>   <dbl>
#> 1 JP_money_market -0.328   -3.13 0.00174
#> 2 JP_government   -0.331   -2.94 0.00328
#> 3 US_money_market -0.00711 -1.99 0.0465

3 of 21. The Japanese bond asymmetry is real: t = -2.94. The credit asymmetry, which is the one an allocator would most like to believe in, is not: US_credit has a gap of +0.113 with t = 0.82 and p = 0.41.

There are 160 down months in this sample. Estimating a separate beta on them, for twenty-one series, will hand you differences whether or not any exist. Report the split as a diagnostic. Do not build a hedge on it without the test.

4.2.2 Check what your factor is actually sorting on

Size is the second factor everyone reaches for, and here it is a trap worth walking into deliberately.

capW <- W
reg  <- sim_assets$region[match(colnames(Ra), sim_assets$asset_id)]

# Global sort: bottom half by capitalisation minus top half.
smb_global <- vapply(seq_len(nrow(Ra)), function(i) {
  w <- capW[i, ]; ok <- w > 0; med <- median(w[ok])
  mean(zoo::coredata(Ra)[i, ok & w <= med]) - mean(zoo::coredata(Ra)[i, ok & w > med])
}, numeric(1))

# Region-neutral: rank within each region, then average the five spreads.
smb_neutral <- vapply(seq_len(nrow(Ra)), function(i) {
  w <- capW[i, ]
  mean(vapply(unique(reg), function(g) {
    j <- reg == g & w > 0; med <- median(w[j])
    mean(zoo::coredata(Ra)[i, j & w <= med]) - mean(zoo::coredata(Ra)[i, j & w > med])
  }, numeric(1)))
}, numeric(1))

Before using either, look at what the global sort put in each leg.

legs <- vapply(seq_len(nrow(Ra)), function(i) {
  w <- capW[i, ]; ok <- w > 0; med <- median(w[ok])
  c(big_us   = sum(w[ok & w > med & reg[ok] == "US"]) / sum(w[ok & w > med]),
    small_us = sum(w[ok & w <= med & reg[ok] == "US"]) / sum(w[ok & w <= med]))
}, numeric(2))
round(rowMeans(legs), 3)
#>   big_us small_us 
#>    0.628    0.152

The “big” leg is 63% United States. The “small” leg is 15%. A global capitalisation sort in a five-region universe does not sort on size, it sorts on region — and since region carries a currency, it sorts on currency too.

The consequence is measurable:

med_r2 <- function(f = NULL) {
  median(vapply(risky, function(s) {
    y <- as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf
    summary(if (is.null(f)) stats::lm(y ~ mkt_e) else stats::lm(y ~ mkt_e + f))$r.squared
  }, numeric(1)))
}
c(market_only  = med_r2(),
  plus_global  = med_r2(smb_global),
  plus_neutral = med_r2(smb_neutral))
#>  market_only  plus_global plus_neutral 
#>       0.1046       0.1333       0.1052

c(global_premium  = ann_ret(smb_global),
  neutral_premium = ann_ret(smb_neutral),
  correlation     = cor(smb_global, smb_neutral))
#>  global_premium neutral_premium     correlation 
#>         0.01663         0.01069         0.45911

Adding the global size factor lifts the median R² from 0.105 to 0.133. Constructing the same factor region-neutrally lifts it to 0.105 — that is, by 0.001. The explanatory power was not size. It was the regional composition of the legs.

Neither premium is distinguishable from zero over this sample. The lesson is not that size does not exist; it is that a factor is defined by its construction, and a sort you have not inspected is a bet you have not chosen.

4.3 What else is in there

Everything computed above — alpha, beta, conditional betas, R² — comes out of one call.

table.CAPM() returns alpha and beta with their p-values, the bull and bear betas, R², tracking error, the information ratio, Treynor’s ratio and the appraisal ratio, for as many series as you hand it. Most readers compute these one function at a time, and several of them never learn that the conditional betas exist at all.

Use it for the survey and the individual functions for the argument: the table gives no standard errors on the bull-minus-bear gap, which sec-02b-models-hands-on just showed is the number that decides whether the split means anything.

4.4 Why it works

4.4.1 A beta is a time series

win <- 60L
roll_beta <- function(s) {
  y <- as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf
  zoo::rollapply(cbind(y, mkt_e), win,
                 function(z) stats::cov(z[, 1], z[, 2]) / stats::var(z[, 2]),
                 by.column = FALSE, align = "right")
}

travel <- lapply(risky, function(s) {
  b <- roll_beta(s)
  tibble::tibble(series = s,
                 full = stats::cov(as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf, mkt_e) / stats::var(mkt_e),
                 min = min(b), max = max(b), travel = diff(range(b)),
                 sign_flips = mean(sign(b) != sign(stats::cov(as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf, mkt_e))))
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows() |> dplyr::arrange(dplyr::desc(travel))

Two patterns, and the second is the one worth carrying forward.

The betas that travel furthest are not the largest. JP_equity moves 1.30 across the sample, from +0.32 to +1.61, while US_equity — beta near one — travels only 0.46 and never changes sign at all.

And every series whose full-sample beta is near zero changes sign in something approaching half of all windows: JP_government in 49%, US_credit in 38%. The five equity series flip in none.

A small beta is not a stable beta. It is a beta whose sign you do not know.

That sentence is the reason sec-06-why-estimates-matter-standard exists, and the reason a hedge ratio needs an error bar before it needs a decimal place.

4.4.2 An excursus: measuring the wander itself

If moments move, the movement is itself a quantity. A turbulence index measures it as the Mahalanobis distance of a period’s returns from their recent mean, weighted by the covariance structure, so that an unusual pattern counts as turbulent even when no single series is extreme. The implementation used here is Stöckl (2020).

The package is not on CRAN; see sec-91-package-notes.

library(uncertaintymeasures)

R_risky <- R[, risky]
turb <- OSturbulence(R_risky, rolling = TRUE, roll.obs = 60)

OSturbulence() estimates the mean and covariance from data strictly before each observation, so the index never sees the month it is scoring. The in-sample variant exists too, and the gap between them is a clean picture of what look-ahead buys you.

tb  <- as.numeric(turb$turb)
top <- order(tb, decreasing = TRUE, na.last = NA)[1:5]
data.frame(month = format(zoo::index(R_risky)[top], "%Y-%m"),
           turbulence = round(tb[top], 2))
#> # A tibble: 5 × 2
#>   month   turbulence
#>   <chr>        <dbl>
#> 1 2008-10       2.23
#> 2 2020-03       0.96
#> 3 2008-11       0.93
#> 4 2008-12       0.78
#> 5 2009-01       0.73

The highest reading in thirty-one years is Oktober 2008 at 2.23, more than twice the next month. That is the same month sec-02-data-why picked out by counting exceedances, arrived at independently — but as one number rather than a count, and available on the day rather than in hindsight.

The component that makes the index more than a volatility measure is the ratio of the full distance to the one computed from variances alone:

ct <- as.numeric(turb$cturb)
c(median = median(ct, na.rm = TRUE),
  crisis = ct[which.max(tb)],
  max    = max(ct, na.rm = TRUE))
#> median crisis    max 
#>  1.728  1.800 10.385

cturb isolates how much of a turbulent month is correlations breaking rather than volatilities rising. sec-02-data-why showed that average pairwise correlation roughly doubles into a crisis, but only by choosing the windows by hand and knowing the answer first. This is the same statement made continuously and without hindsight — and it is what a regime-aware allocation in sec-17-regimes-standard would have to react to.

Exercises

  1. Rebuild the market proxy with equal rather than capitalisation weights. Which betas move most, and does the ranking in Table tbl-02b-rolling survive? Explain what that says about the phrase “the market”.
  2. The asymmetry test uses a single interaction term. Replace it with separate regressions on the up and down months and confirm that the point estimates agree while the standard errors do not. Which is the honest one, and why?
  3. Take the two series with the highest sign_flips in Table tbl-02b-rolling and plot their rolling betas against the turbulence index. Does the beta move because the market is turbulent, or independently of it?
  4. OSturbulence() has an s.k argument controlling the length of the short window whose mean is compared with the long-run mean. Vary it from 1 to 12 and describe what the index measures at each end.
