3  Return, Risk, and the Investor’s Problem

Draft|Code: script · exercises · solutions · all chapters|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

What does an investor want from a portfolio, and in which units should the answer be measured? Markowitz (1952) reduced the first question to two numbers per portfolio, an expected return and a variance, and the reduction has held up well enough that most of this book is built on it. The second question is older and is usually skipped. Neumann and Morgenstern (1944) gave the criterion that ranks risky prospects by their expected utility, and every mean-variance objective is an approximation to that criterion whose quality depends on how a return is defined, over which horizon, in which currency, and relative to which riskless alternative. Those definitions are fixed here once, so that no later chapter has to revisit them.

We first fix the return conventions: simple against log returns, arithmetic against geometric means, annualization, and how a Sharpe ratio aggregates over a horizon. We make excess returns the book’s basis and say what nominal, real and currency mean for it. We state the investor’s problem as expected utility, derive the mean-variance criterion and the certainty equivalent from it, and say what the risk-aversion parameter \(\gamma\) means in basis points. We define mean-variance dominance and the northwest rule, show where \(\gamma\) enters PortfolioAnalytics and what raising it does on the sixteen class series, and close by defining strategic and tactical allocation for the rest of the book. On that horizon axis this chapter is neither strategic nor tactical: it is where the terms are defined, and the last part of Section 3.1 says which later chapters are which.

The data are the sixteen risky class series of Section 2.2, loaded as every chapter loads them, in the symbols of Section 2.1: \(\boldsymbol{R}\) for the matrix of total returns, \(r_f\) for the Treasury bill, \(\boldsymbol{R}^e\) for the difference. The environment is the one Section 1.2 builds.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)   # sixteen risky classes
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return                                  # the bill
Re <- R - rf                                                       # excess returns
stopifnot(ncol(R) == 16L, !anyNA(R),
          all(sim_riskfree_monthly$date == zoo::index(R)))         # same month ends

3.1 Returns, Horizons, and the Basis of Comparison

3.1.1 Simple returns, log returns, and compounding

A simple return over one period is the change in value relative to the value at the start, income included, and a log return is its logarithm:

\[ R_t = \frac{P_t + D_t}{P_{t-1}} - 1, \qquad r_t = \log\left(1 + R_t\right). \tag{3.1}\]

The two aggregate in opposite directions. Simple returns add across assets: the return on a portfolio with weights \(\boldsymbol{w}\) is \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{R}_t\), which is why every weight vector in this book multiplies a matrix of simple returns. Log returns add across time. Over \(T\) periods,

\[ 1 + R_{0,T} = \prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(1 + R_t\right) = \exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t\right), \tag{3.2}\]

so a multi-period simple return is a product and the matching log return is a sum. The shipped data are simple monthly returns, and they stay simple wherever a portfolio is formed. Log returns appear only where a horizon is aggregated.

3.1.2 Averages, annualization, and the variance drag

There are two averages of a return series and they answer different questions. The arithmetic mean \(\hat\mu = T^{-1}\sum_t R_t\) is the expected one-period return and the quantity \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) that enters every objective in this book, because variance is defined around it. The geometric mean \(g = \left(\prod_t (1 + R_t)\right)^{1/T} - 1\) is the constant return that would have produced the same terminal wealth, and it is what an investor who held the asset actually earned per period. To second order,

\[ \log(1 + g) \approx \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}, \tag{3.3}\]

where \(\sigma^2\) is the variance of the simple return. The gap is the variance drag, half the variance lost to compounding. It is small for a government bond and not small for an equity market, and it lets a series have the higher arithmetic mean and the lower geometric one, a case Section 3.3 meets on this data.

Monthly moments are annualized by

\[ \mu_{\text{a}} = 12\,\mu_{\text{m}}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{a}} = \sqrt{12}\,\sigma_{\text{m}}, \qquad 1 + g_{\text{a}} = \left(1 + g_{\text{m}}\right)^{12}. \tag{3.4}\]

The first two follow from independence across months: the mean of a sum is the sum of the means, and so is the variance, so the standard deviation grows with the square root of the horizon. The third is compounding. Two mistakes follow from mixing them. Multiplying a geometric monthly mean by twelve understates the compounded annual return, and multiplying a monthly standard deviation by \(\sqrt{12}\) is wrong whenever monthly returns are autocorrelated, because the variance of a sum then carries covariance terms that independence set to zero.

3.1.3 The Sharpe ratio and its horizon

The ratio of expected excess return to standard deviation was introduced by Sharpe (1966) as a measure of fund performance and restated by Sharpe (1994) in the form the industry uses:

\[ \text{SR} = \frac{\mu - r_f}{\sigma} = \frac{\mu^e}{\sigma^e}. \tag{3.5}\]

Under independence the numerator scales with \(T\) and the denominator with \(\sqrt{T}\), so the ratio over \(q\) periods is \(\sqrt{q}\) times the one-period ratio, and \(\sqrt{12}\) turns a monthly Sharpe ratio into an annual one. Lo (2002) derived the general case. With autocorrelations \(\rho_k\) at lags \(k = 1, \ldots, q - 1\),

\[ \text{SR}_q = \text{SR}_1 \cdot \frac{q}{\sqrt{q + 2\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}(q - k)\,\rho_k}}, \tag{3.6}\]

which reduces to \(\sqrt{q}\,\text{SR}_1\) when every \(\rho_k\) is zero. Positive autocorrelation makes the naive \(\sqrt{q}\) overstate the annual ratio, negative autocorrelation makes it understate it, and Section 3.2 measures the error on this data.

3.1.4 Excess returns as the basis

Every return this book optimizes over is measured in excess of the Treasury bill,

\[ R^e_{it} = R_{it} - r_{f,t}, \tag{3.7}\]

for four reasons, in increasing order of consequence.

First, the objects the theory talks about are defined relative to a riskless rate. Tobin (1958) showed that when a riskless asset exists, every investor holds the same portfolio of risky assets and differs only in how much of it is held against the riskless asset; the tangency portfolio at the center of that result is the point where a ray from \(r_f\) touches the frontier, and it moves when \(r_f\) moves. Sharpe (1964) builds the market equilibrium on the same portfolio. Neither exists without a riskless rate to measure from.

Second, PortfolioAnalytics has no argument for one. optimize.portfolio() takes a return matrix and a specification, and its maximum-Sharpe objective maximizes the ratio of mean to standard deviation over zero. Handing it excess returns is the only way to make that ratio the Sharpe ratio of Equation 3.5.

Third, when the weights may sum to less than one, the objective treats the balance as earning exactly zero. With total returns that is false, because the balance earns the bill. With excess returns it is true, because the balance earns zero excess return. Section 3.3 shows by how much the difference raises the invested fraction at the same risk aversion.

Fourth, the bill moves. Under rebalancing the riskless rate is a different number in every period, so a total-return objective compares each period’s assets against a different alternative, while the excess formulation subtracts each period’s own bill and is the only one consistent through time.

rate <- sim_riskfree_monthly$rate_annual
c(first = rate[1], lowest = min(rate), highest = max(rate), last = rate[length(rate)])
#>   first  lowest highest    last 
#>  0.0384 -0.0001  0.0619  0.0430

The bill paid 3.8% a year in January of model year 1 and -0.01% at its lowest, in September of model year 24; an objective that compares risky assets against zero in both periods is wrong in at least one.

The riskless rate is sim_riskfree_monthly, the three-month Treasury bill, and not any of the five money-market series. Those are marked-to-market quarter-year bonds, they carry duration, and they lose money in far more months than the bill does; Section 4.2 measures the difference. A riskless asset has to be riskless over the holding period, and only the bill is.

3.1.5 Nominal and real

Every return above is nominal. A real return removes inflation \(\pi\) through the relation of Fisher (1930),

\[ 1 + i = (1 + r)(1 + \pi), \qquad r \approx i - \pi, \tag{3.8}\]

with \(i\) the nominal and \(r\) the real rate. The modeled market carries no inflation series, so this book works in nominal dollars throughout, and the bill is riskless in nominal terms only. An investor whose liabilities are real, a pension fund or an endowment spending a fixed share of purchasing power, would measure every return in excess of realized inflation, would find the bill risky over any horizon longer than its maturity, and would treat an inflation-indexed bond as the riskless asset. Campbell and Viceira (2002) develop that investor’s problem in full, and Campbell and Viceira (2001) show that for a long-horizon investor the indexed bond, not the bill, is the safe asset. Nothing in the machinery of later chapters changes for such an investor; only the series handed to it does.

3.1.6 Currency

Every series in the modeled market is a dollar return, unhedged. A foreign asset’s dollar return compounds its local return with the return \(f_t\) on its currency, quoted as dollars per unit of local currency so that a positive \(f_t\) means the local currency appreciated:

\[ 1 + R^{\$}_t = \left(1 + R^{\text{loc}}_t\right)\left(1 + f_t\right), \qquad R^{\text{hedged}}_t = R^{\$}_t - f_t \approx R^{\text{loc}}_t . \tag{3.9}\]

Hedging in this book is therefore a subtraction: the matching fx_return from sim_fx_monthly is removed from the unhedged series, to first order. A forward hedge also earns the interest differential between the two currencies, which the data do not carry, so the convention is the spot return alone, and Section 15.2 and Section 22.1 keep it. Perold and Schulman (1988) argued that hedging removes volatility at no expected cost, a free lunch; Black (1989) derived a single universal hedge ratio for every investor; Campbell et al. (2010) found that the answer differs between bonds, which should be fully hedged, and equities, whose currencies covary with the local market in ways that sometimes hedge them for free. Section 4.3 decomposes the variance of a foreign series into its local and currency parts, and Section 3.2 shows what the subtraction does to a foreign government bond.

3.1.7 The investor’s objective

Write \(W_0\) for initial wealth, \(\boldsymbol{w}\) for the weights on the risky assets, and \(1 - \boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{w}\) for the balance in the bill. The investor of Neumann and Morgenstern (1944) chooses the weights that maximize the expected utility of terminal wealth,

\[ \max_{\boldsymbol{w}} \; \mathbb{E}\!\left[U(W_1)\right], \qquad W_1 = W_0\left(1 + r_f + \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{R}^e\right), \tag{3.10}\]

for a utility function \(U\) that is increasing and concave. Concavity is risk aversion, and Pratt (1964) and Arrow (1965) measured it: the coefficient of absolute risk aversion is \(A(W) = -U''(W)/U'(W)\), the coefficient of relative risk aversion is \(\gamma(W) = W A(W)\), and an investor with relative risk aversion \(\gamma\) facing a return with variance \(\sigma^2\) demands a premium, as a fraction of wealth, of approximately

\[ \text{premium} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\,\gamma\,\sigma^2 . \tag{3.11}\]

A second-order expansion of Equation 3.10 around expected terminal wealth turns it into a function of two moments. Dropping the terms that do not depend on \(\boldsymbol{w}\), the investor maximizes

\[ U(\boldsymbol{w}) = \mu_{\boldsymbol{w}} - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}, \tag{3.12}\]

with \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) the mean vector and covariance matrix of the excess returns. This is the mean-variance criterion of Markowitz (1952), and Markowitz (1959) gave the two conditions under which the expansion is exact: \(U\) is itself quadratic, or returns are normal so that every higher moment is a function of the first two. Neither holds for the series of this book, and the question is what that costs. Levy and Markowitz (1979) compared the expected utility of the mean-variance optimum with that of the true optimum across utility functions and historical returns and found the two almost perfectly correlated; Markowitz (2014) revisited the evidence with thirty more years of data and reached the same conclusion. The approximation fails where returns are far from normal, and Section 11.1 takes up that case by adding skewness and kurtosis to Equation 3.12 rather than abandoning it.

Two readings of Equation 3.12 are worth having. First, \(U(\boldsymbol{w})\) is a certainty equivalent: the riskless excess return the investor would accept in place of the portfolio, the expected return less the premium of Equation 3.11 applied to the portfolio’s own variance. Second, \(\gamma\) is a price. Along an indifference curve \(U(\boldsymbol{w})\) is constant, so an increase in expected return of \(\Delta\mu\) buys an increase in variance of

\[ \sigma_1^2 - \sigma_0^2 = \frac{2\,\Delta\mu}{\gamma}, \tag{3.13}\]

and the price has units. With \(\Delta\mu\) one percentage point and \(\sigma_0 = 0\), an investor with \(\gamma = 2\) accepts a volatility of \(\sqrt{0.02 / 2} = 10.0%\) for one point of expected return; one with \(\gamma = 10\) accepts \(\sqrt{0.02 / 10} = 4.5%\). Read the other way, ten percent of volatility costs an investor with \(\gamma = 4\) a certainty equivalent of \(\tfrac{4}{2} \times 0.10^2 = 200\) basis points a year.

Table 3.1 is the table to keep in mind whenever a chapter reports a \(\gamma\). The customary range in the literature runs from about two to about ten, and the data give one more anchor: an investor who holds the US equity market and nothing else, and is at an optimum, has \(\gamma = \mu^e / \sigma^2\) by the first-order condition of Equation 3.12 with one asset.

mu_us <- mean(Re[, "US_equity"]) * 12
sd_us <- stats::sd(Re[, "US_equity"]) * sqrt(12)
c(excess_return = mu_us, volatility = sd_us, implied_gamma = mu_us / sd_us^2)
#> excess_return    volatility implied_gamma 
#>        0.1130        0.1562        4.6332

On this sample that investor has \(\gamma = 0.113 / 0.156^2 = 4.6\).

The alternative to quadratic utility that the rest of the literature uses is constant relative risk aversion, \(U(W) = W^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)\), under which the premium of Equation 3.11 is the same fraction of wealth at every level of wealth. When returns are lognormal, maximizing expected power utility is equivalent to maximizing \(\mu - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\sigma^2\) in log returns with the same \(\gamma\), so the two objectives agree exactly on that distribution and approximately on any distribution close to it. The disagreements are in the tails, and Section 11.1 measures them on the credit series where they matter.

3.1.8 The horizon of the problem

Equation 3.10 is a one-period problem, and a reader may object that the investor lives for many. Samuelson (1969) in discrete time and Merton (1969) in continuous time solved the multi-period problem for a power-utility investor and found that when returns are independent across periods and the investment opportunities do not change, the optimal weights are the same in every period and at every horizon: the long-lived investor behaves myopically, and the one-period rule is exact. When opportunities do change, because the riskless rate or the expected returns move, the long-horizon investor holds additional positions that hedge those changes, which is the case Campbell and Viceira (2002) treat and the reason their safe asset is the indexed bond. This book solves the one-period problem throughout and re-solves it at each rebalancing date, which is the myopic policy; the chapters on the horizon axis, defined below, are about how the inputs to that policy are formed rather than about intertemporal hedging.

3.1.9 Dominance, efficiency, and the northwest rule

Two moments per portfolio make comparison a matter of geometry. In the plane with volatility on the horizontal axis and expected return on the vertical, portfolio \(A\) dominates portfolio \(B\) if it offers at least as much return for at most as much risk, with strictly more of one or strictly less of the other:

\[ A \succ B \iff \mu_A \ge \mu_B \;\text{ and }\; \sigma_A \le \sigma_B, \;\text{ with at least one inequality strict.} \tag{3.14}\]

The relation has a direction. A dominating portfolio lies above, to the left, or both: a better allocation always lies to the northwest. A portfolio or asset that no feasible alternative dominates is efficient, and the set of efficient portfolios is the efficient frontier that Section 9.1 derives. Equation 3.14 is weaker than Equation 3.12, because it ranks only pairs one of which is northwest of the other and says nothing about a pair where one has more return and more risk; the utility function settles those by choosing a point on the frontier. It is also stronger in one respect: a dominated portfolio is rejected by every investor with any \(\gamma > 0\), so the vocabulary applies before a risk aversion is chosen, and every chapter from here on uses dominated and efficient in this sense.

3.1.10 Strategic and tactical allocation

Two distinct cuts run through the book and are easily confused. The first is granularity: allocate across asset classes, then select within a class, which is the order of the parts. The second is horizon, and it needs two terms that are defined here once.

Strategic allocation is the long-run policy: the portfolio an investor would hold given unconditional beliefs about the assets, with \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) estimated over the whole available history and no attempt to time anything. Tactical allocation is a deviation from that policy using conditioning information available at the time: a signal, a regime, a view. The two are orthogonal to granularity, because a tactical tilt can be taken across classes and a permanent tilt inside a sleeve is strategic. Brinson et al. (1986) and Brinson et al. (1991) measured how much of the variation in a set of pension funds’ returns the policy alone explained and found most of it; Ibbotson and Kaplan (2000) clarified what that number does and does not mean, in particular that it says nothing about the level of return the policy earns. Sharpe (1987) gave the framework in which the two are stages of one decision, the policy set from capital-market assumptions and the tactical position from the same assumptions updated with current information, and Ang (2014) organizes an entire treatment of asset management around the strategic question of which bad times an investor can bear. The strategic policy is itself an estimate from a finite history, and DeMiguel et al. (2009) measured what that costs out of sample.

The chapters of this book sort as follows. Chapters 9 to 14 and chapter 15 are strategic: they solve Equation 3.12 and its relatives from full-sample moments, and Section 9.1 opens by saying so. Chapters 16 to 19 are tactical: they condition the inputs on information available at the time, beginning with Section 16.1. Chapters 23 to 25 are about implementation and evaluation of either, and Section 25.3 is where the strategic policies of Part IV are finally run as they would have been run. Each chapter says which it is doing in its opening paragraph.

One rule follows from the objective and applies on either axis. The measure a portfolio is judged by must be the measure it was chosen by. A portfolio that minimizes expected shortfall and is then reported on its Sharpe ratio has been misreported: it was never asked to maximize that ratio, and its shortfall, the quantity it was built for, has gone unmentioned. Artzner et al. (1999) and Acerbi and Tasche (2002) give the properties a risk measure should have; Section 10.1 chooses among them, and Section 25.3 reports every strategy on every measure so that the reader can see which portfolio won on which criterion.

3.2 Implementation

3.2.1 Return conventions in PerformanceAnalytics

PerformanceAnalytics implements every convention above, and the point of showing them is to see which convention each function defaults to. The class tables ship a total-return index, so a price series can be rebuilt and Equation 3.1 checked against the shipped return.

us <- dplyr::filter(sim_class_returns_monthly, class == "US_equity")
P  <- xts::xts(us$index, order.by = us$date)

simple <- Return.calculate(P, method = "discrete")
logret <- Return.calculate(P, method = "log")
stopifnot(all.equal(as.numeric(simple[-1]), as.numeric(R[-1, "US_equity"])))
head(merge(simple, logret, shipped = R[, "US_equity"]), 3)
#>              simple   logret US_equity
#> 2101-01-31       NA       NA  0.025013
#> 2101-02-28 0.022790 0.022534  0.022790
#> 2101-03-31 0.005306 0.005292  0.005306

The first observation is NA, because a return needs two prices. The log return is smaller than the simple one when the simple return is positive and larger in absolute value when it is negative, which is Equation 3.1.

Return.annualized() with scale = 1 returns the per-period geometric mean, because it evaluates \(\left(\prod (1 + R_t)\right)^{\text{scale}/T} - 1\), so the two averages and the drag between them come from one series.

x    <- R[, "US_equity"]
mu_m <- mean(x)                                          # arithmetic, monthly
g_m  <- as.numeric(Return.annualized(x, scale = 1))      # geometric, monthly
s2_m <- stats::var(as.numeric(x))
c(arithmetic = mu_m, geometric = g_m, gap = mu_m - g_m, half_variance = s2_m / 2)
#>    arithmetic     geometric           gap half_variance 
#>      0.011284      0.010262      0.001022      0.001019
NoteWorked: the drag of Equation 3.3 on US_equity

With the sample numbers substituted, the drag is

\[ \mu - g = 0.011284 - 0.010262 = 0.001022, \qquad \frac{\sigma^2}{2} = \frac{0.002038}{2} = 0.001019, \]

and the two agree to 3.0e-06, which is the third-order term Equation 3.3 drops. The drag is 10.2 basis points a month, or 1.23 percentage points a year on a series with 15.6% annualized volatility.

Annualizing the same series under the three conventions of Equation 3.4 gives three different numbers, and the package’s default is the compounded one.

c(arithmetic_x12    = as.numeric(Return.annualized(x, scale = 12, geometric = FALSE)),
  compounded        = as.numeric(Return.annualized(x, scale = 12)),          # the default
  cumulative        = as.numeric(Return.cumulative(x)),
  volatility_sqrt12 = as.numeric(StdDev.annualized(x, scale = 12)))
#>    arithmetic_x12        compounded        cumulative volatility_sqrt12 
#>            0.1354            0.1303           43.6152            0.1564

A dollar in US_equity at the start of the sample became 44.6 dollars by its end, which is \(\left(1 + 0.1303\right)^{31}\) to rounding and not \(1 + 31 \times 0.1354\). The arithmetic figure is the right input to Equation 3.12 and the wrong answer to the question of what the asset paid.

3.2.2 The Sharpe ratio, in two conventions

Return.excess() implements Equation 3.7 and reproduces the subtraction of the data block exactly, so the two routes to Re cannot drift apart.

bill  <- xts::xts(rf, order.by = zoo::index(R)); colnames(bill) <- "bill"
Re_pa <- Return.excess(R, Rf = bill)
stopifnot(all.equal(zoo::coredata(Re_pa), zoo::coredata(Re), check.attributes = FALSE))

table.AnnualizedReturns() reports the compounded return, the annualized volatility and a Sharpe ratio per series; SharpeRatio.annualized() reports the last of these on its own. On the same series they disagree.

three <- c("US_equity", "US_credit", "US_government")
table.AnnualizedReturns(R[, three], scale = 12, Rf = bill)
#> # A tibble: 3 × 3
#>   US_equity US_credit US_government
#>       <dbl>     <dbl>         <dbl>
#> 1     0.130    0.063         0.0454
#> 2     0.156    0.0725        0.0478
#> 3     0.676    0.545         0.469
SharpeRatio.annualized(R[, three], Rf = bill, scale = 12)
#>                                           US_equity US_credit US_government
#> Annualized Sharpe Ratio (Rf=2.2%, p=95%):    0.7236    0.5726        0.4882
NoteTwo functions, two defaults for the same ratio

table.AnnualizedReturns() defaults to geometric = TRUE and divides the compounded excess return by the annualized volatility. SharpeRatio.annualized() defaults to geometric = FALSE and divides twelve times the arithmetic monthly excess return by the same volatility, which is \(\sqrt{12}\) times the monthly ratio of Equation 3.5. The gap is the drag of Equation 3.3 divided by the volatility, so it grows with the volatility of the series, and it can reorder a ranking. Either convention is defensible; mixing them across a table is not. When this book reports the Sharpe ratio of a series it uses the compounded convention, as Section 4.2 does; when it optimizes, the objective contains the arithmetic mean, because that is what Equation 3.12 is a function of.

The reordering is visible on the sixteen series, and both rankings come from one function with the convention as an argument.

sr_compounded <- SharpeRatio.annualized(R, Rf = bill, scale = 12, geometric = TRUE)[1, colnames(R)]
sr_arithmetic <- SharpeRatio.annualized(R, Rf = bill, scale = 12, geometric = FALSE)[1, colnames(R)]
rank_of <- function(s, a) as.integer(rank(-s)[[a]])

sr_rank <- tibble::tibble(
  asset           = colnames(R),
  volatility      = unname(StdDev.annualized(R, scale = 12)[1, asset]),
  rank_arithmetic = as.integer(rank(-sr_arithmetic[asset])),
  rank_compounded = as.integer(rank(-sr_compounded[asset]))
)
moved <- dplyr::filter(sr_rank, rank_arithmetic != rank_compounded)

# Pairs that trade places under compounding: one series falls, one rises.

3.2.3 Aggregation over horizons

Equation 3.6 says when \(\sqrt{12}\) is the right factor. The direct test is to compound the monthly series to quarters and years and compute the ratio at each horizon.

sr <- function(v) mean(v) / stats::sd(v)
by_horizon <- function(series) {
  z <- Re[, series]
  q <- xts::apply.quarterly(z, function(v) prod(1 + v) - 1)
  y <- xts::apply.yearly(z,    function(v) prod(1 + v) - 1)
  c(monthly_x_sqrt12 = sr(z) * sqrt(12), quarterly_x_2 = sr(q) * 2, annual = sr(y))
}
horizons  <- rbind(US_equity = by_horizon("US_equity"),
                   US_government = by_horizon("US_government"))
shortfall <- 1 - horizons[, "annual"] / horizons[, "monthly_x_sqrt12"]
rho1      <- apply(Re, 2, function(v) stats::acf(as.numeric(v), lag.max = 1, plot = FALSE)$acf[2])
stopifnot(shortfall[["US_government"]] > shortfall[["US_equity"]], shortfall[["US_equity"]] > 0,
          rho1[["US_government"]] > 0.1, abs(rho1[["US_equity"]]) < 0.05)
round(cbind(horizons, shortfall, rho1 = rho1[rownames(horizons)]), 3)
#>               monthly_x_sqrt12 quarterly_x_2 annual shortfall   rho1
#> US_equity                0.724         0.663  0.652     0.098 -0.004
#> US_government            0.488         0.417  0.379     0.224  0.147

For US_government the annual ratio is 22% below the \(\sqrt{12}\) figure, because its monthly returns have a first-order autocorrelation of 0.147: the variance of a twelve-month sum exceeds twelve monthly variances, and the annual ratio falls short of \(\sqrt{12}\) times the monthly one. For US_equity the annual ratio is 10% below it, and a first-order autocorrelation of -0.004 does not explain that. The correction of Equation 3.6, applied to every series, says how far the naive factor is off on account of autocorrelation at every lag.

lo_factor <- function(v, q = 12) {
  rho <- stats::acf(as.numeric(v), lag.max = q - 1, plot = FALSE)$acf[-1]
  q / sqrt(q + 2 * sum((q - seq_len(q - 1)) * rho))
}
lo_ratio  <- apply(Re, 2, lo_factor) / sqrt(12)   # 1 means sqrt(12) was right
lo_sorted <- sort(lo_ratio)

The naive factor overstates the annual Sharpe ratio of 10 of the sixteen series, US_government most, by 15%, and understates that of the other 6, CH_government most, by 6%; the median correction across the sixteen is 7%. The 7 largest overstatements are all on government and credit series, whose monthly returns are positively autocorrelated. The correction puts the annual ratio of US_government 13% below the \(\sqrt{12}\) figure, where the direct calculation had it 22% below, and that of US_equity 3% below against 10%. The rest is not autocorrelation: the direct figure rests on 31 annual observations and is itself imprecise. \(\sqrt{12}\) is the convention this book uses, with that caveat attached.

3.2.4 The bill is not a seventeenth asset

A reader who has just been told that the riskless rate is essential will be tempted to add it to the return matrix as another column. With the actual bill as a seventeenth column, a minimum-variance optimizer buys almost nothing else; with a constant riskless rate, the covariance matrix loses rank and cannot be inverted, which is why Tobin (1958) treats the riskless asset separately in the algebra rather than as one more row of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\).

gmv_of <- function(X) {
  spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(X)) |>
    add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
    add.constraint(type = "long_only") |>
    add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")
  w <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(X, spec, optimize_method = "ROI"))
  w[colnames(X)]
}
R_bill  <- merge(R, bill)

round(w_bill[w_bill > 0.001], 4)
#> CH_credit JP_equity      bill 
#>    0.0034    0.0021    0.9941
c(rank_with_bill = qr(stats::cov(R_bill))$rank, rank_with_constant = qr(stats::cov(R_const))$rank)

The optimizer puts 99.4% into the bill, and the constant column leaves a 17-by-17 covariance matrix of rank 16. The riskless asset enters through the excess-return basis and the budget constraint, not through the covariance matrix.

3.2.6 Where risk aversion enters the package

PortfolioAnalytics states Equation 3.12 as two objectives on one specification: a return objective named mean and a risk objective named var carrying a risk_aversion argument. quadratic_utility_objective() builds the same pair in one call, to be assigned to the specification’s objectives slot. Before either is used, one thing about the argument has to be known.

Warningrisk_aversion is \(\gamma/2\), not \(\gamma\)

The package builds \(\boldsymbol{Q} = 2\lambda\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) from risk_aversion =\(\lambda\) and hands it to ROI, whose quadratic program is stated as \(\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}'\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{x}\). The two factors of two do not cancel: the coefficient on \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\) in the solved problem is \(\lambda\), where Equation 3.12 puts \(\gamma/2\). To solve the problem a textbook writes with \(\gamma\), pass risk_aversion =\(\gamma/2\). The behavior is undocumented upstream, the derivation is in Section B.5, and the check below verifies it numerically on this data. Every \(\gamma\) in this book is the textbook one, and every call halves it.

base_spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only")

qu_spec <- function(spec, gamma) {
  spec |>
    add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
    add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "var", risk_aversion = gamma / 2)
}

pa_weights <- function(spec, gamma) {
  w <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(Re, qu_spec(spec, gamma), optimize_method = "ROI"))
  w[colnames(Re)]
}

# The same objective through the package's own constructor.
spec_qu <- base_spec
spec_qu$objectives <- quadratic_utility_objective(risk_aversion = 10 / 2)
w_ctor <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(Re, spec_qu, optimize_method = "ROI"))[colnames(Re)]
stopifnot(all.equal(pa_weights(base_spec, gamma = 10), w_ctor))
round(w_ctor[w_ctor > 0.005], 4)
#>     EU_equity     US_credit     US_equity US_government 
#>        0.1499        0.0611        0.2838        0.5038

The check is a second solver that takes \(\gamma\) literally. quadprog minimizes \(\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}'\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{d}'\boldsymbol{x}\), so \(\boldsymbol{D} = \gamma\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) and \(\boldsymbol{d} = \boldsymbol{\mu}\) is exactly the negative of Equation 3.12. The weights may sum to anything between zero and one, because a budget that binds would hide the scale on which the two conventions differ.

mu <- colMeans(Re)
S  <- stats::cov(Re)

relaxed_spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "weight_sum", min_sum = 0, max_sum = 1) |>   # 0 <= sum(w) <= 1
  add.constraint(type = "long_only")

qp_weights <- function(gamma) {
  n <- ncol(Re)
  w <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = gamma * S, dvec = mu,
                          Amat = cbind(-rep(1, n), diag(n)),        # -sum(w) >= -1, w >= 0
                          bvec = c(-1, rep(0, n)))$solution
  setNames(w, colnames(Re))
}

gamma0 <- 10
w_pa   <- pa_weights(relaxed_spec, gamma0)                  # passes risk_aversion = 5
c(gap_if_argument_is_half_gamma = max(abs(w_pa - qp_weights(gamma0))),
  gap_if_argument_is_gamma      = max(abs(w_pa - qp_weights(gamma0 / 2))))
#> gap_if_argument_is_half_gamma      gap_if_argument_is_gamma 
#>          0.000000000000000451          0.400563221028796157

The package’s answer at risk_aversion = 5 matches the textbook problem at \(\gamma = 10\) to machine precision and misses the one at \(\gamma = 5\) by 0.40 in the largest weight. The claim of the callout is measured rather than asserted.

3.3 What the Objective Does on Sixteen Assets

3.3.1 The northwest rule applied

The application opens with the picture. Each series is one point in the plane of Equation 3.14, with the arithmetic excess mean on the vertical axis because that is what \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) is, and every coordinate is assembled into one data frame before anything is drawn, so that a volatility can never be paired with another asset’s return.

mom <- tibble::tibble(

  sigma = as.numeric(apply(Re, 2, stats::sd)) * sqrt(12)

n_dominating <- function(i) sum(mom$mu >= mom$mu[i] & mom$sigma <= mom$sigma[i]) - 1L

12 of the sixteen series are dominated by at least one other, and EU_equity, US_equity, US_credit, US_government are efficient. GLOBAL_commodity is one of 3 series dominated by 4 others, each offering more excess return at less volatility over this sample. This is a statement about one sample and carries no test, but it needs no optimizer, and it is the first thing to look at before one runs. An investor with any \(\gamma\) prefers each efficient series to every series it dominates; which efficient series, or which combination of them, is what Equation 3.12 decides and what Section 9.2 computes.

The basis matters even here. Repeating the count on compounded total returns, which is how Section 9.2 draws the same picture, leaves the efficient set as it is and changes which series dominates which.

3.3.2 Raising \(\gamma\) under full investment

With the weights forced to sum to one, raising \(\gamma\) walks the solution along the efficient frontier toward its left end and stops there. It does not reach cash, because there is no cash to reach: the whole portfolio must be in the sixteen risky series, and the least risky thing they can form is the minimum-variance portfolio of Section 9.1.

p_vol <- function(w) sqrt(as.numeric(t(w) %*% S %*% w)) * sqrt(12)
p_ret <- function(w) sum(mu * w) * 12

w_gmv <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(
  Re, base_spec |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var"),
  optimize_method = "ROI"))[colnames(Re)]

gammas <- c(1, 2, 4, 10, 20, 50, 100, 1000, 10000)
sweep_full <- purrr::map_dfr(gammas, function(g) {
  w <- pa_weights(base_spec, g)
  tibble::tibble(gamma = g, rule = "fully invested", invested = sum(w),
                 vol = p_vol(w), ret = p_ret(w), largest = max(w),
                 to_gmv = sum(abs(w - w_gmv)) / 2)
})

Volatility falls from 20.0% at \(\gamma = 1\) to 4.13% at \(\gamma = 10{,}000\), against 4.13% for the minimum-variance portfolio, and the distance between the two portfolios is 0.0004 at the top of the range. A very risk-averse investor who must be fully invested holds the minimum-variance portfolio, the one portfolio that needs no estimate of \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\), and Jagannathan and Ma (2003) explain why that is an advantage rather than a limitation. At the other end the solution approaches the single highest-return series, the corner every long-only frontier ends in.

3.3.3 Relaxing full investment

Let the weights sum to anything between zero and one, so that the balance sits in the bill at zero excess return. Without the budget constraint the first-order condition of Equation 3.12 is \(\boldsymbol{\mu} = \gamma\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\), so that

\[ \boldsymbol{w}^\ast(\gamma) = \frac{1}{\gamma}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{w}^\ast(\gamma) = \frac{\boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\gamma}: \tag{3.15}\]

the composition of the risky holdings does not depend on \(\gamma\), and the invested fraction is proportional to \(1/\gamma\). That is Tobin (1958)’s separation with the algebra written out, and it survives the long-only constraint, because scaling a feasible long-only vector keeps it feasible. The cap at one adds only a range of small \(\gamma\) over which the investor would borrow and is not allowed to.

sweep_relaxed <- purrr::map_dfr(c(gammas, 1e5), function(g) {
  w <- pa_weights(relaxed_spec, g)
  tibble::tibble(gamma = g, rule = "budget relaxed", invested = sum(w),
                 vol = p_vol(w), ret = p_ret(w), largest = max(w), to_gmv = NA_real_)
})

3.3.4 The basis, once more, in the invested fraction

The third argument for excess returns in Section 3.1 can now be measured. Hand the same relaxed problem total returns instead of excess returns and the optimizer believes the alternative to the risky series earns zero rather than the bill.

At every \(\gamma\) the total-return problem invests 64% more of the portfolio in risky assets than the excess-return problem, because it credits each risky series with the bill’s 2.2% a year as if that were a reward for bearing risk. Key Takeaways

  • Simple returns add across assets and log returns add across time. The arithmetic mean is the input to every objective, the geometric mean is what was earned, and the gap is half the variance Equation 3.3. Annualizing a Sharpe ratio by \(\sqrt{12}\) assumes independence across months, and Equation 3.6 says by how much autocorrelation breaks it (Lo 2002).
  • Returns enter every optimizer in excess of the Treasury bill: the tangency portfolio is defined relative to a riskless rate (Tobin 1958), PortfolioAnalytics has no argument for one, an uninvested fraction then earns exactly what the objective assumes, and the bill moves. The money-market series are not the bill.
  • \(\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}} - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}}\) is a second-order approximation to expected utility (Neumann and Morgenstern 1944; Markowitz 1959) whose value is a certainty equivalent and whose \(\gamma\) is a price: at \(\gamma = 4\), ten percent of volatility costs 200 basis points a year Table 3.1.
  • A portfolio dominates another if it has at least as much expected return and at most as much volatility, with one inequality strict; a better allocation lies to the northwest, and an asset nothing dominates is efficient. On this sample 12 of the sixteen series are dominated before any optimizer runs.
  • risk_aversion in PortfolioAnalytics is \(\gamma/2\). Under full investment a rising \(\gamma\) converges to the minimum-variance portfolio, not to cash; with the budget relaxed the risky composition is fixed and the invested fraction is proportional to \(1/\gamma\), which is two-fund separation.
  • Strategic allocation is the long-run policy from unconditional moments; tactical allocation deviates from it on conditioning information. Chapters 9 to 15 are strategic, 16 to 19 tactical, 23 to 25 about implementing and evaluating either, and the measure a portfolio is judged by must be the one it was chosen by.
