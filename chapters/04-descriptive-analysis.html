4  Descriptive Analysis of Asset Returns

Every later chapter takes a return series and does something to it: estimates a moment, imposes a constraint, holds a portfolio through a crisis. None of that is interpretable without knowing what the series are, and none of it is trustworthy without knowing which of their properties can be estimated at all. Since Mandelbrot (1963) and Fama (1965) it has been understood that financial returns are not Gaussian, and Cont (2001) collects the regularities that survive across markets and periods. This chapter asks which of those regularities hold in the market this book uses, and which of the numbers we are about to rely on are worth relying on.

We first set out what the moments of a return series are and how precisely each can be estimated, then compute them, then examine the two properties of this market that shape every portfolio result that follows: its tails arrive as events rather than as a distribution, and its currency exposure is a property of the asset rather than of the country.

The data are the modeled market of Section 2.2: five regions, the United States, Europe, the United Kingdom, Switzerland and Japan, each with equities, government bonds and credit, plus a global commodity index, which makes sixteen risky class series over 372 months, model years 1 to 31, all in US dollars and unhedged, with a money-market series per region beside them and the US Treasury bill as the risk-free rate. No series in it is the history of a real security, so it ships with the package, and every number below reproduces on the reader’s machine without an account anywhere.

4.1 What Can Be Estimated

Let \(r_t\) denote the return on an asset in period \(t\), observed for \(t = 1, \dots, T\). The four sample moments in universal use are

\[ \hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t, \qquad \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(r_t - \hat{\mu})^2, \tag{4.1}\]

\[ \hat{S} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{r_t - \hat{\mu}}{\hat{\sigma}}\right)^{3}, \qquad \hat{K} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{r_t - \hat{\mu}}{\hat{\sigma}}\right)^{4} - 3 . \tag{4.2}\]

They are conventionally reported side by side, in one table, as though they were four facts of the same kind. They are not, and the difference is the single most consequential thing in this chapter.

4.1.1 The mean is the moment that does not improve

Suppose returns are independent with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) per unit of time, observed over a calendar span \(\tau\) at intervals of length \(\Delta\), so that \(T = \tau / \Delta\). The standard errors of the first two moments behave quite differently:

\[ \operatorname{se}(\hat{\mu}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\tau}}, \qquad \operatorname{se}(\hat{\sigma}^2) \approx \sigma^2\sqrt{\frac{2}{T}} = \sigma^2\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta}{\tau}}. \tag{4.3}\]

The precision of the mean depends only on the calendar span \(\tau\). Sampling the same thirty years weekly rather than monthly multiplies \(T\) by roughly four and does nothing whatever to the standard error of \(\hat{\mu}\), because each observation carries proportionally less of the drift. The precision of the variance depends on the number of observations, so the same change cuts its standard error in half. This is the point of Merton (1980), and it is why expected returns remain the hard problem while volatility modeling has made steady progress. Section 6.1 and Section 7.1 are the two chapters this asymmetry splits the book into.

Higher moments are worse still. \(\hat{S}\) and \(\hat{K}\) depend on third and fourth powers of standardized returns, so a single extreme observation can move them by more than the rest of the sample combined.

4.1.2 Testing normality rather than assuming it

Jarque and Bera (1980) combine the two shape moments into a single statistic,

\[ \mathrm{JB} = \frac{T}{6}\left(\hat{S}^2 + \frac{\hat{K}^2}{4}\right) \;\xrightarrow{\;d\;}\; \chi^2_2 \tag{4.4}\]

under the null of normality. It is a specification test, not a model, and a rejection says only that some combination of skewness and excess kurtosis is larger than sampling error allows.

Whether normality matters depends on what is being computed. For a portfolio variance it hardly does. For a tail quantile it decides the answer. Write \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha\) for the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the loss distribution and

\[ \mathrm{ES}_\alpha = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\, r \mid r \leq \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha \right] \tag{4.5}\]

for the expected shortfall, the average loss conditional on being in the tail. Expected shortfall is preferred to value at risk because it is coherent in the sense of Artzner et al. (1999), in particular subadditive, so that combining two positions can never make the measured risk larger than the sum of its parts (Acerbi and Tasche 2002). Value at risk fails that test, which matters precisely when portfolios are being built.

Equation 4.5 can be estimated directly from the sample tail, or from a normal quantile corrected for the observed skewness and kurtosis using the Cornish-Fisher expansion,

\[ z_{\mathrm{CF}} = z + \frac{(z^2-1)\hat{S}}{6} + \frac{(z^3-3z)\hat{K}}{24} - \frac{(2z^3-5z)\hat{S}^2}{36}, \tag{4.6}\]

with \(z\) the standard normal quantile. The resulting modified expected shortfall was brought into portfolio work by Favre and Galeano (2002) and refined by Boudt et al. (2008), and it is what PerformanceAnalytics computes by default. The gap between the two estimates measures how much the normal approximation is costing.

Three features of Table 4.1 matter for what follows.

The return ranking is not the Sharpe ranking. US_credit earns 6.3% a year against US_equity’s 13.0%, less than half, and on Sharpe ratio it ranks 3 of 16, at 0.56 against 0.69 for US_equity and above 3 of the 5 equity markets, because its volatility is under half that of US_equity. Whether that makes it the better holding depends on whether variance is the right measure of its risk, which Section 4.3 takes up and Section 10.1 settles.

The money market is not the risk-free rate. The five money-market series are kept outside the risky universe, and the reason is worth one calculation. US_money_market is a three-month instrument marked to market off a fitted curve rather than a bill held to maturity.

#>             0.188172             0.002688             0.930009 
#>          mm_drawdown 
#>            -0.009095

It tracks the bill, at a correlation of 0.93, but it carries duration: it loses money in 19% of months against the bill’s 0.3%. A risk-free rate has to be riskless over the holding period, and this series is not. We treat the money markets as short-duration assets and use sim_riskfree_monthly as the risk-free rate; every risk-free figure in this book comes from there.

Currency is a first-order driver. The four foreign money markets have volatilities near ten percent.

round(apply(M, 2, ann_vol), 3)
#> CH_money_market EU_money_market JP_money_market UK_money_market US_money_market 
#>           0.104           0.096           0.101           0.092           0.007

A three-month instrument has almost no duration, so unhedged in dollars it is very nearly a pure currency position, and the same currency sits inside every foreign bond and equity series in Table 4.1. Section 4.3 takes that apart.

4.2.2 Testing the distribution

Applying Equation 4.4 to each series gives one verdict and sixteen very different statistics, and their order is not the one intuition suggests.

jb_stat <- vapply(colnames(R), function(s) {
  x <- as.numeric(R[, s]); n <- length(x)
  s3 <- mean((x - mean(x))^3) / stats::sd(x)^3
  s4 <- mean((x - mean(x))^4) / stats::sd(x)^4 - 3
  n / 6 * (s3^2 + s4^2 / 4)
}, numeric(1))
jb <- stats::pchisq(jb_stat, df = 2, lower.tail = FALSE)

sum(jb < 0.05)                      # of 16
#> [1] 16
round(sort(jb_stat), 1)             # the statistic, closest to Gaussian first
#>    US_government        JP_equity    EU_government        CH_credit 
#>              8.0             18.5             23.2             31.7 
#> GLOBAL_commodity    CH_government        CH_equity        US_equity 
#>             39.7             48.1             68.3             74.0 
#>    UK_government        JP_credit        EU_equity        UK_equity 
#>            120.1            131.7            165.1            181.7 
#>    JP_government        EU_credit        US_credit        UK_credit 
#>            205.0            825.0           2420.7           2480.3

16 of 16 series reject normality at the five percent level, and the statistic runs from 8 to 2480. JP_equity, at 19.6% volatility the second most volatile series in the set, is the second closest to Gaussian, with a statistic of 18: its skewness is -0.28 and its excess kurtosis 0.96, against -1.45 and 12.24 for US_credit, whose statistic is 2421 at less than half the volatility. Volatility and non-normality are different properties, and this is the cleanest demonstration of it in the book.

The three largest statistics belong to credit series rather than to equity, and Equation 4.6 puts a number on what that costs.

For US_credit the two estimates disagree by a factor of 2.2. For US_equity they disagree by a factor of 1.11. A Gaussian risk model is roughly right for equities and roughly half right for credit, and US_credit is the series that ranked 3 of 16 on Sharpe ratio in Table 4.1.

NoteES() does not default to the historical estimate

PerformanceAnalytics::ES() defaults to method = "modified", so a call without that argument returns the Cornish-Fisher figure of Equation 4.6 rather than the sample tail. It is the better default, given how few observations a 95% tail contains, and it is rarely noticed. Whenever a reported expected shortfall is compared against another source, check which estimator produced it.

4.2.3 How well each moment is estimated

Equation 4.3 is a statement about standard errors under independence. Its practical counterpart is what a rolling window would have told an investor at each point in the sample.

win <- 60L
risky <- colnames(R)

roll_stat <- function(x, f) zoo::rollapply(x, win, f, align = "right")

instab <- lapply(risky, function(s) {
  x <- as.numeric(R[, s])
  full <- c(mean = mean(x), vol = stats::sd(x),
            skew = mean((x - mean(x))^3) / stats::sd(x)^3)
  rw <- list(mean = roll_stat(x, mean), vol = roll_stat(x, stats::sd),
             skew = roll_stat(x, function(z)
               mean((z - mean(z))^3) / stats::sd(z)^3))
  tibble::tibble(
    series = s, moment = names(full),
    rel_iqr   = vapply(names(full), function(m)
      as.numeric(stats::IQR(rw[[m]]) / abs(full[[m]])), numeric(1)),
    sign_flip = vapply(names(full), function(m)
      mean(sign(rw[[m]]) != sign(full[[m]])), numeric(1))
  )
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

instab_med <- instab |>
  dplyr::group_by(moment) |>
  dplyr::summarise(median_rel_iqr = median(rel_iqr),
                   median_sign_flip = median(sign_flip))
instab_med
#> # A tibble: 3 × 3
#>   moment median_rel_iqr median_sign_flip
#>   <chr>           <dbl>            <dbl>
#> 1 mean            1.18             0.107
#> 2 skew            1.48             0.342
#> 3 vol             0.289            0

Read the second column first. Across 16 risky series and 313 overlapping sixty-month windows, the rolling volatility never once disagrees in sign with its full-sample value, which it cannot, being a positive quantity, and for the median series its interquartile range is 29% of its own level. The rolling mean wanders by more than its own level, 118% of it. The rolling skewness disagrees in sign with the full-sample skewness in 34% of all windows.

That is Equation 4.3 made concrete, and it is the statement this book leans on repeatedly: volatility is estimable, the mean is not, and skewness will regularly report the opposite of the truth.

4.2.4 Drawdowns are episodes, not a number

Maximum drawdown is the summary an investor asks for first. PerformanceAnalytics will return it in one call, and that call discards most of what the reader needs.

4.3 Two Properties That Shape Every Portfolio

Neither is visible in a table of moments, and both decide what the optimizers of Part IV can achieve.

4.3.1 Fat tails are events, not a distribution

Table 4.1 reports an excess kurtosis for each series separately, which invites the reading that each series independently has a fat tail. That reading is wrong, and the error matters for a portfolio, because independent fat tails diversify and shared ones do not.

Rr  <- R[, risky]
z   <- scale(Rr)
hit <- rowSums(abs(z) > 2.5)
p   <- mean(abs(z) > 2.5)

obs <- table(factor(hit, levels = 0:ncol(Rr)))
exp <- stats::dbinom(0:ncol(Rr), ncol(Rr), p) * nrow(Rr)

tibble::tibble(k = 0:ncol(Rr), observed = as.integer(obs), expected = round(exp, 2)) |>
  dplyr::filter(k >= 3, observed > 0)
#> # A tibble: 6 × 3
#>       k observed expected
#>   <int>    <int>    <dbl>
#> 1     3        9     1.91
#> 2     4        4     0.15
#> 3     5        2     0.01
#> 4     7        1     0   
#> 5     9        1     0   
#> 6    13        1     0

Months in which five or more of the 16 risky series moved beyond two and a half standard deviations number 5 observed against 0.01 expected under independence. The excess is not marginal; it is a different phenomenon.

d <- zoo::index(Rr)
w <- d[which.max(hit)]
stopifnot(in_episode(w, "credit_crisis"))   # the text below places it there
c(month = fmt_model(w), series_beyond_2.5sd = max(hit), of = ncol(Rr))
#>                      month        series_beyond_2.5sd 
#> "October of model year 17"                       "13" 
#>                         of 
#>                       "16"

In October of model year 17, a month of the credit crisis described below, 13 of the 16 risky series moved beyond two and a half standard deviations at once. The fat tail in this market is not sixteen fat tails. It is 5 months in which a large part of the market moves together, which is the joint counterpart of the marginal non-normality Equation 4.4 detected, and the reason Longin and Solnik (2001) study correlation in the tails rather than on average.

4.3.2 The crises are not one crisis

We keep four episodes apart throughout the book, because they are economically different and a lesson learned from one is false in another. They ship with the data as sim_episodes, each named for what happens in it, and Appendix A tabulates them.

ep  <- sim_episodes                 # episode, label, start, end, description
cum <- vapply(ep$episode, function(e) {
  apply(Rr[in_episode(d, e), ], 2, function(x) prod(1 + x) - 1)
}, numeric(ncol(Rr)))
round(100 * cum[c("US_equity", "US_government", "JP_government",
                  "US_credit", "EU_credit", "GLOBAL_commodity"), ], 1)
#>                  equity_bear credit_crisis liquidity_shock rates_shock
#> US_equity              -33.6         -27.9           -10.0       -13.8
#> US_government           34.0           8.2             6.2       -10.7
#> JP_government           -6.9          14.6             2.3       -15.9
#> US_credit               28.1          -8.5            -2.0       -15.1
#> EU_credit               17.3         -13.1            -8.4       -23.3
#> GLOBAL_commodity        20.7         -19.6            -1.9        11.8

In the credit crisis the safest government bonds did what the textbook says they do: US_government gained 8.2% while US_equity lost 27.9%, and credit fell with equity, US_credit by 8.5%. The equity bear market was a different decline. US_equity lost 33.6% over 30 months while US_credit gained 28.1% beside a government bond that gained 34.0%, so credit sided with bonds in one equity decline and with equities in the other. In the 3 months of the liquidity shock 14 of the 16 series fell. In the rates shock the government bond that had gained in each of the other three episodes lost 10.7%, every government bond and credit series lost with it, and only 2 of the 16 series gained.

names(which(cum[, "rates_shock"] > 0))
#> [1] "GLOBAL_commodity" "UK_equity"

One of them, GLOBAL_commodity, had lost 19.6% in the credit crisis. A book that teaches diversification from the credit crisis alone teaches a rule that reverses in the rates shock, which is why every backtest in Section 25.3 reports these episodes separately rather than averaging over them.

The correlation matrix says the same thing in the form an optimizer will feel.

mean_cor <- function(idx) {
  C <- stats::cor(Rr[idx, ]); mean(C[upper.tri(C)])
}
in_ep <- vapply(ep$episode, function(e) in_episode(d, e), logical(nrow(Rr)))
calm  <- rowSums(in_ep) == 0

regime <- tibble::tibble(
  regime   = c("calm", ep$episode),
  months   = c(sum(calm), unname(colSums(in_ep))),
  mean_cor = c(mean_cor(calm), unname(apply(in_ep, 2, mean_cor)))
)
regime
#> # A tibble: 5 × 3
#>   regime          months mean_cor
#>   <chr>            <dbl>    <dbl>
#> 1 calm               320    0.297
#> 2 equity_bear         30    0.219
#> 3 credit_crisis        7    0.509
#> 4 liquidity_shock      3    0.719
#> 5 rates_shock         12    0.612

Average pairwise correlation rises from 0.30 in calm months to 0.51 through the credit crisis and 0.61 through the rates shock. In the equity bear market it falls to 0.22, because bonds rose while equities fell. The diversification an optimizer found in the calm sample is not the diversification available when it is needed. Longin and Solnik (2001) and Ang and Chen (2002) document the same asymmetry in real equity markets: correlations rise in falling markets and not in rising ones.

One caution about the numbers themselves. The credit crisis is 7 months and the liquidity shock 3, and a correlation matrix estimated on so few observations is a description rather than an estimate. Forbes and Rigobon (2002) show further that measured correlation rises mechanically with volatility even when the underlying dependence is unchanged, so a rise of this kind is not by itself evidence of contagion. The cumulative returns above carry far more weight than the correlations do.

4.3.3 Currency is not one decision

Everything here is unhedged in dollars, so a foreign holding carries its local return and its currency’s move together. How much of it is currency depends on the asset rather than on the country, and that is the non-obvious part.

fx <- sim_fx_monthly
share <- function(series, region) {
  u <- as.numeric(R[, series])
  f <- fx$fx_return[fx$region == region][
        match(zoo::index(R), fx$date[fx$region == region])]
  c(unhedged  = ann_vol(u),
    hedged    = ann_vol(u - f),
    fx_share  = 1 - stats::var(u - f) / stats::var(u),
    if_indep  = stats::var(f) / stats::var(u))
}
round(rbind(
  JP_government = share("JP_government", "JP"),
  JP_equity     = share("JP_equity", "JP"),
  CH_equity     = share("CH_equity", "CH"),
  EU_credit     = share("EU_credit", "EU")
), 3)
#>               unhedged hedged fx_share if_indep
#> JP_government    0.107  0.031    0.916    0.882
#> JP_equity        0.196  0.212   -0.175    0.265
#> CH_equity        0.182  0.179    0.034    0.318
#> EU_credit        0.125  0.066    0.717    0.584

Hedging JP_government takes its volatility from 10.7% to 3.1%. Hedging JP_equity takes it from 19.6% to 21.2%, which is to say it removes nothing and adds a little.

The if_indep column explains why. Writing \(r^{\text{usd}} = r^{\text{loc}} + f\) for the unhedged return of a foreign asset, where \(f\) is the currency return, the variance decomposes as

\[ \operatorname{Var}(r^{\text{usd}}) = \operatorname{Var}(r^{\text{loc}}) + \operatorname{Var}(f) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(r^{\text{loc}}, f). \tag{4.7}\]

If the local return and the currency were unrelated the covariance term would vanish and the currency’s share of variance would be \(\operatorname{Var}(f)/\operatorname{Var}(r^{\text{usd}})\), which is the if_indep column. For JP_equity that benchmark is 26% and the measured share is -18%. The gap is a negative covariance: the Japanese market rises when the yen falls, so the hedge is supplied by the economics rather than by a forward contract. For EU_credit the covariance runs the other way and hedging removes more than the currency’s own share.

The practical conclusion matches Campbell et al. (2010), who find that the optimal currency hedge differs systematically between bonds and equities because currencies covary differently with each. Whether to hedge is therefore not one question with one answer per currency. It is a different question for the bond sleeve and the equity sleeve of the same country, and Section 15.2 treats it as such. Perold and Schulman (1988) and Black (1989) are the two classic and opposed statements of the general case.

Key Takeaways

  • The four moments of Equation 4.1 and Equation 4.2 are not four facts of the same kind. By Equation 4.3 the mean’s precision depends only on the calendar span, while the variance improves with the number of observations, so sampling more finely helps one and not the other (Merton 1980).
  • Measured on sixty-month rolling windows, volatility never changes sign and moves by 29% of its level, the mean moves by 118% of its own level, and skewness reverses sign in 34% of windows.
  • Non-normality and volatility are unrelated. All 16 series reject the normality test of Equation 4.4, but JP_equity, the second most volatile, is the second closest to Gaussian, while US_credit, at less than half its volatility, has two estimates of expected shortfall that differ by a factor of 2.2.
  • Fat tails in this market are events rather than a distribution. 5 months show joint exceedances of a kind that independence would produce once in 114 samples of this length. Independent fat tails diversify; shared ones do not.
  • The four episodes of sim_episodes are not one crisis. Average correlation rises in the credit crisis and the rates shock and falls in the equity bear market, and a correlation estimated on a 7-month window is a description, part of whose rise is mechanical (Forbes and Rigobon 2002).
  • The currency share of a foreign holding is a property of the asset, not of the country. Hedging a Japanese government bond removes most of its volatility; hedging Japanese equity removes none (Campbell et al. 2010).

Exercises

  1. sim_class_returns_weekly holds the same market at the model’s native frequency. Recompute Table 4.1 on it, annualizing by \(\sqrt{52}\) instead of \(\sqrt{12}\). Which of the six columns change materially and which do not? Explain the pattern using Equation 4.3.
  2. The exceedance count above used a threshold of 2.5 standard deviations. Repeat it at 2, 3 and 4. At which threshold does the excess over independence first appear, and what does that tell you about where in the distribution the dependence lives?
  3. Pick two series with similar volatility but opposite skewness, for example US_credit and JP_government. Construct the equally weighted combination and compute its skewness. Is it the average of the two? Explain why not.
  4. Using sim_fx_monthly, build the fully hedged version of all sixteen risky series and recompute the average pairwise correlation in calm months and in each of the four episodes. Does hedging change the diversification of the set, or only its volatility?
  5. Verify Equation 4.3 by simulation. Draw 30 years of returns at monthly and at weekly frequency from the same process, estimate the mean and the variance from each, repeat 1,000 times, and compare the two sets of standard errors against the formula.
