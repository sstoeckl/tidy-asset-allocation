7  Covariance

The question. Sigma has far too many free parameters. What do you do about it?

You have an equity sleeve to fill. There are a hundred and twenty-three names in the eligible universe, you have thirty-one years of monthly returns, and you would like the combination of them with the lowest risk. That is the most modest request anyone ever makes of a portfolio optimiser: no view, no forecast, no tilt, just the quiet corner of the frontier.

You will not get it. Not because the data is poor and not because the optimiser is badly written, but because the object it needs in order to answer cannot be estimated from the data you have. This chapter is about recognising that situation, understanding what it costs you as an investor rather than as a statistician, and choosing between the four things you can do instead.

cls <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)
cls <- cls[, !grepl("money_market", colnames(cls))]

ins <- as_xts_pm(sim_asset_returns_monthly, date, asset, return)
eq  <- ins[, colnames(ins) %in% sim_assets$asset_id[sim_assets$asset_class == "equity"]]

c(classes = ncol(cls), equities = ncol(eq), months = nrow(cls))
#>  classes equities   months 
#>       16      123      372

7.1 What you need

7.1.1 What the optimiser is asking you for

A minimum-variance portfolio is the weight vector that makes portfolio variance as small as your constraints allow, and the covariance matrix is the entire input. Nothing else about the assets enters: not their names, not their sectors, not their expected returns. Whatever the matrix says about risk is risk as far as the optimiser is concerned.

It holds \(N(N+1)/2\) distinct numbers. The sixteen asset classes of Section 3.1 need 136. The 123 equities need 7,626, from 372 months of history. You are asking the data to pin down more parameters than it has observations, and at some point the answer stops being an estimate.

7.1.2 The investor’s version of the failure

The statistical statement is that the sample covariance matrix has rank at most the number of observations, so with fewer months than assets it is singular. True, and not the part that should worry you.

The part that should worry you is what a singular matrix claims about portfolios. If the matrix is singular then some combination of your assets has an estimated variance of exactly zero: a portfolio the matrix says cannot lose money. There are as many independent such portfolios as the matrix is short of full rank. An optimiser told to minimise variance will find them, because that is precisely its job, and it will put everything there.

They are not real. They are long-short cancellations that happened to net out over the particular months you estimated on, and they will not net out again. This is the mechanism behind the familiar complaint that mean-variance optimisation produces absurd weights. Michaud (1989) called the optimiser an error maximiser for this reason, and the label is exact rather than rhetorical: among all the portfolios consistent with your constraints, the procedure is designed to select the one the estimate is most optimistic about, which is the one the estimate is most wrong about.

Chopra and Ziemba (1993) measured how the cost divides. Errors in expected returns hurt an order of magnitude more than errors in variances, which in turn hurt more than errors in covariances — which is why Section 6.1 is the harder chapter and why this one is nonetheless unavoidable: a singular matrix is not an error of degree.

7.1.3 Telling before the solver tells you

Two numbers, neither of which requires inverting anything.

covariance_health <- function(X) {
  S  <- stats::cov(zoo::coredata(X))
  ev <- eigen(S, symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
  c(assets = ncol(X), observations = nrow(X), rank = qr(S)$rank,
    riskless_directions = sum(ev < 1e-12),
    condition = if (min(ev) <= 0) Inf else max(ev) / min(ev))
}

rbind(classes  = covariance_health(cls[1:60, ]),
      equities = covariance_health(eq[1:60, ]))
#>          assets observations rank riskless_directions condition
#> classes      16           60   16                   0     16995
#> equities    123           60   59                  64       Inf

rank below assets means the matrix is singular, and riskless_directions counts the portfolios it believes are free. Five years of data on 123 equities produces 64 of them.

condition is the subtler warning. It is the ratio of the largest eigenvalue to the smallest, and it bounds how much the inverse magnifies an error in the input. A finite but very large value is more dangerous than an infinite one, because everything still runs: the matrix inverts, weights come back, and they are a magnified copy of the sampling noise. Past roughly \(10^{8}\) you are reading noise with double precision arithmetic.

rank_report <- function(X, label) {
  vapply(c(60L, 120L, 180L), function(W) {
    S  <- stats::cov(zoo::coredata(X[seq_len(W), ]))
    ev <- eigen(S, symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
    c(T = W, N = ncol(X), rank = qr(S)$rank, min_eigen = min(ev),
      condition = if (min(ev) <= 0) Inf else max(ev) / min(ev))
  }, numeric(5)) |> t() |> as.data.frame() |> dplyr::mutate(universe = label)
}

dplyr::bind_rows(rank_report(cls, "16 classes"),
                 rank_report(eq,  "123 equities")) |>
  dplyr::select(universe, T, N, rank, min_eigen, condition)
#> # A tibble: 6 × 6
#>   universe         T     N  rank min_eigen condition
#>   <chr>        <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>
#> 1 16 classes      60    16    16  5.63e- 7    16995.
#> 2 16 classes     120    16    16  9.41e- 7    11153.
#> 3 16 classes     180    16    16  8.78e- 7    10929.
#> 4 123 equities    60   123    59 -3.81e-17      Inf 
#> 5 123 equities   120   123   119 -1.21e-18      Inf 
#> 6 123 equities   180   123   123  1.50e- 4     1510.

Look at min_eigen on the two singular rows. It is negative. A covariance matrix cannot have a negative eigenvalue; that is floating-point noise around an eigenvalue which is exactly zero. The matrix is not imprecise. It describes a world in which some portfolio has negative variance.

7.1.4 Why this book has not needed the chapter until now

Every allocation up to here happened across asset classes, and at that level the problem does not arise.

bad <- sum(vapply(seq.int(61L, nrow(cls)), function(i) {
  qr(stats::cov(zoo::coredata(cls[(i - 60L):(i - 1L), ])))$rank < ncol(cls)
}, logical(1)))

c(rolling_windows = nrow(cls) - 60L, rank_deficient = bad)
#> rolling_windows  rank_deficient 
#>             312               0

Not one sixty-month window in thirty-one years is rank deficient at class level. The moment Section 20.1 descends into individual securities it becomes the only question that matters, and nothing about the data changed. What changed is how many things you asked to hold.

7.2 How to actually do it

There are four routes. Two change what you are allowed to own, two change how you estimate. Take them in the order of what they cost you.

7.2.1 Route one: hold fewer things

The cheapest fix is not to have the problem. Allocate across sixteen classes rather than 123 securities, or across sector sleeves rather than names, and the matrix is comfortably estimable on five years.

This is not a dodge. Most institutional mandates are set at exactly this level for exactly this reason, and Section 15.1 treats the sleeve structure as a decision rather than a convenience. It does not help when the mandate is “these 123 names”, which is what a stock selection process hands you.

7.2.2 Route two: impose a structure

If you cannot estimate 7,626 free parameters, stop trying to. Assert that returns are driven by a few common factors plus asset-specific noise. The covariance then follows from the factor loadings and the residual variances, which for three factors on 123 assets is 492 numbers rather than several thousand. Fan et al. (2008) give the conditions under which the factor estimate converges where the sample estimate does not.

The factors can be economic, as in Section 4.1, or purely statistical — the principal components of the returns themselves. The second is six lines.

factor_covariance <- function(X, k = 3L) {
  x  <- zoo::coredata(X)
  pc <- prcomp(x)
  B  <- pc$rotation[, seq_len(k), drop = FALSE]      # loadings, N x k
  f  <- x %*% B                                      # factor realisations
  e  <- x - f %*% t(B)                               # residuals
  e  <- sweep(e, 2, colMeans(e))
  B %*% stats::cov(f) %*% t(B) + diag(colSums(e^2) / (nrow(x) - k - 1L))
}

S_fac <- factor_covariance(eq[1:120, ], k = 3L)
e_fac <- eigen(S_fac, symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
c(rank = qr(S_fac)$rank, min_eigen = min(e_fac),
  condition = max(e_fac) / min(e_fac))
#>       rank  min_eigen  condition 
#> 123.000000   0.001779 138.255107

Full rank on a window where the sample matrix has 4 riskless directions, and a condition number four orders of magnitude smaller. The structure does the work: because every asset’s risk runs through three shared factors, no combination can cancel to zero unless the residuals do, and those are positive by construction.

PortfolioAnalytics ships this as statistical.factor.model(), with one constraint worth knowing about in the released version.

sapply(c(60L, 120L, 180L), function(W) {
  tryCatch({ statistical.factor.model(eq[seq_len(W), ], k = 3L); "accepted" },
           error = function(e) conditionMessage(e))
})
#> [1] "fewer observations than assets" "fewer observations than assets"
#> [3] "accepted"

It requires at least as many observations as assets, which is the condition under which you did not need it. The body does not: principal components need enough components, not enough columns, which is why the six lines above run on the same window. Appendix B records the patch that relaxes the check and where it stands.

7.2.3 Route three: shrink the matrix

Keep every asset and keep the sample estimate, but pull it part of the way towards a target that is well conditioned by construction — commonly a scaled identity, sometimes a constant-correlation or factor matrix. Ledoit and Wolf (2003) and Ledoit and Wolf (2004) show that the intensity of the pull can be estimated from the data itself, so it is not a tuning parameter you have to guess. Schäfer and Strimmer (2005) give the analytic form that corpcor implements.

This is the workhorse. It needs no assumption about what drives returns, it cannot fail, and the result is invertible whenever the target is and the intensity is above zero.

S_shr <- corpcor::cov.shrink(zoo::coredata(eq[1:120, ]), verbose = FALSE)
c(lambda = attr(S_shr, "lambda"),
  rank = qr(S_shr)$rank,
  min_eigen = min(eigen(as.matrix(S_shr), symmetric = TRUE,
                        only.values = TRUE)$values))
#>      lambda        rank   min_eigen 
#>   0.1618718 123.0000000   0.0008539

lambda is the weight on the target rather than on the data, about a sixth of the way towards independence here.

7.2.4 Route four: estimate robustly

The three routes above address a matrix that is badly conditioned because the sample is short. A robust estimator addresses one that is badly centred because a few observations dominate it. Different problem, and Section 7.3 takes it.

7.2.5 Getting any of this into the optimiser

PortfolioAnalytics routes every moment estimate through one function, and the released version of that function does not offer shrinkage.

eval(formals(set.portfolio.moments)$method)
#> [1] "sample"          "boudt"           "black_litterman" "meucci"

sample is the sample matrix, boudt a factor-based cleaning of the higher moments, black_litterman and meucci view-blending machinery belonging in Section 17.1. None of them is route three.

This is the point at which people give up and compute weights outside the package, which throws away every constraint and diagnostic it provides. It is unnecessary. optimize.portfolio() takes a momentFUN argument and accepts a function object, not merely the name of one.

shrinkage_moments <- function(R, portfolio) {
  X <- zoo::coredata(R)
  S <- corpcor::cov.shrink(X, verbose = FALSE)
  S <- matrix(as.numeric(S), ncol(X), ncol(X),
              dimnames = list(colnames(X), colnames(X)))
  list(mu = colMeans(X), sigma = S, m3 = NULL, m4 = NULL,
       mean = colMeans(X), var = S)
}

spec <- portfolio.spec(colnames(eq)) |>
  add.constraint("full_investment") |> add.constraint("long_only") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")

opt <- optimize.portfolio(eq[1:120, ], spec, optimize_method = "ROI",
                          momentFUN = shrinkage_moments)
w <- extractWeights(opt)
c(holdings = sum(w > 1e-6), sum = sum(w), max_weight = max(w))
#>   holdings        sum max_weight 
#>    39.0000     1.0000     0.1173

Because it is a function rather than a name it can be built on the fly with its settings baked in — one per shrinkage intensity you want to compare, or one that closes over a factor model fitted elsewhere. Nothing has to live in the global environment.

Two details save an afternoon. The list carries both mu/sigma and mean/var: different code paths inside the package read different names, and a moment function setting only one pair works until the day you change objective. And the matrix must be a plain matrix with dimnames, because cov.shrink() returns extra attributes that some downstream code does not expect.

7.2.6 When the package function is the thing in the way

There is a third option beyond using it and working around it, and on an open source package it is usually the right one: fix it and send the fix back. The constraint in statistical.factor.model() is one line, the reason it is wrong is demonstrable in a paragraph, and a patch with a test is smaller work than the workaround it replaces. Appendix B lists the patches this book contributed and their state, which is also the honest way to tell a reader which behaviour they will see.

7.2.7 Does any of it pay?

Not a question the matrix can answer. Every estimator reports a flattering number about itself in sample. The only honest test is to estimate on one window, hold the portfolio for a month, and score what happened.

R/ch05-estimator-race.R does that for four estimators and three window lengths, long only and fully invested, rebalanced monthly. All three windows are scored on the same months, so a longer window earns no credit for starting later.

FIRST_OOS <- 181L
oos    <- seq.int(FIRST_OOS, nrow(eq))
ew_ret <- rowMeans(zoo::coredata(eq[oos, ]))
ew_ex  <- ew_ret - sim_riskfree_monthly$return[oos]

ew <- c(ann_vol = stats::sd(ew_ret) * sqrt(12),
        sharpe  = mean(ew_ex) / stats::sd(ew_ex) * sqrt(12))
round(ew, 4)
#> ann_vol  sharpe 
#>  0.2024  0.2258

Read failed first. At sixty and a hundred and twenty months two of the four estimators produce nothing — not a poor portfolio, nothing, on every one of the 192 rebalancing dates. Route three and the diagonal target get as far as a weight vector; the sample matrix and the released factor model do not.

Where they run, shrinkage gives the lowest volatility at every window: 13.5% at a hundred and twenty months against 18.4% for the diagonal and 20.2% for holding everything equally.

Then the row that keeps this from being an advertisement.

estimator ann_vol sharpe lambda
sample2 sample 0.1327 0.082 NA
shrunk2 shrunk 0.1328 0.112 0.096
diagonal2 diagonal 0.1852 0.231 NA
factor32 factor3 0.1310 0.089 NA

At a hundred and eighty months the sample matrix is finally estimable and shrinkage stops buying anything: 0.1328 against 0.1327 is a dead heat, and the factor model is marginally ahead of both. Shrinkage is not a better estimator in general. It is the estimator that exists when the others do not, which is a different and more useful property.

The lambda column says so from the inside: it falls from 0.241 to 0.096 as the window lengthens. The procedure asks for less help as it needs less, which is the practical content of the Ledoit-Wolf result.

7.2.8 The column that should make you uncomfortable

sharpe is not ann_vol, and the ordering is not the same.

Minimum variance did what it was asked. It cut annualised volatility from 20.2% to 13.5%, about a third. It also finished behind equal weighting on Sharpe ratio, 0.163 against 0.226. The risk came out; so did the return. DeMiguel et al. (2009) found the same ordering across a range of universes and estimators, which is the subject of Section 8.1.

7.3 What else is in there

PortfolioAnalytics ships a family of robust covariance estimators that almost nobody uses and that no textbook of this generation covers.

grep("covRob", ls("package:PortfolioAnalytics"), value = TRUE)
#> [1] "custom.covRob.Mcd"   "custom.covRob.MM"    "custom.covRob.Rocke"
#> [4] "custom.covRob.TSGS"  "MycovRobMcd"         "MycovRobTSGS"

They answer route four. Shrinkage repairs a matrix badly conditioned because the sample is short. A robust estimator repairs one badly centred because a few months dominate it: a flash crash, a redenomination, a bad print in a vendor file. The minimum covariance determinant estimator behind custom.covRob.Mcd() searches for the subset of observations with the smallest generalised variance and estimates from that, discarding the rest.

They plug in exactly where route three did.

robust_moments <- function(R, portfolio) {
  m <- custom.covRob.Mcd(zoo::coredata(R))
  list(mu = m$mu, sigma = m$sigma, m3 = NULL, m4 = NULL,
       mean = m$mu, var = m$sigma)
}

rb <- robust_moments(cls[1:120, ])
sb <- stats::cov(zoo::coredata(cls[1:120, ]))
c(robust_mean_vol = mean(sqrt(diag(rb$sigma))) * sqrt(12),
  sample_mean_vol = mean(sqrt(diag(sb))) * sqrt(12))
#> robust_mean_vol sample_mean_vol 
#>          0.1165          0.1329

The robust estimate of volatility is lower, because it has decided that part of what the sample matrix calls risk is a few unusual months rather than the process. Whether that is an improvement is a judgement about the world, not about the estimator: right if those months were contamination, wrong if they were the tail you are paid to bear. Section 27.1 makes the case properly, and Section 10.1 is where discarding the tail stops being defensible.

7.4 Why it works

Every route is one operation on the eigenvalues, and it is easier to see than to derive.

X   <- zoo::coredata(eq[1:120, ])
ev  <- function(S) pmax(eigen(as.matrix(S), symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values, 0)
e_s <- ev(stats::cov(X))
e_h <- ev(corpcor::cov.shrink(X, verbose = FALSE))

tibble::tibble(
  matrix      = c("sample", "shrunk"),
  largest     = c(e_s[1], e_h[1]),
  smallest    = c(min(e_s), min(e_h)),
  zero_eigens = c(sum(e_s < 1e-12), sum(e_h < 1e-12)),
  condition   = c(e_s[1] / max(min(e_s), .Machine$double.eps), e_h[1] / min(e_h)))
#> # A tibble: 2 × 5
#>   matrix largest smallest zero_eigens condition
#>   <chr>    <dbl>    <dbl>       <int>     <dbl>
#> 1 sample   0.240 0                  4   1.08e15
#> 2 shrunk   0.200 0.000854           0   2.34e 2

4 eigenvalues of the sample matrix are zero. Those are the riskless portfolios of the first section seen from the other side, and they are not a property of these particular equities: they would be there if the returns were coin flips, because 120 observations cannot pin down 123 directions.

The largest falls by 16.5% and the smallest non-zero one rises by a factor of 509. That is what a shrinkage target is: not a better guess at any individual covariance, but a statement that the extremes of a sample spectrum are mostly sampling error and belong nearer the middle. A factor model reaches the same place by another road, building the matrix from a few directions plus a positive residual for every asset, so small eigenvalues cannot be zero to begin with.

7.4.1 Shrink towards what, when the target moves?

The usual target is a scaled identity: equal variances, no correlations. It is chosen for being maximally unlike the noisy thing it corrects, not for being true. How untrue it is depends on when you ask.

d <- zoo::index(cls)
periods <- list(calm   = d >= as.Date("1993-01-01") & d <= as.Date("1997-12-31"),
                crisis = d >= as.Date("2007-07-01") & d <= as.Date("2009-06-30"))

regime_shape <- function(keep) {
  e <- ev(stats::cov(zoo::coredata(cls[keep, ])))
  C <- stats::cor(cls[keep, ])
  data.frame(months = sum(keep), pc1_share = e[1] / sum(e),
             directions = sum(e)^2 / sum(e^2),
             mean_correlation = mean(C[upper.tri(C)]))
}

shape <- do.call(rbind, lapply(periods, regime_shape))
shape$regime <- rownames(shape)
shape[, c("regime", "months", "pc1_share", "directions", "mean_correlation")]
#> # A tibble: 2 × 5
#>   regime months pc1_share directions mean_correlation
#>   <chr>   <int>     <dbl>      <dbl>            <dbl>
#> 1 calm       60     0.428       4.12            0.280
#> 2 crisis     24     0.691       2.00            0.439

In the calm stretch the first principal component carries 43% of the variance and the matrix behaves like roughly 4.1 independent directions. Through the crisis the same sixteen series carry 69% in the first component and collapse to 2.0. Average pairwise correlation goes from 0.28 to 0.44.

A diagonal target asserts the assets are unrelated. In calm markets that is wrong by a little; in a crisis it is wrong by a lot, and precisely when the portfolio depends on it. Shrinking towards independence is least defensible exactly when it does the most work — the honest reason to prefer a factor target where you can have one, and Section 19.1 is where the choice stops being a footnote.

7.4.2 The shrinkage you have been using all along

One last connection, because it reframes most of this book. Every optimisation in chapters 7 to 12 was long only. Jagannathan and Ma (2003) showed that a non-negativity constraint is not merely a restriction: it acts on the solution the way shrinkage acts on the matrix, because it forbids exactly the large offsetting long-short positions that a noisy \(\Sigma\) invites. Their empirical finding is that imposing the constraint improves out-of-sample risk even when it is known to be wrong, which is why a constrained optimisation on a sample matrix is far better behaved than the unconstrained arithmetic suggests.

That is worth holding next to Table 7.1. Route three bought a third off the volatility of an equal-weighted sleeve. The long-only constraint, which you imposed for reasons of mandate rather than of statistics, had already done a comparable amount of work for free.

Exercises

  1. Rerun Table 7.1 with the money-market series added to the equity universe. The covariance is now singular for a second, unrelated reason. Identify it from the eigenvectors rather than from the asset names.
  2. corpcor::cov.shrink() chooses lambda analytically. Fix it instead at 0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5 and 1 and plot realised out-of-sample volatility against it. Where is the minimum, and is the analytic choice near it?
  3. Replace shrinkage_moments() with one built on factor_covariance() and compare them on the same rolling windows. At which window length does the factor structure stop helping, and does that coincide with the point where the sample matrix becomes estimable?
  4. Repeat the unconstrained minimum-variance optimisation of Section 9.1 on the 123 equities with the shrunk matrix, once long only and once with weights free to go negative. Quantify the Jagannathan and Ma (2003) effect on this data.
