5  Factor Models and Beta Estimation

Draft|Code: script · exercises · all chapters|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

Section 4.3 described the market one series at a time. This chapter asks what the series have in common. That question has a canonical answer, the single-factor model of Sharpe (1964), Lintner (1965) and Black (1972), in which an asset’s expected excess return is proportional to its covariance with the market. The regression form of it, due to Jensen (1968), is what every risk report still prints: a beta that is the input to a hedge, and an alpha that is the claim an active manager makes.

We first set out the model and what its components mean, then estimate it on this market, and then examine how much of the resulting explanation survives being measured properly. The finding worth stating in advance is that most of it does not. Outside equity the typical beta changes sign in about a third of all estimation windows, an apparent asymmetry between rising and falling markets survives inference in two cases out of sixteen, and a factor that appears to add explanatory power turns out to be sorting on something other than its name.

5.1 The Single-Factor Model

Write \(r_{it}\) for the return on asset \(i\) in period \(t\), \(r_{ft}\) for the risk-free rate, and \(r_{mt}\) for the return on the market. Returns enter in excess of the bill throughout, for the reasons set out in Section 3.1. The regression is

\[ r_{it} - r_{ft} = \alpha_i + \beta_i\left(r_{mt} - r_{ft}\right) + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{it}] = 0, \quad \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_{it}, r_{mt}) = 0, \tag{5.1}\]

with the population slope

\[ \beta_i = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(r_i, r_m)}{\operatorname{Var}(r_m)} . \tag{5.2}\]

Under the asset pricing theory, \(\alpha_i = 0\) for every asset. Under the regression read as a statistical description, \(\alpha_i\) is whatever the market does not account for, and Equation 5.1 splits the variance of each asset into a part the market explains and a part it does not:

\[ \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(r_i)}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\beta_i^2 \operatorname{Var}(r_m)}_{\text{systematic}} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i)}_{\text{idiosyncratic}}, \qquad R^2_i = \frac{\beta_i^2\operatorname{Var}(r_m)}{\operatorname{Var}(r_i)} . \tag{5.3}\]

The \(R^2\) is therefore not a goodness-of-fit statistic in the usual decorative sense. It is the share of an asset’s risk that the model claims to describe, and an asset with a low \(R^2\) is one the model has nothing to say about.

Two extensions matter here. The first allows the slope to differ in rising and falling markets, in the spirit of the lower partial moment framework of Bawa and Lindenberg (1977) and the downside betas of Ang et al. (2006):

\[ r_{it} - r_{ft} = \alpha_i + \beta_i\,\tilde{r}_{mt} + \gamma_i\,\tilde{r}_{mt}\mathbf{1}\{\tilde{r}_{mt} < 0\} + \varepsilon_{it}, \tag{5.4}\]

where \(\tilde{r}_{mt} = r_{mt} - r_{ft}\), so that \(\beta_i\) is the beta in up markets and \(\beta_i + \gamma_i\) the beta in down markets. The second allows the slope to move over time, which is the conditional CAPM of Ferson and Harvey (1991) and Jagannathan and Wang (1996) and which we approach here descriptively, through rolling windows.

5.1.1 Inference is where these models are usually lost

Monthly returns are serially dependent and heteroskedastic, so the textbook standard error of an ordinary least squares slope is wrong. Throughout this chapter we use the heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent estimator of Newey and West (1987), which replaces \(\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}\) with

\[ (X'X)^{-1}\left(\sum_{j=-L}^{L} w_j \hat{\Gamma}_j\right)(X'X)^{-1}, \qquad w_j = 1 - \frac{|j|}{L+1}, \tag{5.5}\]

where \(\hat{\Gamma}_j\) is the sample autocovariance of the score at lag \(j\) and \(L\) the bandwidth, six lags here.

The reason to insist on this is that Equation 5.4 is estimated sixteen times, once per series, and any procedure repeated sixteen times will produce significant-looking differences whether or not any exist. Harvey et al. (2016) make the case at scale for the cross-section of expected returns; the same arithmetic applies to a table of sixteen betas.

5.2 Implementation

A factor model needs a factor. The obvious one here is the market, and it has to be built rather than assumed. We build it from the instrument universe described in Section 2.2, not from the class series, because a capitalization weight only exists at instrument level.

returns <- sim_class_returns_monthly
R  <- as_xts_pm(returns, date, class, return)
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return
risky <- colnames(R)
stopifnot(length(risky) == 16L)

# Capitalization-weighted global equity, from all 123 equity instruments. The
# weight in month t is the last capitalization observed on or before the end of
# t-1: a weight an investor could actually have held.
eq  <- sim_assets$asset_id[sim_assets$asset_class == "equity"]
Ra  <- as_xts_pm(sim_asset_returns_monthly[sim_asset_returns_monthly$asset %in% eq, ],
                 date, asset, return)
cap <- sim_marketcap_weekly[sim_marketcap_weekly$asset %in% eq, ]
d   <- zoo::index(Ra)
stopifnot(ncol(Ra) == 123L, identical(d, zoo::index(R)))

W <- t(vapply(seq_along(d), function(i) {
  cutoff <- if (i == 1) d[1] - 40 else d[i - 1]
  cc   <- cap[cap$date <= cutoff, ]
  last <- cc[!duplicated(cc$asset, fromLast = TRUE), ]
  w <- last$market_cap[match(colnames(Ra), last$asset)]
  w[is.na(w)] <- 0
  if (sum(w) == 0) rep(1 / ncol(Ra), ncol(Ra)) else w / sum(w)
}, numeric(ncol(Ra))))

MKT   <- rowSums(W * zoo::coredata(Ra))
mkt_e <- MKT - rf

The weights are lagged by one period deliberately. A capitalization weight observed at the end of month \(t\) is not available for investing during month \(t\), and using it would give the proxy a return no investor could have earned.

c(annualized = ann_ret(MKT), volatility = ann_vol(MKT))
#> annualized volatility 
#>     0.1234     0.1488

# Which region is this "global" market, really?
usm <- sim_assets$region[match(colnames(Ra), sim_assets$asset_id)] == "US"
us_share <- rowSums(W[, usm])
c(first = us_share[1], mean = mean(us_share), last = us_share[length(us_share)])
#>  first   mean   last 
#> 0.4889 0.5696 0.6998

The second line deserves attention before any beta in this chapter is used. The proxy is on average 57% United States by weight and ends the sample at 70%. It is global in construction and American in effect. Every beta below is largely a beta on the United States market, and Roll (1977) is the standing reminder that a test of Equation 5.1 is a joint test of the model and of whatever proxy stands in for the market.

5.2.1 One factor, sixteen series

fit1 <- lapply(colnames(R), function(s) {
  y <- as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf
  m <- stats::lm(y ~ mkt_e)
  nw <- sandwich::NeweyWest(m, lag = 6, prewhite = FALSE)
  tibble::tibble(series = s,
                 alpha_ann = 12 * stats::coef(m)[1],
                 alpha_t   = stats::coef(m)[1] / sqrt(nw[1, 1]),
                 beta      = stats::coef(m)[2],
                 r2        = summary(m)$r.squared)
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

Read Table 5.1 through Equation 5.3. The model explains 4 series and fails on the rest. Above an \(R^2\) of one half sit CH_equity, EU_equity, UK_equity, US_equity. JP_equity reaches 0.321, 9 of the 16 fall below 0.10, and the extreme is JP_government at 0.000950, a regression that accounts for nothing whatsoever.

The median \(R^2\) across all 16 is 0.077. A single-factor model is a model of equities that happens to be applied to everything else.

Of the 16 alphas only one clears a t-statistic of two, that of US_government at +2.92% a year with t = 2.73. US_credit comes next, at +2.79% with t = 1.76, and both return later. A government bond carrying significant alpha against an equity market is not a mispricing. It is the model reporting that it has the wrong factors, exactly as Fama and French (1993) argued when adding a term structure and a default factor for precisely these assets.

5.2.2 Splitting the beta, and then testing the split

A single beta assumes Equation 5.2 is the same on the way up and on the way down. PerformanceAnalytics estimates the two halves of Equation 5.4 in one call.

Rm <- xts::xts(MKT, order.by = d)
rfx <- xts::xts(rf, order.by = d)

cond <- lapply(colnames(R), function(s) {
  tibble::tibble(
    series = s,
    beta   = PerformanceAnalytics::CAPM.beta(R[, s], Rm, rfx),
    bull   = PerformanceAnalytics::CAPM.beta.bull(R[, s], Rm, rfx),
    bear   = PerformanceAnalytics::CAPM.beta.bear(R[, s], Rm, rfx)
  )
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows() |>
  dplyr::mutate(gap = bear - bull)

cond |> dplyr::arrange(gap) |> head(4)
#> # A tibble: 4 × 5
#>   series           beta  bull    bear    gap
#>   <chr>           <dbl> <dbl>   <dbl>  <dbl>
#> 1 JP_government -0.0223 0.159 -0.120  -0.279
#> 2 UK_government  0.186  0.283  0.138  -0.145
#> 3 JP_credit      0.0953 0.227  0.105  -0.121
#> 4 CH_government  0.0642 0.163  0.0424 -0.121

JP_government has an unconditional beta of -0.0223, indistinguishable from zero and the sort of number that files a series under uncorrelated and forgets it. It is not uncorrelated. It rises with the market when the market rises, at +0.159, and rises against it when the market falls, at -0.120. The single number is the average of two opposite behaviors and destroys the only interesting property of the series.

Now the step that is usually skipped. An apparent asymmetry has to survive a test, because splitting a sample in two and comparing the halves is a machine for producing differences.

asym <- lapply(colnames(R), function(s) {
  y  <- as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf
  dn <- as.numeric(mkt_e < 0)
  m  <- stats::lm(y ~ mkt_e + I(mkt_e * dn))
  nw <- sandwich::NeweyWest(m, lag = 6, prewhite = FALSE)
  tibble::tibble(series = s,
                 gap = stats::coef(m)[3],
                 t   = stats::coef(m)[3] / sqrt(nw[3, 3]),
                 p   = 2 * stats::pnorm(-abs(stats::coef(m)[3] / sqrt(nw[3, 3]))))
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

asym |> dplyr::filter(p < 0.05) |> dplyr::arrange(p)
#> # A tibble: 2 × 4
#>   series           gap     t      p
#>   <chr>          <dbl> <dbl>  <dbl>
#> 1 JP_government -0.283 -2.37 0.0177
#> 2 US_government -0.121 -2.30 0.0213

This is Equation 5.4 with the standard errors of Equation 5.5, and 2 of 16 survive, both of them government bonds. The Japanese bond asymmetry is real, at t = -2.37. The credit asymmetry, which is the one an allocator would most like to believe in, is not: US_credit has a gap of +0.098 with t = 0.66 and p = 0.51.

There are 130 down months in this sample. Estimating a separate beta on them, for sixteen series, will produce differences whether or not any exist. Report the split as a diagnostic; do not build a hedge on it without the test.

5.2.3 What a factor is actually sorting on

Size is the second factor everyone reaches for, following Fama and French (1992) and Fama and French (1993), and here it is a trap worth walking into deliberately.

capW <- W
reg  <- sim_assets$region[match(colnames(Ra), sim_assets$asset_id)]

# Global sort: bottom half by capitalization minus top half.
smb_global <- vapply(seq_len(nrow(Ra)), function(i) {
  w <- capW[i, ]; ok <- w > 0; med <- median(w[ok])
  mean(zoo::coredata(Ra)[i, ok & w <= med]) - mean(zoo::coredata(Ra)[i, ok & w > med])
}, numeric(1))

# Region-neutral: rank within each region, then average the five spreads.
smb_neutral <- vapply(seq_len(nrow(Ra)), function(i) {
  w <- capW[i, ]
  mean(vapply(unique(reg), function(g) {
    j <- reg == g & w > 0; med <- median(w[j])
    mean(zoo::coredata(Ra)[i, j & w <= med]) - mean(zoo::coredata(Ra)[i, j & w > med])
  }, numeric(1)))
}, numeric(1))

Before using either, look at what the global sort placed in each leg.

legs <- vapply(seq_len(nrow(Ra)), function(i) {
  w <- capW[i, ]; ok <- w > 0; med <- median(w[ok])
  c(big_us   = sum(w[ok & w > med & reg[ok] == "US"]) / sum(w[ok & w > med]),
    small_us = sum(w[ok & w <= med & reg[ok] == "US"]) / sum(w[ok & w <= med]))
}, numeric(2))
round(rowMeans(legs), 3)
#>   big_us small_us 
#>    0.628    0.152

The big leg is 63% United States. The small leg is 15%. A global capitalization sort in a five-region universe does not sort on size. It sorts on region, and since region carries a currency, it sorts on currency too.

The consequence is measurable.

med_r2 <- function(f = NULL) {
  median(vapply(risky, function(s) {
    y <- as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf
    summary(if (is.null(f)) stats::lm(y ~ mkt_e) else stats::lm(y ~ mkt_e + f))$r.squared
  }, numeric(1)))
}
c(market_only  = med_r2(),
  plus_global  = med_r2(smb_global),
  plus_neutral = med_r2(smb_neutral))
#>  market_only  plus_global plus_neutral 
#>      0.07726      0.09156      0.07894

# The premium of each construction, with its Newey-West t-statistic.
nw_t <- function(x) {
  m <- stats::lm(x ~ 1)
  unname(stats::coef(m)[1] / sqrt(sandwich::NeweyWest(m, lag = 6, prewhite = FALSE)[1, 1]))
}
c(global_premium  = ann_ret(smb_global),  global_t  = nw_t(smb_global),
  neutral_premium = ann_ret(smb_neutral), neutral_t = nw_t(smb_neutral),
  correlation     = cor(smb_global, smb_neutral))
#>  global_premium        global_t neutral_premium       neutral_t     correlation 
#>         0.02395         1.99148         0.03393         3.24015         0.61953

Adding the global size factor lifts the median \(R^2\) from 0.077 to 0.092. Constructing the same factor region-neutrally lifts it only to 0.079, an improvement of 0.002. The explanatory power was not size. It was the regional composition of the legs.

The premium runs the other way. The region-neutral factor earns 3.4% a year with a t-statistic of 3.24, the global sort 2.4% with 1.99: the construction that explains less variance carries the more reliable premium. The lesson is not that size does not exist. It is that a factor is defined by its construction, and a sort nobody has inspected is a bet nobody has chosen. Griffin (2002) makes the empirical case that the size and value factors are country-specific rather than global, and Hou et al. (2011) reach a similar conclusion on a wider cross-section. A practitioner building a global factor without neutralizing the country dimension is, on this evidence, building a country factor.

5.3 How Much of This Survives

5.3.1 A beta is a time series

Equation 5.2 is written as though \(\beta_i\) were a constant. The conditional asset pricing literature starting with Ferson and Harvey (1991) and Jagannathan and Wang (1996) assumes it is not, and a rolling window shows directly how much it moves.

win <- 60L
roll_beta <- function(s) {
  y <- as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf
  zoo::rollapply(cbind(y, mkt_e), win,
                 function(z) stats::cov(z[, 1], z[, 2]) / stats::var(z[, 2]),
                 by.column = FALSE, align = "right")
}

travel <- lapply(risky, function(s) {
  b <- roll_beta(s)
  tibble::tibble(series = s,
                 full = stats::cov(as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf, mkt_e) / stats::var(mkt_e),
                 min = min(b), max = max(b), travel = diff(range(b)),
                 sign_flips = mean(sign(b) != sign(stats::cov(as.numeric(R[, s]) - rf, mkt_e))))
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows() |> dplyr::arrange(dplyr::desc(travel))

Two patterns emerge, and the second is the one to carry forward.

The betas that travel furthest are not the largest. UK_credit moves 0.96 across the sample, from -0.13 to +0.82, while US_equity, with a beta near one, travels only 0.33 and never changes sign. Blume (1971) documented the same regularity in individual stocks: extreme betas are the unreliable ones, and they revert.

The largest full-sample beta outside equity is 0.40 in absolute value, and every one of the 11 changes sign, the median in 31% of all windows. JP_government, whose beta is closest to zero, does so in 47% and US_credit in 31%; US_government, at 7%, is the one small beta that is reliably negative. The five equity series flip in none.

A small beta is not a stable beta. It is a beta whose sign is unknown. That is why Section 8.1 exists, and why a hedge ratio needs an error bar before it needs a decimal place.

5.3.2 Measuring the instability itself

If the moments move, the movement is itself a quantity. A turbulence index measures it as the Mahalanobis distance of a period’s returns from their recent mean, scaled by the covariance structure,

\[ d_t = \left(\boldsymbol{r}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}\right)' \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \left(\boldsymbol{r}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}\right), \tag{5.6}\]

so that an unusual pattern of returns counts as turbulent even when no single series is extreme. Chow et al. (1999) introduced the construction for portfolio work and Kritzman and Li (2010) developed it as a risk-management signal. The implementation used here is Stöckl (2020), which is not on CRAN; see Appendix D.

library(uncertaintymeasures)

R_risky <- R[, risky]
turb <- OSturbulence(R_risky, rolling = TRUE, roll.obs = 60)

OSturbulence() estimates \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) from data strictly before each observation, so the index never sees the month it is scoring. An in-sample variant exists, and the gap between them is a clean picture of what look-ahead buys.

tb  <- as.numeric(turb$turb)
top <- order(tb, decreasing = TRUE, na.last = NA)[1:5]

# Which of the named episodes, if any, each month belongs to.
episode_of <- rep("none", length(d))
for (i in seq_len(nrow(sim_episodes))) {
  episode_of[in_episode(d, sim_episodes$episode[i])] <- sim_episodes$label[i]
}

data.frame(month = fmt_model(d[top]), turbulence = round(tb[top], 2),
           episode = episode_of[top])
#> # A tibble: 5 × 3
#>   month                     turbulence episode        
#>   <chr>                          <dbl> <chr>          
#> 1 October of model year 17        1.93 credit crisis  
#> 2 November of model year 17       0.99 credit crisis  
#> 3 December of model year 17       0.83 credit crisis  
#> 4 January of model year 18        0.82 credit crisis  
#> 5 March of model year 29          0.81 liquidity shock

The highest reading in the sample is October of model year 17, in the credit crisis, at 1.93, 1.95 times the next one, and 4 of the 5 highest readings fall in that episode. The peak is the same month Section 4.3 identified by counting exceedances, arrived at independently, expressed as one number rather than a count, and available on the day rather than in hindsight.

The component that makes Equation 5.6 more than a volatility measure is the ratio of the full distance to the one computed from variances alone.

ct <- as.numeric(turb$cturb)
c(median     = median(ct, na.rm = TRUE),
  peak_month = ct[peak],
  max        = max(ct, na.rm = TRUE))
#>     median peak_month        max 
#>      1.569      1.631      9.580

This isolates how much of a turbulent month is correlations breaking rather than volatilities rising, which is the distinction Forbes and Rigobon (2002) showed is easy to confuse. In the peak month the ratio is 1.63 against a median of 1.57: the most turbulent month of the sample was a month of extreme moves in an ordinary correlation structure. Section 4.3 found average pairwise correlation higher in the credit crisis than in calm months, but only by choosing the windows by hand and knowing the answer first. This is the same question asked continuously and without hindsight, and it is what a regime-aware allocation in Section 19.1 would have to react to.

Key Takeaways

  • The \(R^2\) of Equation 5.1 is not decoration. By Equation 5.3 it is the share of an asset’s risk the model claims to describe, and the median across these sixteen series is 0.08. A one-factor model is an equity model applied to everything else.
  • A market proxy has a composition, and this one is on average 57% United States and 70% at the end of the sample. Every beta computed against it is largely a United States beta (Roll 1977).
  • Conditional betas are worth computing and are rarely worth believing. Of sixteen asymmetries estimated with Equation 5.4, 2 survive Newey-West inference, and the one an allocator would most want is not among them.
  • A factor is defined by its construction. A global size sort in a multi-region universe puts 63% United States in one leg and 15% in the other, so it is a region bet wearing a size label (Griffin 2002; Hou et al. 2011).
  • Betas move. Equity betas do so without changing sign; outside equity the median beta has the wrong sign in 31% of all sixty-month windows and the one closest to zero in 47% (Blume 1971).
  • The instability is itself measurable without hindsight. The Mahalanobis turbulence of Equation 5.6 picks out October of model year 17, in the credit crisis, from a trailing window, and its correlation component separates dependence breaking down from volatility merely rising (Chow et al. 1999; Kritzman and Li 2010).

Exercises

  1. Rebuild the market proxy with equal rather than capitalization weights. Which betas move most, and does the ranking in Table 5.2 survive? Explain what that says about the phrase “the market”.
  2. The asymmetry test uses a single interaction term. Replace it with separate regressions on the up and down months, and confirm that the point estimates agree while the standard errors do not. Which is the honest one, and why?
  3. Take the two series with the highest sign_flips in Table 5.2 and plot their rolling betas against the turbulence index. Does the beta move because the market is turbulent, or independently of it?
  4. OSturbulence() has an s.k argument controlling the length of the short window whose mean is compared with the long-run mean. Vary it from 1 to 12 and describe what the index measures at each end.
  5. Harvey et al. (2016) argue that a t-statistic of two is too low a bar once many hypotheses have been tested. Sixteen asymmetries were tested here. Apply a Bonferroni correction and a Benjamini-Hochberg correction to the p-values in asym, and report how many survive each.
