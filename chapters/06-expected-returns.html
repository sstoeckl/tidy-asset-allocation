6  Expected Returns

Draft|Code: script · exercises · solutions · all chapters|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

Every optimizer in this book consumes a vector of expected returns, and nothing in the data pins that vector down as well as the covariance matrix beside it. Merton (1980) established why: the standard error of a mean estimated over a span of \(\tau\) years is \(\sigma/\sqrt{\tau}\) whatever the sampling frequency, so only a longer history helps, and thirty years of it leaves an annual risk premium known to within several percentage points. That is a property of the object rather than of any estimator, and it is the reason expected returns are the hard input of Section 9.1 while the covariance matrix of Section 7.1 is merely a large one.

This chapter is about strategic expected returns in the sense of Section 3.1: the unconditional vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) a long-run policy is set on, with no information about where in the cycle we stand. Conditional forecasts are the tactical work of Section 16.1, and turning an opinion into a return is the subject of Section 17.1. We first measure how imprecisely each of the sixteen means is known and what that does to a return target, then set out the two alternatives to the sample mean, shrinkage toward a common value and reverse optimization from market weights, then implement both and show how PortfolioAnalytics takes a mean vector it did not compute, and finally feed the three vectors to the same tangency problem to see what each buys and how much each moves when the sample changes.

The data are the sixteen risky class series of Section 2.2, in excess of the Treasury bill for the reasons given in Section 3.1.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return
Re <- R - rf
stopifnot(ncol(Re) == 16L, !anyNA(Re))
N <- ncol(Re); T <- nrow(Re); years <- T / 12

6.1 How Precisely a Mean Can Be Known

Write \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) for the vector of sample means of the excess returns and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) for their sample covariance, as in Section 9.1. Each element of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) is an average of \(T\) monthly observations, and if those observations are independent with volatility \(\sigma\) per year, the standard error of the annualized mean is

\[ \operatorname{se}(\hat{\mu}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\tau}}, \tag{6.1}\]

where \(\tau\) is the calendar span in years. Equation 4.3 derived this alongside the standard error of the variance, and the contrast between the two is the point of Merton (1980): the sampling interval does not appear in Equation 6.1, so nothing short of more calendar time improves it. A 95 percent interval for the mean is

\[ \hat{\mu} \pm 1.96\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\tau}}, \tag{6.2}\]

and setting its half-width equal to the mean itself gives the span of history needed before the interval excludes zero:

\[ \tau^{*} = \left(\frac{1.96\,\sigma}{\mu}\right)^{2}. \tag{6.3}\]

Both quantities are computable for every series, and the table they produce is the right way to begin a chapter on expected returns.

mu_s <- colMeans(Re)                 # monthly sample means, the mu of chapter 9
S    <- stats::cov(Re)               # the sample covariance of chapter 7

precision <- tibble::tibble(
  asset = colnames(Re),
  mean  = mu_s * 12,
  vol   = apply(Re, 2, stats::sd) * sqrt(12)
) |>
  dplyr::mutate(se    = vol / sqrt(years),                 # @eq-06-se
                t     = mean / se,
                lower = mean - 1.96 * se,                  # @eq-06-interval
                upper = mean + 1.96 * se,
                years_needed = (1.96 * vol / mean)^2) |>   # @eq-06-years
  dplyr::arrange(dplyr::desc(t))

n_straddle <- sum(precision$lower < 0)
c(series = nrow(precision), interval_includes_zero = n_straddle,
  median_se = median(precision$se), median_years_needed = median(precision$years_needed))
#>                 series interval_includes_zero              median_se 
#>               16.00000               10.00000                0.02184 
#>    median_years_needed 
#>               45.32200

Take the series whose mean is furthest from zero relative to its standard error, US_equity. Its annualized volatility is 0.156 and the sample spans 31 model years, so by Equation 6.1

\[ \operatorname{se}(\hat{\mu}) = \frac{0.156}{\sqrt{31}} = 0.0280, \]

a standard error of 2.8 percentage points on a mean of 11.3%, and an interval from 5.8% to 16.8%. That is the best case. For 10 of the 16 series the interval of Equation 6.2 includes zero: the data do not reject the hypothesis that the asset carries no risk premium at all. The median series would need 45 years of history before it did, and JP_government, with a mean of 0.6% against a standard error of 1.9 points, would need 1263. Fama and French (2002) make the same point on a century and a quarter of United States data: the realized equity premium is estimated with a standard error that leaves its level open to debate by several percentage points, which is why they estimate it from dividend and earnings growth instead. Section 4.2 measured the practical counterpart on this market, a rolling sixty-month mean that wanders by more than its own level, and Figure 4.2 shows it. Nothing in this chapter improves on Equation 6.1; the estimators below manage the imprecision rather than remove it.

NoteSampling more finely helps the variance and not the mean

The obvious response to an imprecise mean is more observations, and the weekly panel sim_class_returns_weekly holds the same 31 model years at more than four times the resolution. Equation 4.3 says what to expect: the standard error of the variance falls with \(\sqrt{T}\), and the standard error of the mean does not move.

6.1.1 A target set on a sample mean

Equation 9.2 fixes a return target \(\bar{\mu}\) and asks for the least variance that attains it, and Section 9.2 solves that program with \(\bar{\mu}\) set at the upper quartile of the sample means. The constraint \(\boldsymbol{w}'\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = \bar{\mu}\) is then satisfied to the solver’s tolerance, on an input known to the precision of Table 6.1.

target_m <- as.numeric(quantile(mu_s, 0.75))      # the target chapter 9 imposes
se_med   <- median(precision$se)
c(target_annual = target_m * 12, median_se = se_med,
  target_lower = target_m * 12 - 1.96 * se_med,
  target_upper = target_m * 12 + 1.96 * se_med)
#> target_annual     median_se  target_lower  target_upper 
#>       0.06650       0.02184       0.02370       0.10930

The target is 6.65% a year, held to eight decimal places. The inputs that define it carry a median standard error of 2.2 percentage points, so a mean of that size is itself known only to somewhere between 2.4% and 10.9%. A constraint set on a sample mean is a constraint set on noise, and the optimizer honors it exactly.

The literature on what follows is old and consistent, and one sentence each suffices here because Section 9.1 has already used it. Jobson and Korkie (1980) showed that the sampling distribution of the estimated tangency weights is so wide that the portfolio’s expected Sharpe ratio falls far short of the one the estimate promises. Best and Grauer (1991) traced the mechanism to Equation 9.5: the weights are \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\) applied to \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\), so an error in a mean is magnified by the condition number of the covariance matrix on its way into the portfolio. Chopra and Ziemba (1993) priced the two inputs against each other and found errors in means roughly an order of magnitude more costly than errors in variances. Michaud (1989) named the consequence, an optimizer that systematically overweights whatever the sample flattered. Kan and Zhou (2007) derived the expected out-of-sample loss of the plug-in rule, showed that the term due to \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) dominates, and proposed holding the sample tangency portfolio alongside the minimum-variance portfolio of Equation 9.3, which is exactly what the shrinkage estimator below turns out to do. DeMiguel et al. (2009) supplied the empirical verdict with fourteen rules on seven datasets: none that estimates the mean was consistently better than equal weighting out of sample. 1 measures the cost on this market and Section 26.1 derives it.

6.1.2 Shrinkage of the mean

The response from statistics predates all of that. Stein (1956) showed that for three or more means estimated jointly, the vector of sample means is inadmissible under quadratic loss: an estimator that pulls every element toward a common value has lower expected error whatever the true means are. James and Stein (1961) gave the estimator its form, a shrinkage factor that depends on how far the sample means sit from their target, and Efron and Morris (1975) the intuition, that a set of estimates spread by sampling error is always spread too far. Jorion (1985) brought the argument to portfolios and Jorion (1986) gave it the form that is still used. The target is the expected return of the minimum-variance portfolio, the one number about \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) that \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) alone pins down, and the estimator is

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\text{BS}} = (1-v)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} + v\,\mu_0\,\boldsymbol{\iota}, \qquad \mu_0 = \frac{\boldsymbol{\iota}'\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}} {\boldsymbol{\iota}'\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}}, \qquad v = \frac{N+2} {(N+2) + T\,(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-\mu_0\boldsymbol{\iota})' \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1} (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-\mu_0\boldsymbol{\iota})}. \tag{6.4}\]

The intensity \(v\) is set by the data rather than chosen. The quadratic form in its denominator is \(T\) times the squared distance of the sample means from their target, measured in the metric of the covariance matrix; when the means are far apart relative to their sampling error, \(v\) is small and the sample is trusted, and when they are indistinguishable, \(v\) approaches one and every asset is assigned the same expected return. As \(T\) grows, \(v\) falls to zero, so the estimator is consistent. This is the same construction as Equation 7.6 for the covariance matrix, where Ledoit and Wolf (2004) derive the analogous intensity, and it has the same character: a biased estimator with lower total error than the unbiased one.

What shrinkage does to the portfolio is visible from Equation 9.5. Because the map from means to unconstrained tangency weights is linear,

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\text{BS}} = (1-v)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} + v\,\mu_0\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}, \tag{6.5}\]

a combination of the sample tangency direction and the minimum-variance direction of Equation 9.3. Shrinking the means toward \(\mu_0\) therefore moves the tangency portfolio toward the minimum-variance portfolio, which is the three-fund rule of Kan and Zhou (2007) arrived at from the estimator’s side. Other targets are possible and each carries an argument: Pástor (2000) and MacKinlay and Pástor (2000) shrink toward the means a factor model such as that of Section 5.2 implies, Tu and Zhou (2011) combine the sample rule with equal weighting, and Garlappi et al. (2007) reach a shrinkage form from ambiguity aversion rather than from loss. The target used here is the one that needs no model beyond \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\).

6.1.3 Equilibrium returns by reverse optimization

The third route asks a different question. Rather than estimating \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) from the history of returns, it asks what \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) would have to be for a portfolio someone actually holds to be optimal. Sharpe (1974) posed the problem in that form, and Black and Litterman (1992) chose the portfolio: the market, weighted by capitalization, which is the portfolio the equilibrium of Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965) says every investor holds. An investor who maximizes \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} - \tfrac{\delta}{2}\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\) has the first-order condition \(\boldsymbol{\mu} = \delta\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\), derived in Section B.5, and if the aggregate investor holds the market, the expected returns consistent with that are

\[ \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}} = \delta\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mkt}}. \tag{6.6}\]

The scalar \(\delta\) is the market’s risk aversion, and premultiplying Equation 6.6 by \(\boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mkt}}'\) shows how to read it from the market’s own history:

\[ \delta = \frac{\mu_{\text{mkt}}}{\sigma^{2}_{\text{mkt}}} = \frac{\text{SR}_{\text{mkt}}}{\sigma_{\text{mkt}}}, \tag{6.7}\]

the market’s expected excess return divided by its variance, which is the same number whether computed from monthly or from annual moments. He and Litterman (1999) give the intuition for what the resulting vector does inside an optimizer, Satchell and Scowcroft (2000) the Bayesian derivation, and Idzorek (2007) a step-by-step account of the full model with views attached; the views are Section 17.1’s subject, and only the prior is used here.

Three properties of Equation 6.6 matter here. It is not a forecast: it is the vector for which the market portfolio is the tangency portfolio of Equation 9.5, by construction, so an optimizer handed \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) returns \(\boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mkt}}\), a portfolio that is held, diversified and consistent with the covariance matrix. \(\delta\) scales every element alike, and the tangency portfolio is invariant to scale, so \(\delta\) sets how much of the portfolio is risky rather than what the risky part holds; it matters for position size and, in Section 17.1, for how heavily a view is weighed against the prior. And the package’s risk_aversion argument is \(\gamma/2\) in the notation of Section B.5, so reproducing the market portfolio requires risk_aversion set to \(\delta/2\), verified numerically below.

The weakness of the method is the weight vector. Roll (1977) is the reminder that the market portfolio is not observable, and on this market the problem is concrete: sim_marketcap_weekly carries capitalizations for the 123 equity instruments and for nothing else. No capitalization exists for the government or credit series or for the commodity index, so a full class-level equilibrium cannot be read from the data as shipped. We do the two things that can be done: a clean demonstration on the five equity classes, whose weights are their capitalizations, and a class-level version on a stated proxy for the rest, kept in a box and labelled as the assumption it is.

6.2 Three Estimators in Code

6.2.1 Bayes-Stein in a few lines

Equation 6.4 is a function of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\), \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\), \(N\) and \(T\), and nothing else. Jorion (1986) scales the sample covariance by \((T-1)/(T-N-2)\) before inverting it, which makes the inverse unbiased; on 372 observations and 16 assets the factor is 1.048 and changes \(v\) at the second decimal.

bayes_stein <- function(R) {
  X <- zoo::coredata(R); T <- nrow(X); N <- ncol(X)
  mu   <- colMeans(X)
  S    <- stats::cov(X) * (T - 1) / (T - N - 2)   # Jorion's unbiased scaling
  Sinv <- solve(S); iota <- rep(1, N)
  mu0  <- as.numeric(t(iota) %*% Sinv %*% mu) / as.numeric(t(iota) %*% Sinv %*% iota)
  d    <- mu - mu0
  v    <- (N + 2) / ((N + 2) + T * as.numeric(t(d) %*% Sinv %*% d))
  list(mu = setNames((1 - v) * mu + v * mu0, colnames(X)), mu0 = mu0, v = v)
}

bs <- bayes_stein(Re)
stopifnot(bs$v > 0.5, bs$v < 0.6)    # "more than half" and "roughly halved" below
c(v = bs$v, mu0_annual = bs$mu0 * 12)
#>          v mu0_annual 
#>    0.52768    0.03234

The intensity is \(v = 0.528\) and the target is \(\mu_0 = 3.23%\) a year, the expected return of the minimum-variance portfolio. For US_equity the estimator of Equation 6.4 reads

\[ \hat{\mu}_{\text{BS}} = (1 - 0.528) \times 0.1130 + 0.528 \times 0.0323 = 0.0704, \]

so more than half of the distance between the sample mean and the common value is removed. The same happens to every series, in both directions.

mu_long <- tibble::tibble(
  asset = rep(colnames(Re), 2),
  kind  = rep(c("sample mean", "Bayes-Stein"), each = N),
  value = c(mu_s, bs$mu) * 12
) |>
  dplyr::mutate(asset = factor(asset, levels = names(sort(mu_s))))
stopifnot(nrow(mu_long) == 2 * N, !anyNA(mu_long$value))

# The ordering is untouched: the same ranks, asset by asset.
stopifnot(identical(names(mu_s), names(bs$mu)), identical(rank(mu_s), rank(bs$mu)))
c(spread_sample = diff(range(mu_s)) * 12, spread_shrunk = diff(range(bs$mu)) * 12,
  rank_correlation = stats::cor(mu_s, bs$mu, method = "spearman"))
#>    spread_sample    spread_shrunk rank_correlation 
#>          0.12818          0.06054          1.00000

The spread between the highest and the lowest expected return falls from 12.8 to 6.1 percentage points, and the rank correlation between the two vectors is 1.00, because Equation 6.4 is a linear map with a positive slope. Shrinkage changes how different the assets look, not which looks better.

6.2.2 Equilibrium returns for the five equity classes

The clean case first. Each regional equity class is the capitalization-weighted sum of its instruments, so its weight in the world equity market is the sum of their capitalizations. As in Section 5.2 the weight used in month \(t\) is the last one observed on or before the end of month \(t-1\), a weight that could have been held.

eq_ids     <- sim_assets$asset_id[sim_assets$asset_class == "equity"]
region_of  <- setNames(sim_assets$region, sim_assets$asset_id)
eq_classes <- grep("_equity$", colnames(Re), value = TRUE)

# Capitalization of each region's listed equities, week by week.
cap_region <- sim_marketcap_weekly |>
  dplyr::filter(asset %in% eq_ids) |>
  dplyr::mutate(class = paste0(region_of[asset], "_equity")) |>
  dplyr::group_by(date, class) |>
  dplyr::summarise(cap = sum(market_cap), .groups = "drop") |>
  tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = class, values_from = cap) |>
  dplyr::arrange(date)
stopifnot(setequal(setdiff(names(cap_region), "date"), eq_classes), !anyNA(cap_region))
cap_mat <- as.matrix(cap_region[, eq_classes])      # columns in class order, by name

d <- zoo::index(Re)
W_eq <- t(vapply(seq_along(d), function(i) {
  cutoff <- if (i == 1) d[1] - 31 else d[i - 1]     # last week of the previous month
  j <- max(which(cap_region$date <= cutoff))
  cap_mat[j, ] / sum(cap_mat[j, ])
}, numeric(length(eq_classes))))
colnames(W_eq) <- eq_classes
stopifnot(all(abs(rowSums(W_eq) - 1) < 1e-10), identical(colnames(W_eq), eq_classes))

round(rbind(first = W_eq[1, ], average = colMeans(W_eq), last = W_eq[nrow(W_eq), ]), 3)
#>         CH_equity EU_equity JP_equity UK_equity US_equity
#> first       0.034     0.161     0.185     0.123     0.497
#> average     0.075     0.175     0.095     0.085     0.570
#> last        0.059     0.142     0.056     0.044     0.700

The composition drifts, from 50% United States at the start to 70% at the end, which Section 5.2 found for the same proxy. An unconditional \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) is matched to an unconditional weight vector, so we use the average composition over the sample; the last month’s weights are an exercise. The market’s own excess return calibrates \(\delta\) through Equation 6.7.

mkt_e  <- rowSums(W_eq * zoo::coredata(Re[, eq_classes]))   # the market's excess return
delta  <- mean(mkt_e) / stats::var(mkt_e)                    # @eq-06-delta
w_mkt5 <- colMeans(W_eq)
S5     <- S[eq_classes, eq_classes]
mu_eq5 <- setNames(delta * as.numeric(S5 %*% w_mkt5), eq_classes)   # @eq-06-equilibrium
stopifnot(abs(sum(w_mkt5 * mu_eq5) - delta * as.numeric(t(w_mkt5) %*% S5 %*% w_mkt5)) < 1e-12)

c(market_return = mean(mkt_e) * 12, market_vol = stats::sd(mkt_e) * sqrt(12),
  market_sharpe = mean(mkt_e) / stats::sd(mkt_e) * sqrt(12), delta = delta)
#> market_return    market_vol market_sharpe         delta 
#>        0.1058        0.1486        0.7117        4.7886

With an annualized excess return of 0.1058 and a variance of 0.0221, Equation 6.7 gives

\[ \delta = \frac{0.1058}{0.0221} = 4.79, \]

and Equation 6.6 then produces one expected return per class.

prem_eq <- sum(w_mkt5 * mu_eq5)               # the market's own equilibrium premium
beta5   <- mu_eq5 / prem_eq                     # (Sigma w) / (w' Sigma w), asset by asset

cmp5 <- tibble::tibble(
  asset       = eq_classes,
  weight      = w_mkt5,
  beta        = beta5,
  sample      = mu_s[eq_classes] * 12,
  se          = precision$se[match(eq_classes, precision$asset)],
  equilibrium = mu_eq5 * 12
) |>
  dplyr::mutate(gap_in_se = (equilibrium - sample) / se)
stopifnot(identical(cmp5$asset, eq_classes), !anyNA(cmp5),
          abs(sum(w_mkt5 * beta5) - 1) < 1e-12)
# The two revisions the text reads off the table: JP_equity up, US_equity down.
stopifnot(cmp5$gap_in_se[cmp5$asset == "JP_equity"] > 0,
          cmp5$gap_in_se[cmp5$asset == "US_equity"] < 0)

Dividing Equation 6.6 by the market’s own equilibrium premium \(\boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mkt}}'\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}}\) shows what the vector is: each element is the asset’s beta on the market times that premium,

\[ \mu_{\text{eq},i} = \frac{(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mkt}})_i} {\boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mkt}}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mkt}}} \times \boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mkt}}'\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}} = \beta_i \times 10.28%, \]

which is the CAPM of Equation 5.1 read as a prior rather than tested as a model. Every equilibrium return sits within 0.6 standard errors of the corresponding sample mean, so the two vectors are not in conflict; they answer different questions. The sample mean says what each market paid. The equilibrium says what each would have to pay for investors to hold it in the proportion they do, and it pays a beta of 0.75 for JP_equity at the market premium where the sample paid less, and revises US_equity, with a beta of 1.00, down from a sample mean the market premium does not support.

NoteWorked: a class-level equilibrium on a stated proxy

The application below needs \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}}\) for all sixteen classes, and eleven of them have no capitalization. What follows is a placeholder, and it should be read as one. Assumption: world investable wealth is split 40 percent equity, 40 percent government bonds, 15 percent credit and 5 percent commodities, and within the equity, government and credit blocks each region’s share is its share of equity capitalization. Nothing in the data supports the block split; it is a round-number stand-in for a figure that a real allocation would take from a global market-capitalization survey.

# ASSUMPTION, not data: the split of world wealth across the four blocks.
block <- c(equity = 0.40, government = 0.40, credit = 0.15, commodity = 0.05)
stopifnot(abs(sum(block) - 1) < 1e-12)

W16 <- matrix(0, nrow(Re), N, dimnames = list(NULL, colnames(Re)))

Four things about this function are measured rather than documented, and a reader who expects Black and Litterman (1992) will walk into each of them.

First, there is no market-weight argument and no \(\delta\): Mu defaults to colMeans(R), so the prior is the sample mean unless Equation 6.6 is computed and passed in, as above. Second, there is no \(\tau\): the prior’s uncertainty is \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) itself and the view uncertainty is fixed at \(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}'\). Third, Views = NULL is not the absence of a view. The default view is the standard deviation of each view portfolio, a bullish opinion on everything, and with P = diag(N) it lifts every mean by 20 percentage points a year on average; set.portfolio.moments(method = "black_litterman") inherits this with one default view on the plain sum of the assets’ returns and shifts the means by between 1 and 21 points. Fourth, BLSigma is the posterior covariance of the mean, not of returns: with the prior alone it is exactly \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}/2\), and set.portfolio.moments() hands it to the optimizer as sigma, which then optimizes on half the risk. Supply Mu yourself, set Views to P %*% Mu when you want the prior alone, and never let BLSigma reach a risk objective.

One more trap, this time in how arguments travel. set.portfolio.moments() recovers Mu, P and Views through match.call(), which returns the expression a caller typed rather than its value.

Called directly with a variable, the function sees the symbol mu_eq, a vector of length one, and stops. Called through optimize.portfolio(), which assembles the call with do.call(), the values arrive intact and the mean is honored; the covariance arrives halved, which is the fourth point of the callout. The custom momentFUN of the previous chunk has neither problem, and it is what the rest of this chapter uses.

Finally, the \(\gamma/2\) convention. The equilibrium vector was built so that the market portfolio maximizes \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} - \tfrac{\delta}{2}\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\), and the package’s quadratic utility objective puts risk_aversion where \(\delta/2\) stands. Feeding \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}}\) back in is therefore a test of both the vector and the convention.

NoteWorked: the long-only tangency portfolio in one quadratic program

A Sharpe ratio is invariant to the scale of the weight vector, and so is a sign constraint. The long-only tangency portfolio can therefore be found by minimizing variance at a unit expected return with no budget constraint, and rescaling the answer to sum to one; this is one quadratic program rather than a search over a frontier, and it makes the rolling exercise below cheap.

tangency_lo <- function(mu, S) {
  if (max(mu) <= 0) return(setNames(rep(NA_real_, length(mu)), names(mu)))  # no long-only tangency
  N <- length(mu)
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S, dvec = rep(0, N),
                            Amat = cbind(mu, diag(N)), bvec = c(1, rep(0, N)), meq = 1)
  w <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  setNames(w / sum(w), names(mu))
}

Read Table 6.3 across. The sample-mean portfolio holds 4 series with 2.0 effective bets and 68% in US_government, and promises a Sharpe ratio of 0.99, the highest of the three by construction, since it is scored on the vector it maximized. The Bayes-Stein portfolio is more concentrated still, with 76% in the same series. That is Equation 6.5 at work: shrinking the means toward \(\mu_0\) mixes the tangency direction with the minimum-variance direction, and the minimum-variance portfolio of Section 9.2 is 82% US_government. Shrinkage did not make the portfolio more diversified. It made it lean on the one input that is estimated well.

The equilibrium portfolio is different in kind. It holds all 16 series with 7.6 effective bets, because it is the market, and its in-sample Sharpe ratio of 0.82 gives up 18% of the sample tangency’s 0.99. That is the wrong yardstick, because the number the sample tangency is scored on here is the one it was fitted to. Table 9.5 reads the same rule out of sample from the register of Section 25.3: on a sixty-month rolling window rebalanced monthly it earns a Sharpe ratio of 0.55, 55% of the fitted figure and the lowest of the three portfolios compared there. What matters for a strategic allocation is how much each answer moves when the sample does.

6.3.2 Which vector moves least

We re-estimate each of the three vectors on every rolling sixty-month window, the window Section 25.3 uses, and solve the same tangency problem on each. Two things are measured: how far the mean vector itself moves from one window to the next, and the turnover of the portfolio it implies. The equilibrium’s inputs re-estimated each window are \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) and the average market weights over the window; \(\delta\) is held at its full-sample value, because it is a preference parameter rather than a forecast and, by the scale invariance noted above, it does not affect the tangency weights.

The tangency portfolios move accordingly: the sample-mean answer turns over 8.2% of the portfolio in an average month and 23% in the worst twentieth, Bayes-Stein about 23 percent less, and the equilibrium answer 0.1%, which is the rate at which capitalizations drift. The last row is no trick: the tangency portfolio of Equation 6.6 is the market portfolio in every window, whatever that window’s covariance matrix says, so its turnover is the market’s. The two estimated vectors move the portfolio 68 and 52 times as much as the one that was not estimated, and Section 23.3 puts a price on the difference.

6.3.3 What the book carries forward

The sample mean is the input every closed form in Section 9.1 assumes and the one Table 6.1 shows is not known: 10 of 16 intervals include zero, and the portfolio it implies is rebuilt every month. Bayes-Stein keeps the ordering, halves the spread and cuts the turnover, and by Equation 6.5 does so by leaning on the minimum-variance portfolio, the part of the problem \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) pins down. The equilibrium vector returns a portfolio that is held, diversified and consistent with the covariance matrix, and is only as good as the weights it is read from, which on this market exist for five classes and are assumed for eleven.

The book therefore carries two of the three. Where market weights exist, the equilibrium return of Equation 6.6 is the anchor: it is the prior that Section 17.1 blends views into and the strategic policy its tactical portfolios deviate from. Where they do not, the Bayes-Stein estimator of Equation 6.4 is the default, because it asks nothing of the data beyond \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) and moves the portfolio in the direction Jagannathan and Ma (2003) and Frost and Savarino (1988) showed a constraint moves it anyway. The sample mean is carried as the benchmark: it is the input whose cost 1 measures and the forecast without a signal that the predictors of Section 16.1 have to improve on.

Key Takeaways

  • By Equation 6.1 the precision of a mean depends on the calendar span alone, and on 31 model years of data 10 of the 16 intervals of Equation 6.2 include zero. Sampling weekly instead of monthly leaves that unchanged and halves the standard error of the variance (Merton 1980).
  • A return target set on a sample mean is honored to machine precision on an input known to a few percentage points, and the optimizer amplifies the difference (Best and Grauer 1991; Michaud 1989). The cost is measured in
  • Bayes-Stein shrinks every mean toward the expected return of the minimum-variance portfolio with an intensity the data set at \(v = 0.53\) here, preserves the ordering, and by Equation 6.5 moves the tangency portfolio toward the minimum-variance portfolio (Jorion 1986; Kan and Zhou 2007).
  • Reverse optimization returns the vector for which the market portfolio is the tangency portfolio. \(\delta\) scales it without changing the weights, and the package’s risk_aversion is \(\delta/2\), verified by feeding the vector back in (Sharpe 1974; Black and Litterman 1992).
  • black.litterman() is the blending formula only: no market weights, no \(\tau\), a default view that is bullish on everything, and a posterior covariance that halves the variances. Supply Mu and Views yourself and keep BLSigma away from a risk objective.
  • On rolling windows the sample tangency turns over about 8% of the portfolio a month, Bayes-Stein 6%, and the equilibrium 0.1%. The book carries the equilibrium as its anchor where market weights exist and Bayes-Stein where they do not.
