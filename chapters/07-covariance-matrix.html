7  The Covariance Matrix

Suppose we have an equity sleeve to fill. There are one hundred and twenty-three names in the eligible universe, thirty-one model years of monthly returns are available, and we want the combination of them with the lowest risk. That is the most modest request anyone makes of a portfolio optimizer: no view, no forecast, no tilt, just the low-volatility end of the frontier of Section 9.1.

We will not get it. Not because the data are poor and not because the optimizer is badly written, but because the object it requires cannot be estimated from the data available. Michaud (1989) named the consequence and Chopra and Ziemba (1993) priced it, but the failure here is not one of degree: on a realistic universe the sample covariance matrix is singular, and a singular matrix does not give a noisy answer, it gives a meaningless one.

We first set out what the optimizer is asking for and why the request cannot be met, then work through the four available responses in order of what they cost, then ask which of them actually pays out of sample. The answer to that last question is less flattering than the literature on shrinkage usually suggests.

cls <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)

ins <- as_xts_pm(sim_asset_returns_monthly, date, asset, return)
eq  <- ins[, colnames(ins) %in% sim_assets$asset_id[sim_assets$asset_class == "equity"]]

c(classes = ncol(cls), equities = ncol(eq), months = nrow(cls))
#>  classes equities   months 
#>       16      123      372

7.1 What the Optimizer Requires

A minimum-variance portfolio is the weight vector minimizing \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\) subject to the constraints, and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is the entire input. Nothing else about the assets enters: not their names, not their sectors, not their expected returns. Whatever the matrix says about risk is risk as far as the optimizer is concerned.

Being symmetric, it holds \(N(N+1)/2\) distinct numbers, estimated from \(T\) observations by

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T} (\boldsymbol{r}_t - \bar{\boldsymbol{r}})(\boldsymbol{r}_t - \bar{\boldsymbol{r}})' . \tag{7.1}\]

Written this way the difficulty is immediate. Equation 7.1 is a sum of \(T\) outer products, each of rank one, so

\[ \operatorname{rank}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}) \leq \min(N,\, T-1). \tag{7.2}\]

Whenever \(N > T-1\) the estimate is singular, whatever the data look like. This is arithmetic, not a property of markets: it would hold if the returns were coin flips.

The sixteen asset classes of Section 4.2 need 136. The 123 equities need 7,626, from 372 months of history. That asks the data to pin down more parameters than it has observations, and at some point the answer stops being an estimate.

7.1.1 What singularity claims about portfolios

Equation 7.2 is a statement about a matrix. Its consequence is a statement about money. If \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) is singular, there exists a weight vector \(\boldsymbol{w} \neq \boldsymbol{0}\) with

\[ \boldsymbol{w}'\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{w} = 0, \tag{7.3}\]

a portfolio the estimate says cannot lose money, and there are as many independent such portfolios as the matrix is short of full rank. An optimizer told to minimize variance will find them, because that is exactly its job, and will put everything there.

They are not real. They are long-short cancellations that happened to net out over the particular months used for estimation, and they will not net out again. This is the mechanism behind the familiar complaint that mean-variance optimization produces absurd weights. Michaud (1989) called the optimizer an error maximizer for this reason, and the label is exact rather than rhetorical: among all portfolios consistent with the constraints, the procedure selects the one the estimate is most optimistic about, which is the one the estimate is most wrong about.

Even short of singularity, the inverse is what the optimizer uses, and the quantity governing how much an error in \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) is magnified on its way into the weights is the condition number

\[ \kappa(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}) = \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}}, \tag{7.4}\]

the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue. Laloux et al. (1999) showed that most of the small eigenvalues of a financial correlation matrix are indistinguishable from those of a random matrix of the same dimensions, a comparison made precise by the limiting spectrum of Marčenko and Pastur (1967). The directions the optimizer most wants to exploit are the ones carrying the least information.

Chopra and Ziemba (1993) measured how the cost divides. Errors in expected returns hurt an order of magnitude more than errors in variances, which in turn hurt more than errors in covariances, which is why Section 6.1 is the harder chapter. It is also why this one is unavoidable: a singular matrix is not an error of degree.

7.1.2 Diagnosing it before the solver does

Two numbers, neither of which requires inverting anything.

covariance_health <- function(X) {
  S  <- stats::cov(zoo::coredata(X))
  ev <- eigen(S, symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
  c(assets = ncol(X), observations = nrow(X), rank = qr(S)$rank,
    riskless_directions = sum(ev < 1e-12),
    condition = if (min(ev) <= 0) Inf else max(ev) / min(ev))
}

h_ins <- covariance_health(ins)             # all instruments, the whole sample
rbind(classes     = covariance_health(cls[1:60, ]),
      equities    = covariance_health(eq[1:60, ]),
      instruments = h_ins)
#>             assets observations rank riskless_directions     condition
#> classes         16           60   16                   0         12297
#> equities       123           60   59                  64           Inf
#> instruments    181          372  178                   3 1570624508843

rank below assets means the matrix is singular, and riskless_directions counts the portfolios it believes are free. Five years of data on 123 equities produces 64 of them.

condition is the subtler warning. It is the ratio of the largest eigenvalue to the smallest, and it bounds how much the inverse magnifies an error in the input. A finite but very large value is more dangerous than an infinite one, because everything still runs: the matrix inverts, weights come back, and they are a magnified copy of the sampling noise. Past roughly \(10^{8}\) the computation is reading noise with double precision arithmetic.

The third row is that case. All 181 instruments on the full 372 months have \(N < T - 1\), so Equation 7.2 does not bind and every eigenvalue is positive, yet qr() reports rank 178 at its default tolerance, 3 eigenvalues lie below \(10^{-12}\) and the condition number is \(10^{12.2}\). No longer sample cures it: the cause is the bond block of Section 2.2, where some instruments nearly replicate others.

rank_report <- function(X, label) {
  vapply(c(60L, 120L, 180L), function(W) {
    S  <- stats::cov(zoo::coredata(X[seq_len(W), ]))
    ev <- eigen(S, symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
    c(T = W, N = ncol(X), rank = qr(S)$rank, min_eigen = min(ev),
      condition = if (min(ev) <= 0) Inf else max(ev) / min(ev))
  }, numeric(5)) |> t() |> as.data.frame() |> dplyr::mutate(universe = label)
}

dplyr::bind_rows(rank_report(cls, "16 classes"),

#> # A tibble: 6 × 6

Look at min_eigen on the two singular rows. It is negative. A covariance matrix cannot have a negative eigenvalue; that is floating-point noise around an eigenvalue which is exactly zero. The matrix is not imprecise. It describes a world in which some portfolio has negative variance.

7.1.3 Why this book has not needed the chapter until now

Every allocation up to here happened across asset classes, and at that level the problem does not arise.

bad <- sum(vapply(seq.int(61L, nrow(cls)), function(i) {
  qr(stats::cov(zoo::coredata(cls[(i - 60L):(i - 1L), ])))$rank < ncol(cls)
}, logical(1)))

c(rolling_windows = nrow(cls) - 60L, rank_deficient = bad)
#> rolling_windows  rank_deficient 
#>             312               0

Not one sixty-month window in the sample is rank deficient at class level. The moment Section 21.1 descends into individual securities it becomes the only question that matters, and nothing about the data has changed. What changed is how many things the portfolio was asked to hold.

7.2 Four Routes

There are four routes. Two change what may be owned, two change how it is estimated. We take them in order of what they cost.

7.2.1 Route one: hold fewer things

The cheapest fix is not to have the problem. Allocating across sixteen classes rather than 123 securities, or across sector sleeves rather than names, leaves a matrix comfortably estimable on five years.

This is not a dodge. Most institutional mandates are set at exactly this level for exactly this reason, and Section 15.1 treats the sleeve structure as a decision rather than a convenience. It does not help when the mandate names 123 securities, which is what a stock selection process hands its allocator.

7.2.2 Route two: impose a structure

If 7,626 free parameters cannot be estimated, the answer is to stop trying to. Assert instead that returns are driven by \(k\) common factors plus asset-specific noise, so that

\[ \boldsymbol{r}_t = \boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{f}_t + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t, \qquad \boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{B}' + \boldsymbol{D}, \tag{7.5}\]

with \(\boldsymbol{B}\) the \(N \times k\) loadings, \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) the covariance of the factors and \(\boldsymbol{D}\) a diagonal matrix of residual variances. That is \(Nk + N\) numbers rather than \(N(N+1)/2\): for three factors on 123 assets, 492 rather than several thousand. Because \(\boldsymbol{D}\) is positive by construction, Equation 7.5 cannot be singular whatever \(T\) is. Fan et al. (2008) give the conditions under which it converges where Equation 7.1 does not, and Bai and Ng (2002) and Connor and Korajczyk (1993) the machinery for choosing \(k\) rather than asserting it.

The factors can be economic, as in Section 5.2, or purely statistical, the principal components of the returns themselves. The second is six lines.

factor_covariance <- function(X, k = 3L) {
  x  <- zoo::coredata(X)
  pc <- prcomp(x)
  B  <- pc$rotation[, seq_len(k), drop = FALSE]      # loadings, N x k
  f  <- x %*% B                                      # factor realizations
  e  <- x - f %*% t(B)                               # residuals
  e  <- sweep(e, 2, colMeans(e))
  B %*% stats::cov(f) %*% t(B) + diag(colSums(e^2) / (nrow(x) - k - 1L))
}

S_fac <- factor_covariance(eq[1:120, ], k = 3L)
e_fac <- eigen(S_fac, symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
c(rank = qr(S_fac)$rank, min_eigen = min(e_fac),
  condition = max(e_fac) / min(e_fac))
#>       rank  min_eigen  condition 
#> 123.000000   0.001926  98.592045

Full rank on a window where the sample matrix has 4 riskless directions, and a condition number of 99 where the sample matrix’s is infinite. The structure does the work: because every asset’s risk runs through three shared factors, no combination can cancel to zero unless the residuals do, and those are positive by construction.

PortfolioAnalytics ships this as statistical.factor.model(), with one constraint worth knowing about in the released version.

sapply(c(60L, 120L, 180L), function(W) {
  tryCatch({ statistical.factor.model(eq[seq_len(W), ], k = 3L); "accepted" },
           error = function(e) conditionMessage(e))
})
#> [1] "fewer observations than assets" "fewer observations than assets"
#> [3] "accepted"

It requires at least as many observations as assets, which is precisely the condition under which it is not needed. The body of the function does not: principal components need enough components, not enough columns, which is why the six lines above run on the same window. Appendix D records the patch that relaxes the check and where it stands.

7.2.3 Route three: shrink the matrix

Keep every asset and keep the sample estimate, but pull it part of the way towards a target that is well conditioned by construction:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\text{shrunk}} = (1-\lambda)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} + \lambda\,\boldsymbol{F}, \qquad \lambda \in [0, 1], \tag{7.6}\]

with \(\boldsymbol{F}\) commonly a scaled identity and sometimes a constant-correlation or factor matrix. The construction descends from Stein (1956), who showed that shrinking a multivariate estimate towards a fixed point can dominate the sample estimate in mean squared error despite being biased. Ledoit and Wolf (2003) and Ledoit and Wolf (2004) derive the \(\lambda\) minimizing expected loss and show that it can be estimated from the data, so it is not a tuning parameter anyone has to guess; Schäfer and Strimmer (2005) give the analytic form corpcor implements, and Ledoit and Wolf (2017) extend it to shrink each eigenvalue by a different amount.

This is the workhorse. It needs no assumption about what drives returns, it cannot fail, and Equation 7.6 is invertible whenever \(\boldsymbol{F}\) is and \(\lambda > 0\).

S_shr <- corpcor::cov.shrink(zoo::coredata(eq[1:120, ]), verbose = FALSE)
c(lambda = attr(S_shr, "lambda"),
  rank = qr(S_shr)$rank,
  min_eigen = min(eigen(as.matrix(S_shr), symmetric = TRUE,
                        only.values = TRUE)$values))
#>      lambda        rank   min_eigen 
#>   0.2329951 123.0000000   0.0009555

lambda is the weight on the target rather than on the data, 23% of the way towards independence here.

7.2.4 Route four: estimate robustly

The three routes above address a matrix badly conditioned because the sample is short. A robust estimator addresses one badly centered because a few observations dominate it. That is a different problem, and it is taken up below.

7.2.5 Getting any of this into the optimizer

PortfolioAnalytics routes every moment estimate through one function, and the released version of that function does not offer shrinkage.

eval(formals(set.portfolio.moments)$method)
#> [1] "sample"          "boudt"           "black_litterman" "meucci"

sample is the sample matrix, boudt a factor-based cleaning of the higher moments, black_litterman and meucci view-blending machinery belonging in Section 17.1. None of them is route three.

This is the point at which many users give up and compute weights outside the package, discarding every constraint and diagnostic it provides. That is unnecessary. optimize.portfolio() takes a momentFUN argument, and it accepts a function object rather than merely the name of one.

shrinkage_moments <- function(R, portfolio) {
  X <- zoo::coredata(R)
  S <- corpcor::cov.shrink(X, verbose = FALSE)
  S <- matrix(as.numeric(S), ncol(X), ncol(X),
              dimnames = list(colnames(X), colnames(X)))
  list(mu = colMeans(X), sigma = S, m3 = NULL, m4 = NULL,
       mean = colMeans(X), var = S)
}

spec <- portfolio.spec(colnames(eq)) |>
  add.constraint("full_investment") |> add.constraint("long_only") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")

opt <- optimize.portfolio(eq[1:120, ], spec, optimize_method = "ROI",
                          momentFUN = shrinkage_moments)
w <- extractWeights(opt)
c(holdings = sum(w > 1e-6), sum = sum(w), max_weight = max(w))
#>   holdings        sum max_weight 
#>   42.00000    1.00000    0.07059

Because it is a function rather than a name, it can be built on the fly with its settings baked in: one per shrinkage intensity to be compared, or one closing over a factor model fitted elsewhere. Nothing has to live in the global environment.

ImportantA moment function must set both pairs of names

The returned list carries mu and sigma, and also mean and var. Different code paths inside the package read different names, so a moment function that sets only one pair works until the objective changes and then fails in a way that points nowhere near the cause. The matrix must also be a plain matrix carrying dimnames, because cov.shrink() returns attributes that some downstream code does not expect.

7.2.6 When the package function is the thing in the way

There is a third option beyond using a function and working around it, and on an open source package it is usually the right one: fix it and send the fix back. The constraint in statistical.factor.model() is one line, the reason it is wrong is demonstrable in a paragraph, and a patch with a test is less work than the workaround it replaces. Appendix D lists the patches this book contributed and their state, which is also the only honest way to tell a reader which behavior to expect.

7.2.7 Route four in full: robust estimation

PortfolioAnalytics ships a family of robust covariance estimators that almost nobody uses and that no textbook of this generation covers.

grep("covRob", ls("package:PortfolioAnalytics"), value = TRUE)
#> [1] "custom.covRob.Mcd"   "custom.covRob.MM"    "custom.covRob.Rocke"
#> [4] "custom.covRob.TSGS"  "MycovRobMcd"         "MycovRobTSGS"

These answer route four. Shrinkage repairs a matrix badly conditioned because the sample is short; a robust estimator repairs one badly centered because a few months dominate it, whether a flash crash, a redenomination or a bad print in a vendor file. The minimum covariance determinant estimator behind custom.covRob.Mcd() searches for the subset of \(h\) observations whose sample covariance has the smallest determinant and estimates from that subset alone, discarding the rest (Rousseeuw 1984; Rousseeuw and Van Driessen 1999).

They plug in exactly where route three did.

robust_moments <- function(R, portfolio) {
  m <- custom.covRob.Mcd(zoo::coredata(R))
  list(mu = m$mu, sigma = m$sigma, m3 = NULL, m4 = NULL,
       mean = m$mu, var = m$sigma)
}

rb <- robust_moments(cls[1:120, ])
sb <- stats::cov(zoo::coredata(cls[1:120, ]))
c(robust_mean_vol = mean(sqrt(diag(rb$sigma))) * sqrt(12),
  sample_mean_vol = mean(sqrt(diag(sb))) * sqrt(12))
#> robust_mean_vol sample_mean_vol 
#>          0.1061          0.1244

The robust estimate of volatility is lower, because it has judged part of what the sample matrix calls risk to be a few unusual months rather than the process. Whether that is an improvement is a judgment about the world rather than about the estimator: right if those months were contamination, wrong if they were the tail an investor is paid to bear. Section 4.3 found that this market’s extreme months arrive together across series, which argues for the second reading. Section 28.1 makes the case properly, and Section 10.1 is where discarding the tail stops being defensible.

7.3 What the Routes Do to the Matrix

7.3.1 Scoring the routes out of sample

This is not a question the matrix can answer. Every estimator reports a flattering number about itself in sample. The only honest test is to estimate on one window, hold the resulting portfolio for a month, and score what happened.

R/ch05-estimator-race.R does that for four estimators and three window lengths, long only and fully invested, rebalanced monthly. All three windows are scored on the same months, so a longer window earns no credit for starting later.

FIRST_OOS <- 181L
oos    <- seq.int(FIRST_OOS, nrow(eq))
stopifnot(all(sim_riskfree_monthly$date == zoo::index(eq)))   # the bill lines up
ew_ret <- rowMeans(zoo::coredata(eq[oos, ]))
ew_ex  <- ew_ret - sim_riskfree_monthly$return[oos]

ew <- c(ann_vol = stats::sd(ew_ret) * sqrt(12),
        ann_ret = mean(ew_ret) * 12,
        sharpe  = mean(ew_ex) / stats::sd(ew_ex) * sqrt(12))
round(ew, 4)
#> ann_vol ann_ret  sharpe 
#>  0.1829  0.1182  0.5993

Read failed first. At sixty and at one hundred and twenty months, two of the four estimators produce nothing at all, not a poor portfolio but no portfolio, on every one of the 192 rebalancing dates. That is Equation 7.2 arriving as an empty result. Route three and the diagonal target reach a weight vector; the sample matrix and the released factor model do not.

Where the sample matrix fails, shrinkage gives the lower volatility of the two estimators that run: 12.2% at a hundred and twenty months against 15.9% for the diagonal and 18.3% for holding everything equally.

Then the row that keeps this from being an advertisement.

estimator ann_vol sharpe lambda
sample2 sample 0.1192 0.846 NA
shrunk2 shrunk 0.1196 0.859 0.105
diagonal2 diagonal 0.1604 0.666 NA
factor32 factor3 0.1201 0.738 NA

At a hundred and eighty months the sample matrix is finally estimable and shrinkage stops buying anything: 0.1196 against 0.1192 is a dead heat, and the factor model is marginally behind both on volatility and further behind on Sharpe ratio. Shrinkage is not a better estimator in general. It is the estimator that exists when the others do not, which is a different and more useful property.

The lambda column says so from the inside: it falls from 0.281 to 0.105 as the window lengthens. The procedure asks for less help as it needs less, which is the practical content of the Ledoit-Wolf result.

7.3.2 Lower volatility is not a better portfolio

sharpe is not ann_vol, and the ordering is not the same. At a hundred and eighty months the sample matrix has the lowest volatility and shrinkage the highest Sharpe ratio, and the factor model, within a tenth of a point of both on volatility, trails them by 0.11 on Sharpe ratio. Volatility was the objective, and it does not rank the results.

Minimum variance did what it was asked. It cut annualized volatility from 18.3% to 12.2%, about a third, and gave up 1.0 percentage points of annual return for it, so on this market it finished ahead of equal weighting on Sharpe ratio as well, 0.812 against 0.599. That is not the general finding: DeMiguel et al. (2009) report that across their universes no optimized rule, minimum variance included, is reliably ahead of equal weighting, and whether the advantage here belongs to the method or to this sample is the subject of Section 8.1.

Every route is one operation on the eigenvalues of Equation 7.1, and it is easier to see than to derive.

X   <- zoo::coredata(eq[1:120, ])
ev  <- function(S) pmax(eigen(as.matrix(S), symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values, 0)
e_s <- ev(stats::cov(X))
e_h <- ev(corpcor::cov.shrink(X, verbose = FALSE))

tibble::tibble(
  matrix      = c("sample", "shrunk"),
  largest     = c(e_s[1], e_h[1]),
  smallest    = c(min(e_s), min(e_h)),
  zero_eigens = c(sum(e_s < 1e-12), sum(e_h < 1e-12)),
  condition   = c(e_s[1] / max(min(e_s), .Machine$double.eps), e_h[1] / min(e_h)))
#> # A tibble: 2 × 5
#>   matrix largest smallest zero_eigens condition
#>   <chr>    <dbl>    <dbl>       <int>     <dbl>
#> 1 sample   0.183 0                  4   8.25e14
#> 2 shrunk   0.141 0.000956           0   1.48e 2

4 eigenvalues of the sample matrix are zero. Those are the riskless portfolios of Equation 7.3 seen from the other side, and they are not a property of these particular equities. They would be there if the returns were coin flips, because 120 observations cannot pin down 123 directions, by Equation 7.2.

The largest falls by 23.0% and the smallest non-zero one rises by a factor of 301, which is Equation 7.4 brought back into a range where the inverse means something. That is what a shrinkage target is: not a better guess at any individual covariance, but a statement that the extremes of a sample spectrum are mostly sampling error and belong nearer the middle, which is the reading Laloux et al. (1999) gave them. A factor model reaches the same place by another road, building Equation 7.5 from a few directions plus a positive residual for every asset, so that small eigenvalues cannot be zero to begin with.

7.3.3 Shrink towards what, when the target moves?

The usual \(\boldsymbol{F}\) in Equation 7.6 is a scaled identity: equal variances, no correlations. It is chosen for being maximally unlike the noisy thing it corrects, not for being true. How untrue it is depends on when the question is asked.

d      <- zoo::index(cls)
in_ep  <- vapply(sim_episodes$episode, function(e) in_episode(d, e),
                 logical(length(d)))
crisis <- in_ep[, "credit_crisis"]
calm   <- rowSums(in_ep) == 0               # outside all four episodes

regime_shape <- function(keep) {
  e <- ev(stats::cov(zoo::coredata(cls[keep, ])))
  C <- stats::cor(cls[keep, ])
  data.frame(months = sum(keep), pc1_share = e[1] / sum(e),
             directions = sum(e)^2 / sum(e^2),
             mean_correlation = mean(C[upper.tri(C)]))
}

# A short sample concentrates its spectrum whatever the market does, so score
# every run of calm months of the crisis's length as well.
n_c   <- sum(crisis)
runs  <- which(vapply(seq_len(length(d) - n_c + 1L),
                      function(i) all(calm[i + seq_len(n_c) - 1L]), logical(1)))
short <- do.call(rbind, lapply(runs, function(i) {
  regime_shape(seq_along(d) %in% (i + seq_len(n_c) - 1L))
}))

shape <- rbind(calm       = regime_shape(calm),
               calm_short = as.data.frame(lapply(short, stats::median)),
               crisis     = regime_shape(crisis))

#> 3 crisis          7     0.760       1.66            0.509

The calm sample is the 320 months outside the four episodes of sim_episodes, the crisis the 7 months of the credit crisis. In calm months the first principal component carries 37% of the variance and the matrix behaves like roughly 4.8 independent directions; through the credit crisis the same sixteen series carry 76% and collapse to 1.7. Part of that is arithmetic, since by Equation 7.2 a 7-month matrix has at most 6 non-zero eigenvalues and the median calm window of that length already shows 56% and 2.6, but the crisis lies beyond 97% of those windows, and average pairwise correlation, which no rank bound touches, rises from 0.30 to 0.51, above 92% of them.

A diagonal target asserts that the assets are unrelated. In calm months that is wrong by some margin; in the credit crisis it is wrong by far more, and precisely when the portfolio depends on it. Shrinking towards independence is least defensible exactly when it does the most work, which is the honest reason to prefer a factor target where one is available. Section 19.1 is where this choice stops being a footnote.

7.3.4 The shrinkage already in place

One last connection, because it reframes much of this book. Every optimization so far has been long only. Jagannathan and Ma (2003) showed that a non-negativity constraint is not merely a restriction: it acts on the solution the way Equation 7.6 acts on the matrix, because it forbids exactly the large offsetting long-short positions that Equation 7.3 invites. Their finding is that imposing the constraint improves out-of-sample risk even when it is known to be false, which is why a constrained optimization on a sample matrix behaves far better than the unconstrained arithmetic suggests. Frost and Savarino (1988) reach the same conclusion from the Bayesian side.

That is worth holding next to Table 7.1. Route three took about a third off the volatility of an equal-weighted sleeve. The long-only constraint, imposed for reasons of mandate rather than of statistics, had already done a comparable amount of work for nothing.

Key Takeaways

  • By Equation 7.2 the sample covariance matrix is singular whenever there are more assets than observations, regardless of the data. On 123 equities and five years of monthly returns it is short of full rank by more than sixty.
  • A singular estimate does not merely give a noisy answer. By Equation 7.3 it asserts that some portfolio has zero variance, and a minimizer will find it and hold it.
  • Even without singularity, the condition number of Equation 7.4 measures how much an input error is magnified in the weights, and the smallest eigenvalues of a financial covariance matrix are largely indistinguishable from noise (Laloux et al. 1999; Marčenko and Pastur 1967).
  • There are four responses: hold fewer assets, impose the factor structure of Equation 7.5, shrink towards a well-conditioned target as in Equation 7.6, or estimate robustly. The first three address conditioning; the fourth addresses contamination, which is a different complaint.
  • Shrinkage is not a better estimator in general. Scored out of sample it wins where the sample matrix cannot be computed at all, and ties with it once the window is long enough. Its own \(\lambda\) falls as the window grows, which is the procedure reporting that it is needed less.
  • Minimum variance did what it was asked and cut volatility by about a third, and on this market it finished ahead of equal weighting on Sharpe ratio as well. Whether that survives on other universes is Section 8.1’s subject.
  • The long-only constraint is itself a form of shrinkage and was already doing comparable work before any estimator was changed (Jagannathan and Ma 2003).

Exercises

  1. Apply covariance_health() to the government and credit instruments of sim_assets alone, on the full sample. There are far more observations than assets, so Equation 7.2 does not bind, and the matrix is nearly singular all the same. Identify the reason from the eigenvectors of the smallest eigenvalues rather than from the asset names.
  2. corpcor::cov.shrink() chooses lambda analytically. Fix it instead at 0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5 and 1 and plot realized out-of-sample volatility against it. Where is the minimum, and is the analytic choice near it?
  3. Replace shrinkage_moments() with one built on factor_covariance() and compare them on the same rolling windows. At which window length does the factor structure stop helping, and does that coincide with the point where the sample matrix becomes estimable?
