8  Estimation Error and Its Cost

How much is a better forecast worth, in Sharpe ratio? DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2009) asked the question the other way round: they ran fourteen optimizing rules against the portfolio that forecasts nothing, equal weighting, on seven datasets, and found that none of them beat it reliably out of sample. The estimation window a mean-variance investor would need before optimization paid was measured in thousands of months, about three thousand for twenty-five assets and six thousand for fifty. That paper is the end of a line that runs through most of modern portfolio theory. Jobson and Korkie (1980) derived the sampling distribution of estimated efficient portfolios and showed that the estimated tangency portfolio can be outperformed by equal weighting; Jobson and Korkie (1981) gave the test for whether two Sharpe ratios differ at all. Michaud (1989) named the mechanism: an optimizer is an error maximizer, because it allocates most to whatever the sample happened to flatter. Best and Grauer (1991) showed analytically that small changes in the mean vector move the weights by large amounts while barely moving the portfolio’s own mean and variance. Chopra and Ziemba (1993) priced the inputs, finding errors in means roughly eleven times as costly as errors in variances and twice as costly again as errors in covariances. Broadie (1993) separated the true, the estimated and the actual frontier and showed that the estimated one lies above both. Frost and Savarino (1988) found that constraining the weights improves realized performance, and Jagannathan and Ma (2003) explained why: a short-sale constraint is a shrinkage of the covariance matrix in disguise. Kan and Zhou (2007) gave the expected loss in closed form under normality, and Ledoit and Wolf (2004) gave the estimator of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) that works when the sample one does not.

This chapter is about strategic allocation: the inputs are unconditional moments, and the question is what estimating them costs. We first define the two naive benchmarks and state what is known about the loss, then measure the gap between the Sharpe ratio a rolling window promises and the one the next month delivers, for four rules on the sixteen risky class series, then simulate under a known truth to split the loss between the mean and the covariance and to find the window length at which optimization overtakes equal weighting.

The data are the sixteen risky class series of Section 2.2, in excess of the Treasury bill for the reasons of Section 3.1.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)   # the sixteen risky classes
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return                                  # the bill
Re <- R - rf                                                       # excess returns
N  <- ncol(Re)
mu <- colMeans(Re)                                                 # full-sample moments; the
S  <- stats::cov(Re)                                               # application treats them as the truth
stopifnot(N == 16L, !anyNA(Re), nrow(Re) == length(rf))

8.1 The Plug-In Rule and the Price of Its Inputs

8.1.1 What the estimator is asked to do

The objective of Markowitz (1952), stated in Section 3.1 and solved in Section 9.1, consumes a mean vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and a covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). Neither is observed. From \(T\) months of excess returns \(\boldsymbol{r}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{r}_T\) the sample estimates are

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}_t, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T} (\boldsymbol{r}_t - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})(\boldsymbol{r}_t - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})' , \tag{8.1}\]

and the plug-in rule substitutes them into the solution as though they were the truth. For the tangency portfolio of Equation 9.5 that gives

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{\text{tan}} = \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}} {\boldsymbol{\iota}'\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{\text{mv}} = \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}} {\boldsymbol{\iota}'\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}} , \tag{8.2}\]

with the minimum-variance portfolio of Equation 9.3 beside it. The two differ in one respect that decides this chapter: the second needs no \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\). Section 4.1 showed, following Merton (1980), that the precision of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) depends on the calendar span of the sample and not on how often it is observed, while the precision of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) improves with every observation. A rule that uses only \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) therefore uses only the input that can be estimated. Section 6.1 and Section 7.1 treat the two estimation problems separately; this chapter measures what they cost together, and which of them costs more.

Under a long-only constraint neither expression in Equation 8.2 has a closed form and both become quadratic programs, as Section 9.1 explains. Everything computed below is long only, and Section 8.3 shows that the constraint is not a detail.

8.1.2 Two rules that estimate almost nothing

The benchmarks against which every optimizing rule is judged are two portfolios that require no optimizer. Equal weighting holds

\[ \boldsymbol{w}_{1/N} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\iota}}{N}, \tag{8.3}\]

and estimates nothing at all: it is the same portfolio on every window, of every length, on every history. Inverse-volatility weighting holds

\[ w_i = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_i^{-1}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_j^{-1}}, \tag{8.4}\]

and estimates one moment per asset, the volatility, ignoring the means and every correlation. Section 14.1 treats Equation 8.4 as a risk-based allocation in its own right, the one-line approximation to equal risk contribution; here it is a benchmark, the rule that sits one estimated moment away from \(1/N\). Both are estimators in the statistical sense, with Equation 8.3 the degenerate one whose variance is zero, and that is the correct way to read the comparison that follows: not naive against sophisticated, but a sequence of rules that estimate zero, one, or all of the moments the objective asks for.

8.1.3 Why the promise is inflated and the delivery is not

The mechanism of Best and Grauer (1991) is visible in Equation 8.2. The unconstrained weights are linear in \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) with coefficient matrix \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\), so an error in the mean vector is multiplied by the inverse covariance matrix before it reaches the portfolio, and Section 7.1 showed how large the entries of that inverse become as the matrix approaches singularity. The error in \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) is not small: from Equation 4.3 its standard error over sixty months is of the order of the volatility divided by \(\sqrt{5}\), which for an equity market is a few percent a year, comparable to the premium being estimated.

Two consequences follow, and both can be written down. First, the Sharpe ratio a plug-in portfolio reports on its own estimation window is biased upward. With \(\theta^2 = \boldsymbol{\mu}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\) the true squared maximum Sharpe ratio and \(\hat{\theta}^2\) its sample counterpart, Kan and Zhou (2007) show, under independent normal returns and with \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) scaled by \(T\) rather than \(T-1\), that

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\theta}^2\big] = \frac{T\,\theta^2 + N}{T - N - 2} , \tag{8.5}\]

which exceeds \(\theta^2\) for every finite \(T\) and does so by a term of order \(N/T\) that is present even when \(\theta^2 = 0\). A window of \(T\) months on \(N\) assets manufactures a squared Sharpe ratio of roughly \(N/T\) from noise alone. Second, the portfolio built on those estimates is expected to do worse than the true optimum. For an investor with quadratic utility \(U(\boldsymbol{w}) = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\), whose unconstrained optimum \(\boldsymbol{w}^* = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}/\gamma\) attains \(U(\boldsymbol{w}^*) = \theta^2/(2\gamma)\), Kan and Zhou (2007) derive the expected utility of the plug-in rule \(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}/\gamma\) in closed form:

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[U(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}})\big] = \frac{\theta^2}{\gamma}\,\frac{T}{T-N-2} \;-\;\frac{T^2 (T-2)}{2\gamma\,(T-N-1)(T-N-2)(T-N-4)} \left(\theta^2 + \frac{N}{T}\right). \tag{8.6}\]

The derivation is theirs and we do not repeat it; what matters is the shape. The first term is the expected return of the plug-in portfolio, inflated by \(T/(T-N-2)\) because \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\) is biased upward. The second is its expected variance, and it carries the same \(N/T\) as Equation 8.5: the noise in \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) is converted into positions, and positions taken on noise have variance but no return. The loss \(U(\boldsymbol{w}^*) - \mathbb{E}[U(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}})]\) falls with \(T\) and rises with \(N\), and for small enough \(T\) relative to \(N\) the expected utility is negative, meaning that the plug-in investor is expected to end up worse off than one holding the bill. Klein and Bawa (1976) had reached the qualitative conclusion three decades earlier by treating the parameters as uncertain in a Bayesian predictive distribution; Kan and Zhou (2007) put numbers on it, and the box below puts this universe’s numbers in.

NoteWorked: Equation 8.6 on the sixteen series

The only quantity in Equation 8.6 that belongs to the data is \(\theta^2\). On the full sample of 372 months,

theta2 <- as.numeric(t(mu) %*% solve(S, mu))     # squared maximum Sharpe ratio, monthly

# gamma = 1. Both terms scale with 1 / gamma, so the ratio to the optimum does not.
kz_utility <- function(theta2, N, T) {
  theta2 * T / (T - N - 2) -
    T^2 * (T - 2) / (2 * (T - N - 1) * (T - N - 2) * (T - N - 4)) * (theta2 + N / T)
}

kz <- tibble::tibble(T = c(60L, 120L, 240L, nrow(Re), 1000L)) |>
  dplyr::mutate(expected = kz_utility(theta2, N, T),
                optimum  = theta2 / 2,
                share    = expected / optimum)
round(c(theta2 = theta2, max_sharpe_annualized = sqrt(12 * theta2)), 4)
#>                theta2 max_sharpe_annualized 
#>                0.1012                1.1018

so \(\theta^2 = 0.1012\) a month, an unconstrained maximum Sharpe ratio of 1.10 a year. Substituting \(N = 16\) and \(T = 60\) into Equation 8.6 with \(\gamma = 1\),

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[U(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}})\big] = \frac{60}{42} \times 0.1012 - \frac{60^2 \times 58}{2 \times 43 \times 42 \times 40} \left(0.1012 + \frac{16}{60}\right) = 0.1445 - 0.5316 = -0.3871 , \]

against \(U(\boldsymbol{w}^*) = \theta^2 / 2 = 0.0506\). The expected utility of the plug-in investor is negative: on sixty months of sixteen assets, an unconstrained mean-variance investor is expected to do worse than holding cash. The sample this book uses, 372 months, recovers 45% of the optimum, and a thousand months 81%.

The formula assumes normality and independence, which Section 4.3 showed this market violates. Whether that matters at the first decimal can be checked by drawing sixty months from a normal distribution with these moments, forming the plug-in portfolio and scoring it under the truth, two thousand times.

set.seed(1602)

Both expectations agree with Equation 8.5 and Equation 8.6 to the second decimal, which is the algebra working. The promised squared Sharpe ratio on a sixty-month window is 0.53 against a true 0.1012: an annualized promise of 2.52 for a truth of 1.10.

8.1.4 The Sharpe gap

Equation 8.5 and Equation 8.6 make two statements about the same portfolio: it promises more than the truth, and it delivers less. The distance between the two is what this book measures, and it needs a definition that can be computed on data where the truth is unknown. On a rolling window ending at \(t\), with estimates \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_t\) and a rule producing \(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_t\), the promised Sharpe ratio is the one the window itself reports,

\[ \widehat{SR}^{\,\text{promised}}_t = \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_t'\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t} {\sqrt{\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_t'\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_t\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_t}} , \tag{8.7}\]

and the realized Sharpe ratio is the one earned by the sequence of held portfolios, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_t'\boldsymbol{r}_{t+1}\), over every month of the out-of-sample period. The Sharpe gap is the average promise minus the realization,

\[ \text{gap} = \overline{\widehat{SR}^{\,\text{promised}}} - SR^{\,\text{realized}} , \tag{8.8}\]

annualized as in Sharpe (1966) by \(\sqrt{12}\). The gap is a property of a rule on a universe, not of a single window, and it aggregates over the same rolling scheme that Section 25.1 formalizes as Equation 25.1. For equal weighting the promise is not zero: Equation 8.3 estimates no weights, but the Sharpe ratio it reports on a window is still computed from that window’s \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_t\), so its gap measures how much a window flatters even a portfolio that did not look at it. That is what makes it the control.

The gap is not the same thing as the loss in Equation 8.6. The loss compares a rule with the optimum under a truth; the gap compares a rule with itself across time and needs no truth. Broadie (1993)’s three frontiers are the picture: the promise is a point on the estimated frontier, the realization is a point on the actual one, and the true frontier between them is what a simulation can supply and a backtest cannot. Section 8.2 measures the gap; Section 8.3 supplies a truth and measures the loss, which is also where forecast error is put into the investor’s own units.

8.2.2 The two optimized rules as quadratic programs

The loop solves 624 optimizations and the simulation in Section 8.3 solves several thousand more, so the two long-only problems are written directly as the quadratic programs that optimize.portfolio(optimize_method = "ROI") hands to quadprog in any case. The minimum-variance problem is Equation 9.2 without its return constraint. The long-only tangency portfolio uses a standard change of variable: minimize \(\boldsymbol{y}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{y}\) subject to \(\boldsymbol{y}'\boldsymbol{\mu} = 1\) and \(\boldsymbol{y} \geq \boldsymbol{0}\), then rescale \(\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{y}/\boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{y}\); the rescaling leaves the Sharpe ratio unchanged and the constraint makes it the maximum. The trick needs one asset with a positive estimated mean, and the function records when there is none.

# Long-only minimum variance: min w'Sw subject to w'i = 1, w >= 0. Percent scaling
# keeps quadprog's matrix well away from its positive-definiteness tolerance.
gmv_qp <- function(S) {
  n <- ncol(S)
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S * 1e4, dvec = rep(0, n),
                            Amat = cbind(rep(1, n), diag(n)),
                            bvec = c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)
  w <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  setNames(w / sum(w), colnames(S))
}

# Long-only maximum Sharpe: min y'Sy subject to y'mu = 1, y >= 0, then w = y / sum(y).
# With no positive estimated mean the ratio has no maximum worth holding; the rule
# then holds the minimum-variance portfolio and says so through an attribute.
tan_qp <- function(mu, S) {
  n <- length(mu)
  if (max(mu) <= 0) return(structure(gmv_qp(S), fallback = TRUE))
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S * 1e4, dvec = rep(0, n),
                            Amat = cbind(mu * 1e2, diag(n)),
                            bvec = c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)
  y <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  structure(setNames(y / sum(y), names(mu)), fallback = FALSE)
}

# Annualized Sharpe ratio of w under the moments (mu, S); returns are excess already.
sharpe_of <- function(w, mu, S) {
  as.numeric(sum(w * mu) / sqrt(t(w) %*% S %*% w)) * sqrt(12)
}

Before either function is trusted on four thousand problems it is checked against the package on one.

w_gmv_roi <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(

#>        EU_equity GLOBAL_commodity        US_equity    US_government

The full-sample tangency portfolio places 68% in US_government and 19% in US_equity, and its Sharpe ratio under the full-sample moments is 0.991; that number is the promise every shorter window will be compared with.

8.2.3 Three hundred and twelve windows

Each window of 60 months estimates Equation 8.1, forms the four portfolios, records the Sharpe ratio each promises by Equation 8.7 and the total return each earns in the month that follows. Weights are indexed by name and asserted against the columns before any return is multiplied by them.

win  <- 60L
ends <- seq.int(win, nrow(Re) - 1L)           # every window forecasts the month after it
rf_by_month <- setNames(rf, as.character(zoo::index(R)))
oos_start   <- zoo::index(R)[121]             # the first month the register scores
stopifnot(model_year(oos_start) == 11L, model_year(zoo::index(R)[120]) == 10L)

roll <- lapply(ends, function(e) {
  X <- zoo::coredata(Re)[(e - win + 1L):e, , drop = FALSE]
  m <- colMeans(X)
  V <- stats::cov(X)
  rules <- list(
    max_sharpe   = tan_qp(m, V),
    min_variance = gmv_qp(V),
    equal_weight = setNames(rep(1 / N, N), colnames(Re)),
    inverse_vol  = setNames((1 / sqrt(diag(V))) / sum(1 / sqrt(diag(V))), colnames(Re))
  )
  stopifnot(all(vapply(rules, function(w) identical(names(w), colnames(Re)), logical(1))))
  nxt <- zoo::coredata(R)[e + 1L, ]           # total return in the month held
  tibble::tibble(
    date     = zoo::index(R)[e + 1L],
    rule     = names(rules),
    promised = vapply(rules, sharpe_of, numeric(1), mu = m, S = V),
    realized = vapply(rules, function(w) sum(w * nxt), numeric(1)),
    fallback = isTRUE(attr(rules$max_sharpe, "fallback"))
  )
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

c(windows = length(ends), no_positive_mean = sum(roll$fallback[roll$rule == "max_sharpe"]))
#>          windows no_positive_mean 
#>              312                0

The realized Sharpe ratio is computed the way the register of Section 25.3 computes it, compounding the held returns and the bill separately and dividing their difference by the annualized volatility, so that the two chapters can be compared to the third decimal. The register scores every configuration on the same months, from January of model year 11, so that a longer window earns no credit for starting later; the same start is used here.

sharpe_realized <- function(r, rf) {
  n <- length(r)
  (prod(1 + r)^(12 / n) - prod(1 + rf)^(12 / n)) / (stats::sd(r) * sqrt(12))
}

summarise_gap <- function(d) {
  d |>
    dplyr::group_by(rule) |>
    dplyr::summarise(months   = dplyr::n(),
                     promised = mean(promised),
                     realized = sharpe_realized(realized, rf_by_month[as.character(date)]),
                     .groups  = "drop") |>
    dplyr::mutate(gap = promised - realized, kept = realized / promised) |>

8.2.4 The gap, rule by rule

Read Table 8.2 from the bottom. The tangency portfolio promises 1.50 on the average window and delivers 0.55, keeping 36% of its promise and finishing last of the four. Its gap of 0.96 is 17.4 times the gap of equal weighting, whose own gap of 0.06 is the control: that much of a promise is manufactured by the window on a portfolio that never looked at it. Minimum variance, which estimates only \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\), has the highest realized Sharpe ratio, 0.83, and keeps 78% of what it promised. Inverse volatility keeps 84%. Starting the scoring in January of model year 6 instead, with all 312 windows, moves every number and lifts the tangency portfolio from last to rank 2 of 4: it realizes 0.53 against equal weighting’s 0.52. What the scoring period does not change is the gap. At 1.05 the tangency portfolio’s remains the largest of the four, and the four gaps keep their order.

Figure 8.1 is the chapter’s first result. The ordering of the gaps is the ordering of how much each rule estimates: equal weighting, which estimates nothing, has the shortest segment, inverse volatility with its 16 volatilities the next, minimum variance with the whole of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) the third, and the one rule that estimates \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) and maximizes over it the longest, 4.2 times the third. The realizations are ordered differently: the rule with the highest promise realizes least, and the rule that estimates only \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) realizes most. Whether the differences in realized Sharpe ratios are statistically distinguishable is a separate question, answered by the test of Jobson and Korkie (1981) with the correction of Memmel (2003) and by the error bar of Lo (2002) that Section 25.3 puts on the register; the differences in promise are not in doubt, because they are computed rather than sampled.

8.3 Where the Loss Comes From

8.3.1 A truth to score against

A backtest cannot say which input caused the gap, because the truth against which the estimates erred is never observed. A simulation can. We take the full-sample moments of the sixteen series as the truth, draw \(T\) months from a normal distribution with those moments, estimate Equation 8.1 on the draw, form the long-only tangency portfolio, and score it under the truth. Repeating this a few hundred times per \(T\) gives the expected Sharpe ratio a \(T\)-month investor actually earns, against which the promise and the naive rules can be placed. A twelve-month block bootstrap of the actual returns, as in Section 9.3, would preserve the tails that a normal draw loses; exercise 2 asks what changes.

draw_months <- function(T, mu, L) {
  matrix(rnorm(T * length(mu)), T) %*% L + matrix(mu, T, length(mu), byrow = TRUE)
}

w_star  <- tan_qp(mu, S)                       # the long-only tangency under the truth
sr_star <- sharpe_of(w_star, mu, S)
sr_gmv  <- sharpe_of(gmv_qp(S), mu, S)
sr_ew   <- sharpe_of(w_ew, mu, S)
round(c(tangency = sr_star, min_variance = sr_gmv, equal_weight = sr_ew), 3)
#>     tangency min_variance equal_weight 
#>        0.991        0.795        0.617

Under the truth the long-only tangency portfolio has a Sharpe ratio of 0.99, equal weighting 0.62, and minimum variance 0.80. The first number is the ceiling: no estimated portfolio can exceed it under the truth, so every draw lands at or below it, and the average distance is the loss.

8.3.2 The mean channel and the covariance channel

The decomposition is Chopra and Ziemba (1993)’s, done by substitution rather than by perturbation. Three portfolios are formed on every draw: the plug-in rule with both moments estimated, the rule with the estimated mean and the true covariance, and the rule with the true mean and the estimated covariance. The second isolates what estimating \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) costs, the third what estimating \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) costs.

set.seed(1602)
Ts <- c(60L, 120L, 240L, 372L, 1000L)
B  <- 300L

one_draw <- function(T) {
  X <- draw_months(T, mu, L); m <- colMeans(X); V <- stats::cov(X)
  w_hat <- tan_qp(m, V)
  c(plug_in   = sharpe_of(w_hat, mu, S),             # both estimated
    mean_only = sharpe_of(tan_qp(m, S), mu, S),      # estimated mean, true covariance
    cov_only  = sharpe_of(tan_qp(mu, V), mu, S),     # true mean, estimated covariance
    promised  = sharpe_of(w_hat, m, V),              # what the draw itself claims
    fallback  = as.numeric(isTRUE(attr(w_hat, "fallback"))))
}

sim <- lapply(Ts, function(T) {
  d <- vapply(seq_len(B), function(b) one_draw(T), numeric(5))
  tibble::tibble(T = T, channel = rownames(d), mean = rowMeans(d), sd = apply(d, 1, stats::sd))
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

sim_wide <- sim |>
  dplyr::select(-sd) |>
  tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = channel, values_from = mean) |>
  dplyr::mutate(loss = sr_star - plug_in,
                loss_mean = sr_star - mean_only,
                loss_cov  = sr_star - cov_only)
stopifnot(all(sim_wide$fallback == 0), all(sim_wide$plug_in <= sr_star))

At sixty months the plug-in portfolio delivers 0.76 against a promise of 1.38 and a ceiling of 0.99. Of the loss of 0.23, estimating the mean alone accounts for 0.22 and estimating the covariance alone for 0.04: the mean channel costs 6 times as much. That is Chopra and Ziemba (1993)’s finding reproduced on this universe by substitution. At sixty months the ratio is smaller than their eleven, because the long-only constraint caps what an error in \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) can do to the weights, which is Jagannathan and Ma (2003)’s point again; it rises with the window and reaches 12 at a thousand months, because the covariance channel closes faster. That channel is nearly closed at every \(T\) in the table; on sixteen series a sixty-month window already has nearly four observations per asset, the matrix is well conditioned by the measure of Section 7.1, and the shrinkage of Ledoit and Wolf (2004) has little to shrink. The mean channel is still open at a thousand months, where it costs 0.027, and Equation 4.3 says why: a thousand months is eighty-three years, and the standard error of a mean falls with the square root of the span.

One feature of Figure 8.2 deserves a sentence, because it is not what DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2009) would lead a reader to expect. Under this truth the estimated tangency portfolio is above equal weighting at every window in the table, including 60 months: the crossover on the sixteen series is at or below the shortest window shown, and the subsection after the next one locates it. The reason is the ceiling. The true tangency portfolio’s Sharpe ratio exceeds equal weighting’s by 0.37 on this universe, because bonds and equities sit side by side and \(1/N\) cannot tell them apart, and a long-only estimate that loses 0.23 of that is still 0.14 ahead.

8.3.3 Forecast error in the investor’s units

A forecast of \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) is conventionally scored by its mean squared error, and the score is in the wrong units. It says how far the numbers were from the truth; it does not say what being that far cost, because the cost depends on \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), on the constraint set and on the rule that turned the forecast into positions. The idea behind the risk-adjusted forecast error is to measure the error in the units the investor cares about: the Sharpe ratio the portfolio built on the forecast gives up relative to the portfolio built on the truth. Table 8.3 contains that number for the plug-in tangency rule on sixty months: 0.22 of annualized Sharpe ratio lost to the forecast of the mean alone, with the covariance held at its true value. No mean squared error can produce it, and two forecasts with the same mean squared error can produce very different values of it, which is the whole of the argument (Salcher et al. 2026b, 2026a). Section 26.1 derives the measure and shows which channel it charges, and Section 27.1 asks the question that follows from it, whether a forecaster should be trained on mean squared error at all.

8.3.4 When \(1/N\) stops winning

DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2009) derive from Equation 8.6 the window length at which the unconstrained plug-in rule overtakes equal weighting in expected utility, holding the Sharpe ratios of the tangency portfolio and of \(1/N\) fixed at values typical of their datasets, and obtain the thousands of months quoted at the start of this chapter. In that calculation the benefit of optimizing is held fixed and the cost of estimating grows with \(N\) through Equation 8.6, so the required window grows with \(N\). On this universe nothing is held fixed. We draw sub-universes of four, eight and all sixteen series, and for each one and each window length score the long-only plug-in rule, the unconstrained plug-in rule and equal weighting under that sub-universe’s own truth. The unconstrained rule’s Sharpe ratio is that of the direction \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\); leverage does not change a Sharpe ratio, so no budget normalization is needed and none of the sign reversals that plague the normalized version arise.

set.seed(1602)
Ns <- c(4L, 8L, 16L)
Tg <- c(24L, 36L, 48L, 60L, 120L, 240L, 372L, 600L, 1000L, 2000L)

one_universe <- function(n, T) {
  idx  <- if (n == N) seq_len(N) else sort(sample.int(N, n))
  mu_n <- mu[idx]; S_n <- S[idx, idx]
  X <- draw_months(T, mu_n, chol(S_n)); m <- colMeans(X); V <- stats::cov(X)
  c(long_only     = sharpe_of(tan_qp(m, V), mu_n, S_n),
    unconstrained = sharpe_of(solve(V, m), mu_n, S_n),
    equal_weight  = sharpe_of(rep(1 / n, n), mu_n, S_n),
    ceiling       = sharpe_of(tan_qp(mu_n, S_n), mu_n, S_n),
    ceiling_unc   = sqrt(12 * sum(mu_n * solve(S_n, mu_n))))
}

cells  <- tidyr::expand_grid(n_assets = Ns, T = Tg)
region <- lapply(seq_len(nrow(cells)), function(i) {
  n <- cells$n_assets[i]; T <- cells$T[i]
  d <- vapply(seq_len(B), function(b) one_universe(n, T), numeric(5))
  tibble::tibble(n_assets = n, T = T,
                 long_only     = mean(d["long_only", ]     - d["equal_weight", ]),
                 unconstrained = mean(d["unconstrained", ] - d["equal_weight", ]),
                 share_long_only = mean(d["long_only", ] > d["equal_weight", ]),
                 benefit       = mean(d["ceiling", ]       - d["equal_weight", ]),
                 benefit_unc   = mean(d["ceiling_unc", ]   - d["equal_weight", ]))
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

cross <- region |>
  dplyr::group_by(n_assets) |>
  dplyr::summarise(T_long_only     = min(T[long_only > 0]),
                   T_unconstrained = min(T[unconstrained > 0]),
                   benefit         = mean(benefit),
                   benefit_unc     = mean(benefit_unc),
                   .groups = "drop")

Two things in Figure 8.3 and Table 8.4 are consistent with DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2009) and one is not, and the one that is not is instructive. The unconstrained rule loses to \(1/N\) at every short window and by a wide margin: at twenty-four months on sixteen series it is 0.38 of Sharpe ratio behind, which is Equation 8.6 in Sharpe units. The long-only rule crosses far earlier than the unconstrained one at every \(N\), at 24 rather than 120 months on the sixteen, which is the constraint acting as the shrinkage Jagannathan and Ma (2003) describe and DeMiguel, Garlappi, Nogales, et al. (2009) generalize to a bound on the norm of the weights. What differs is the direction in \(N\). The unconstrained crossover is 240 months at four series and 120 at sixteen, and the long-only one falls from 60 to 24: the required window does not grow with \(N\) here, because the benefit of optimizing grows with it. The true tangency portfolio’s advantage over \(1/N\) rises from 0.15 at four series to 0.48 at sixteen, since a larger draw from this universe is more likely to hold government bonds beside equities, which is exactly the heterogeneity that equal weighting cannot exploit. The mechanism is the same in both calibrations: the cost of estimating is of order \(N/T\), and the benefit is whatever the universe offers. DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2009) hold the benefit at the level of a typical equity universe; on a multi-asset universe it is larger, and the crossover moves accordingly. Tu and Zhou (2011) show that a combination of the two rules dominates both, and Kirby and Ostdiek (2012) that much of \(1/N\)’s advantage in the original comparison came from the turnover of the rules it was set against, which Section 23.3 prices on this universe.

The simulation says that on sixty months the long-only tangency portfolio beats equal weighting under this truth in 82% of draws. The rolling backtest of Table 8.2 says it did not: 0.55 against 0.63. On 120-month windows the register has the order the simulation expects, 0.70 against 0.63, so in the backtest the tangency portfolio overtakes \(1/N\) between sixty and 120 months and not at the 24 of Table 8.4. At both windows the tangency portfolio realizes less than the 0.76 and 0.83 that Table 8.3 expects of it. Part of that shortfall is sampling error in the realized figure, which Equation 25.2 puts at about 0.22 of Sharpe ratio over 252 months. The rest is that \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) are not constants: Section 5.3 measured the betas of these series drifting through the sample, Section 4.3 measured the average correlation rising in the credit crisis and the rates shock, and Section 19.1 takes the drift as its subject. Estimation error under a fixed truth is therefore a lower bound on what estimating costs, and the mean-based rule is the one that pays the rest, because it is the one whose positions depend on the input that moves most. Garlappi et al. (2007) and Section 28.1 treat that as uncertainty about the model rather than about its parameters.

Out of sample on sixty-month windows the order is Minimum CVaR, Minimum variance, Equal weight, Inverse volatility, Maximum Sharpe. The rules that estimate no mean occupy the first four places and the one that does is last, which is the ordering of Table 8.2 with minimum conditional value at risk, the strategy of Section 10.3, added at the top. That order holds on thirty-six-month windows as well and not on 120-month ones, where it is Minimum variance, Minimum CVaR, Maximum Sharpe, Equal weight, Inverse volatility, with the tangency portfolio ranking 3 of 5, ahead of both naive rules. Of this chapter’s four rules minimum variance is the strongest at every window, ranking 2 of 5 here, and that is Jagannathan and Ma (2003)’s result: it estimates only \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), the input that can be estimated, and its short-sale constraint shrinks that estimate further.

Equal weighting is not the winner here, ranking 3 of 5, behind two rules that estimate risk alone. That differs from what DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2009) report, and the two findings are consistent. Their claim is about the mean: no rule that estimates \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) beat \(1/N\) reliably, and none does here. The tangency portfolio is behind \(1/N\) on thirty-six- and sixty-month windows and ahead of it on 120-month ones, each time by less than the standard error of 0.22 that Equation 25.2 attaches to its Sharpe ratio. Whether \(1/N\) also beats a rule that estimates \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) alone is a different question, about how well the covariance matrix is estimated, and on sixteen series with 372 months it is estimated well: Section 7.3 showed shrinkage buying nothing once the sample matrix becomes estimable, and Table 8.3 showed the covariance channel costing a few hundredths of a Sharpe ratio. On the larger universes of DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2009) the matrix is the problem and the ranking is theirs, and on the one-hundred-and-eighty-one instruments of Section 21.1 the matrix is the problem again. The lesson is not that optimization fails. It is that the cost of an input is set by how well it can be estimated, and that the mean cannot.

Key Takeaways

  • A plug-in portfolio promises more than the truth and delivers less. Under normality Equation 8.5 gives the size of the promise’s inflation and Equation 8.6 the expected shortfall, both driven by \(N/T\) (Kan and Zhou 2007). On sixty months of sixteen series the unconstrained rule’s expected utility is negative.
  • The Sharpe gap of Equation 8.8, promised minus realized on rolling windows, is computable without a truth. On this universe the tangency portfolio keeps 36% of its promise; minimum variance keeps 78%; equal weighting’s own gap of 0.06 is what a window manufactures on a portfolio that never used it.
  • Splitting the loss by substitution, the mean channel costs 6 times the covariance channel at sixty months and is still open at a thousand (Chopra and Ziemba 1993). The covariance channel is nearly closed on sixteen series.
  • The window at which optimization overtakes \(1/N\) depends on the cost, of order \(N/T\), and on the benefit, which the universe sets. A long-only constraint shortens it several-fold (Jagannathan and Ma 2003), and on a multi-asset universe the benefit grows with \(N\), so the window does not lengthen with \(N\) the way it does in the calibration of DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2009).
  • Estimation error under a fixed truth is a lower bound. The simulated sixty-month tangency portfolio beats \(1/N\) in 82% of draws; the actual one trailed \(1/N\) on sixty-month windows and overtook it only on 120-month ones, realizing less than the simulation expects at both.
  • Out of sample on sixty-month windows the register reads Minimum CVaR, Minimum variance, Equal weight, Inverse volatility, Maximum Sharpe: the rules that estimate no mean lead. On 120-month windows the tangency portfolio ranks 3 of 5, ahead of both naive rules, and minimum variance, which estimates only \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), is the strongest of this chapter’s four rules at every window.
  • equal.weight() and inverse.volatility.weight() are exported, take a return matrix and a specification, and return an optimize.portfolio object. Passed to optimize.portfolio.rebalancing() by name they produce 313 periods of NA weights and no error.
