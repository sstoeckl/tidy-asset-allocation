9  Mean-Variance Optimization

Draft|Code: script · exercises · all chapters|Slides|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

How should an investor divide wealth across assets that differ in expected return, in volatility, and in how they move together? Markowitz (1952) answered that a portfolio cannot be judged asset by asset, because the risk of the whole depends on the covariances between its parts as much as on the variances of each. That observation turned portfolio choice into an optimization problem with a closed-form solution, and it remains the point from which every later method in this book departs.

This chapter is about strategic allocation: the portfolio an investor would hold given long-run, unconditional beliefs about the assets, with no attempt to time anything. We first derive the mean-variance problem and its two anchor solutions, then implement them on the sixteen risky class series introduced in Section 4.1, then ask what the resulting portfolios actually bought and how much of it survives contact with data the optimizer had not seen.

One warning before any number appears. Every moment in this chapter is estimated on the full sample. Nobody standing at the start of the sample knew the covariance matrix of its last year, so what follows is descriptive rather than implementable, and Section 25.1 does the honest version. The purpose here is to establish what the textbook answer looks like when its inputs are not invented.

The data are the sixteen risky class series of Section 2.2, and they enter in excess of the Treasury bill, for the reasons given in Section 3.1: a tangency portfolio exists only relative to a risk-free rate, PortfolioAnalytics has no argument for one, and the excess formulation is the only one that stays coherent once weights are allowed to sum to less than one.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return
Re <- R - rf
stopifnot(ncol(Re) == 16L, !anyNA(Re))

9.1 The Mean-Variance Problem

Write \(\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N\) for the vector of portfolio weights over \(N\) assets, \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) for the vector of expected excess returns, and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) for the covariance matrix of those excess returns. Let \(\boldsymbol{\iota}\) denote a vector of ones. The portfolio’s expected excess return and variance are

\[ \mu_{\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu}, \qquad \sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}. \tag{9.1}\]

The second expression is the whole of Markowitz (1952) in one line. The variance of a portfolio is not the weighted average of the variances of its parts; the off-diagonal terms of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) enter too, and they can be negative. Combining assets can therefore produce a portfolio with a lower volatility than any of its constituents, which is not true of any other portfolio characteristic we use.

Two moments per portfolio make comparison a matter of geometry. In the plane with volatility on the horizontal axis and expected return on the vertical, a portfolio \(A\) dominates a portfolio \(B\) if \(\mu_A \geq \mu_B\) and \(\sigma_A \leq \sigma_B\) with at least one inequality strict: the same return for less risk, or more return for the same risk. This is the mean-variance criterion of Markowitz (1952), introduced in Section 3.1, and it has a direction: a better portfolio always lies to the northwest of a worse one. A portfolio that no feasible portfolio dominates is efficient. The same vocabulary applies to single assets, and Section 9.2 applies it to the sixteen series before any optimizer runs.

An investor who cares only about these two moments therefore wants, for each attainable level of expected return \(\bar{\mu}\), the portfolio that achieves it at least variance:

\[ \min_{\boldsymbol{w}} \; \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\iota} = 1, \quad \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} = \bar{\mu}. \tag{9.2}\]

The set of solutions, traced over \(\bar{\mu}\), is the minimum-variance frontier. Its upper branch, from the portfolio of smallest variance upward, is the efficient frontier: the set of portfolios no feasible portfolio dominates. The lower branch is attainable but never chosen, because for each of its points the upper branch offers more return at the same volatility.

9.1.1 Two anchors with closed forms

Dropping the return constraint from Equation 9.2 leaves the minimum-variance portfolio, the single portfolio that minimizes variance subject only to being fully invested:

\[ \boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mv}} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}} {\boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}}. \tag{9.3}\]

Note what is absent: \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) does not appear. The minimum-variance portfolio is the one point on the frontier that requires no view on expected returns at all, and that fact will do a great deal of work later in this chapter.

NoteWorked: two assets, by hand

With \(N = 2\) the matrix algebra of Equation 9.3 collapses to a formula anyone can evaluate on paper. Writing \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2\) for the two volatilities and \(\sigma_{12} = \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\) for the covariance,

\[ w_1 = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \sigma_{12}}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\sigma_{12}}, \qquad w_2 = 1 - w_1 . \tag{9.4}\]

Take the two series a textbook would reach for, US_equity and US_government, one with a high volatility and one with a low one. Their annualized sample moments are

pair <- c("US_equity", "US_government")
X    <- Re[, pair]

s1  <- stats::sd(X[, 1]) * sqrt(12)          # equity volatility
s2  <- stats::sd(X[, 2]) * sqrt(12)          # government volatility
rho <- stats::cor(X)[1, 2]
s12 <- rho * s1 * s2

w1       <- (s2^2 - s12) / (s1^2 + s2^2 - 2 * s12)
port_vol <- sqrt(w1^2 * s1^2 + (1 - w1)^2 * s2^2 + 2 * w1 * (1 - w1) * s12)

round(c(sigma_equity = s1, sigma_government = s2,
        correlation = rho, covariance = s12), 4)
#>     sigma_equity sigma_government      correlation       covariance 
#>           0.1562           0.0475          -0.1370          -0.0010

so that \(\sigma_1 = 0.1562\), \(\sigma_2 = 0.0475\) and \(\sigma_{12} = -0.1370 \times 0.1562 \times 0.0475 = -0.00102\). Substituting into Equation 9.4,

\[ w_1 = \frac{0.00226 - -0.00102} {0.02439 + 0.00226 - 2 \times -0.00102} = 0.1142, \qquad w_2 = 1 - w_1 = 0.8858, \]

and the volatility of that portfolio, from Equation 9.1 with two assets, is

\[ \sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}} = \sqrt{w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 w_2 \sigma_{12}} = \sqrt{0.00032 + 0.00177 + -0.00021} = 0.0434 . \]

The same arithmetic in code, so that the numbers above are computed rather than typed:

round(c(w_equity = w1, w_government = 1 - w1,
        portfolio_vol = port_vol,
        lowest_vol_constituent = min(s1, s2),
        discount = port_vol / min(s1, s2) - 1), 4)
#>               w_equity           w_government          portfolio_vol 
#>                 0.1142                 0.8858                 0.0434 
#> lowest_vol_constituent               discount 
#>                 0.0475                -0.0866

The portfolio has a lower volatility than either asset it holds, at 4.34% against 4.75% for the less volatile constituent. That is the whole of diversification in one number, and it happens because \(\sigma_{12}\) is negative: the cross term in Equation 9.1 subtracts where the two variances add.

Everything depends on the correlation, and the classic exhibit is to vary it while holding both volatilities fixed.

vary_rho <- function(r) {
  cv <- r * s1 * s2
  w  <- (s2^2 - cv) / (s1^2 + s2^2 - 2 * cv)
  c(rho = r, w_equity = w,
    # at rho = -1 the variance is zero and floating point can return a tiny
    # negative number; the floor keeps the square root defined
    portfolio_vol = sqrt(pmax(0, w^2 * s1^2 + (1 - w)^2 * s2^2 + 2 * w * (1 - w) * cv)))
}

round(t(vapply(c(-1, -0.5, 0, rho, 0.5, 0.9, 1), vary_rho, numeric(3))), 4)
#>         rho w_equity portfolio_vol
#> [1,] -1.000   0.2334        0.0000
#> [2,] -0.500   0.1753        0.0348
#> [3,]  0.000   0.0848        0.0455
#> [4,] -0.137   0.1142        0.0434
#> [5,]  0.500  -0.0755        0.0464
#> [6,]  0.900  -0.3328        0.0281
#> [7,]  1.000  -0.4376        0.0000

Read the ends first. At \(\rho = -1\) and at \(\rho = +1\) the attainable volatility is zero, because two perfectly correlated assets can be combined into a riskless position: one long pair at \(\rho = -1\), and a long-short pair at \(\rho = +1\), which is why the equity weight turns negative there. Between those extremes the benefit shrinks as \(\rho\) rises. At the measured correlation of -0.14 a small part of it is left: the portfolio’s volatility is 8.7% below that of the less volatile asset.

Two lessons carry into the sixteen-asset case. The riskless combinations at \(\rho = \pm 1\) are the two-asset version of Equation 7.3: when the covariance matrix is singular, some combination has an estimated variance of exactly zero, and an optimizer will find it. And Equation 9.4 needs no positivity: at \(\rho = +1\) it shorts, which is the closed form behaving exactly as it does on sixteen assets, where it wants to short 5 of them.

Finally, the scalar formula and the matrix formula are the same statement, so they must agree.

S2   <- stats::cov(X) * 12
i2   <- rep(1, 2)
w_mx <- as.numeric(solve(S2) %*% i2) / as.numeric(t(i2) %*% solve(S2) %*% i2)

stopifnot(all.equal(w1, w_mx[1]))
round(c(by_hand = w1, by_matrix = w_mx[1]), 8)
#>   by_hand by_matrix 
#>    0.1142    0.1142

The stopifnot() is not decoration. It is the cheapest possible guard that the hand version and the matrix version have not drifted apart, and it fails the render rather than printing a wrong number.

If a risk-free asset is available, Tobin (1958) showed that the investor’s problem separates. Every efficient portfolio is a combination of the risk-free asset and one single risky portfolio, the same one for every investor regardless of risk aversion. That portfolio is the tangency portfolio, and when returns are measured in excess of the risk-free rate it is

\[ \boldsymbol{w}_{\text{tan}} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}} {\boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}}. \tag{9.5}\]

It is the portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio (Sharpe 1966), the point at which a ray from the risk-free rate touches the frontier. Two-fund separation is what allows Sharpe (1964) to move from an individual’s portfolio problem to a market equilibrium, and Roll (1977) is the reminder that testing the resulting model requires identifying that portfolio, which is harder than it looks.

The frontier itself has a closed form, due to Merton (1972). Defining the three scalars \(A = \boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}\), \(B = \boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(C = \boldsymbol{\mu}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\), the minimum variance attainable at expected return \(\bar{\mu}\) is

\[ \sigma^2(\bar{\mu}) = \frac{A\bar{\mu}^2 - 2B\bar{\mu} + C}{AC - B^2}, \tag{9.6}\]

a parabola in mean-variance space and a hyperbola in mean-standard-deviation space. The minimum-variance portfolio sits at its vertex with variance \(1/A\), and the maximum attainable Sharpe ratio is \(\sqrt{C}\). Derivations of Equation 9.3 through Equation 9.6 are in Appendix B.

9.1.2 Why the closed forms are not what we compute

Every expression above assumes weights may take any sign and any size. Real mandates do not permit that, and the moment a short-sale constraint \(\boldsymbol{w} \geq \boldsymbol{0}\) is added, the closed form disappears. Equation 9.2 becomes a quadratic program with inequality constraints, which has no analytic solution but is convex and therefore solvable to numerical precision in milliseconds (Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004). Everything computed in this chapter is a quadratic program of that kind; Section 13.1 is about the machinery.

There is a second, deeper reason the closed forms are a poor guide to practice. They treat \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) as known, when in application both are estimates, produced by the methods of Section 6.1 and Section 7.1 and carrying the errors those chapters measure. Jobson and Korkie (1980) showed how badly the resulting weights are estimated, and Best and Grauer (1991) traced the mechanism: because Equation 9.5 premultiplies \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) by \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\), small changes in estimated means produce large changes in weights, and the effect grows as the covariance matrix becomes more nearly singular. Chopra and Ziemba (1993) put a price on it, finding errors in expected returns roughly an order of magnitude more costly than errors in variances. Michaud (1989) named the consequence: an optimizer is an error-maximizer, systematically overweighting whatever the sample happened to flatter.

That literature frames the rest of this chapter. We compute the textbook answer in Section 9.2, and then in Section 9.3 we measure how much of it is real.

9.2 Implementation

9.2.1 Looking at the inputs first

Mean-variance consumes exactly two numbers per asset and one per pair. On this panel those numbers are lopsided, and it is worth seeing how lopsided before any optimizer runs.

ann_ret <- function(x) prod(1 + x)^(12 / length(x)) - 1
ann_vol <- function(x) stats::sd(x) * sqrt(12)

moments <- tibble::tibble(
  asset = colnames(R),
  mean_arith = apply(R, 2, mean) * 12,
  mean_comp  = apply(R, 2, ann_ret),
  vol        = apply(R, 2, ann_vol)
) |>
  dplyr::mutate(gap = mean_arith - mean_comp,      # between the two annual figures
                half_var = vol^2 / 2) |>           # the drag of chapter 3, per year
  dplyr::arrange(dplyr::desc(mean_comp))
stopifnot(all(moments$gap > 0), all(moments$gap < moments$half_var))

vol_spread <- max(moments$vol) / min(moments$vol)
mean_cor   <- mean(stats::cor(R)[upper.tri(stats::cor(R))])
c(vol_spread = vol_spread, mean_correlation = mean_cor)
#>       vol_spread mean_correlation 
#>           4.4141           0.3367

The most volatile series is 4.4 times the least, and the average pairwise correlation is 0.34. Both numbers constrain what the frontier can do, and Section 9.3 returns to them.

The table carries two definitions of an average return. The arithmetic mean is what enters \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\), because variance is defined around it; the compounded figure is what an investor actually earns over the period. Per period the two differ by the variance drag of Equation 3.3, half the variance. Between the two annualized figures of the table the gap is smaller than half the annual variance in every one of the 16 series, because twelve compounded months also earn return on return, which works in the other direction. For EU_equity, the series with the highest return, the gap is 1.42 percentage points a year against half a variance of 2.23. On the more volatile series it is not a rounding artifact on either count.

A useful diagnostic before optimizing is to ask how many assets could possibly be held. An asset is dominated in sample if some other asset offers a weakly higher mean at a weakly lower volatility.

# j dominates i if it has a weakly higher mean and a weakly lower volatility.
# A sample statement with no test attached: it says what happened, not what
# will happen.
dominated <- vapply(seq_len(nrow(moments)), function(i) {
  any(moments$mean_comp >= moments$mean_comp[i] &
      moments$vol       <= moments$vol[i] &
      seq_len(nrow(moments)) != i)
}, logical(1))

moments$asset[dominated]
#>  [1] "UK_equity"        "CH_equity"        "JP_equity"        "UK_credit"       
#>  [5] "CH_credit"        "EU_credit"        "GLOBAL_commodity" "CH_government"   
#>  [9] "UK_government"    "EU_government"    "JP_credit"        "JP_government"
n_dominating <- function(i) sum(moments$mean_comp >= moments$mean_comp[i] &
                                moments$vol       <= moments$vol[i]) - 1L
by_how_many <- setNames(vapply(seq_len(nrow(moments)), n_dominating, integer(1)),
                        moments$asset)
sort(by_how_many[by_how_many > 0], decreasing = TRUE)
#>        JP_credit        JP_equity GLOBAL_commodity    JP_government 
#>                5                4                4                4 
#>    EU_government        CH_equity        EU_credit    CH_government 
#>                3                2                2                2 
#>    UK_government        UK_equity        UK_credit        CH_credit 
#>                2                1                1                1

12 of 16 series are dominated, in the sense of Section 9.1, by at least one other, and JP_credit is dominated by 5 of the 15 alternatives. The efficient assets, the ones nothing dominates, are EU_equity, US_equity, US_credit, US_government. This is a statement about the sample and not a prediction, but it is visible without an optimizer, and a reader who cannot see it in Figure 9.1 will not see it in a weight vector either.

9.2.2 Specifying and solving the problem

PortfolioAnalytics separates the description of a problem from its solution. A portfolio.spec object collects the assets, the constraints and the objectives; optimize.portfolio() then hands that specification to a solver. The minimum-variance problem of Equation 9.3, with the short-sale constraint that removes its closed form, is four lines.

base_spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>   # weights sum to one
  add.constraint(type = "long_only")            # 0 <= w <= 1

min_var_spec <- base_spec |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")

w_gmv <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(Re, min_var_spec, optimize_method = "ROI"))
round(w_gmv[w_gmv > 0.005], 4)
#>        EU_equity GLOBAL_commodity        JP_equity        UK_equity 
#>           0.0138           0.0507           0.0415           0.0218 
#>        US_equity    US_government 
#>           0.0513           0.8209
S <- stats::cov(Re)
mu <- colMeans(Re)
p_vol <- function(w) sqrt(as.numeric(t(w) %*% S %*% w)) * sqrt(12)
p_ret <- function(w) sum(mu * w) * 12
p_sr  <- function(w) p_ret(w) / p_vol(w)

c(volatility = p_vol(w_gmv), excess_return = p_ret(w_gmv), sharpe = p_sr(w_gmv))
#>    volatility excess_return        sharpe 
#>       0.04133       0.03286       0.79513

The solution holds 6 of the sixteen series and places 82% of the money in US_government. This is not a subtle allocation, and it is worth being clear about why it happens. The least volatile series has an annualized volatility of 4.8% against 7.3% for the next; minimizing variance under a long-only constraint means buying the least volatile asset and adding only whatever reduces the total further.

Concentration of this kind is often read as evidence that the estimates are bad. Green and Hollifield (1992) argue the opposite: extreme weights are what mean-variance optimization produces whenever returns share a dominant common factor, whether or not the inputs are well estimated. Whether this universe has such a factor is a measured question rather than an assumption, and Section 5.2 answered it: one factor explains the four developed equity blocks and almost nothing else. Clarke et al. (2011) derive the composition of the minimum-variance portfolio explicitly and show that it holds few names by construction. The concentration here is a property of the problem, not a symptom.

9.2.3 The tangency portfolio

The maximum-Sharpe portfolio of Equation 9.5 is the point where a ray from the risk-free rate touches the frontier. PortfolioAnalytics offers maxSR = TRUE for it, and the callout below explains why this book does not use it. The portable route is to compute the frontier and read the best point off it.

ef_spec <- base_spec |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "StdDev")

ef <- create.EfficientFrontier(Re, ef_spec, type = "mean-StdDev", n.portfolios = 200)

# `ef$frontier` is a matrix carrying a class attribute, and `as.data.frame()`
# dispatches on that class: it returns every cell in a single column rather
# than one row of 19 per frontier portfolio. Strip the class first, or index it
# as the matrix it is.
fr <- as.data.frame(unclass(ef$frontier))
stopifnot(ncol(fr) == ncol(Re) + 3L)

# Returns are already in excess of the bill, so the Sharpe ratio is the ratio
# itself: no risk-free rate to subtract.
fr$sharpe <- fr$mean / fr$StdDev
i_tan <- which.max(fr$sharpe)

wcols <- grep("^w[.]", colnames(fr))
w_tan <- setNames(as.numeric(fr[i_tan, wcols]), sub("^w[.]", "", colnames(fr)[wcols]))
w_tan <- w_tan[colnames(Re)]
round(w_tan[w_tan > 0.005], 4)
#>        EU_equity GLOBAL_commodity        US_equity    US_government 
#>           0.0941           0.0311           0.1853           0.6853

Two things matter later. The first is in Table 9.2: the tangency portfolio has a Sharpe ratio 0.30 above that of a naive 60/40 allocation, a wide margin and an entirely in-sample one. The second is in the frontier the tangency portfolio was read from. The tangency portfolio holds 0.4% in US_credit, and that is where the series enters the frontier: to the left of the tangency point it is held at no more than that, and to the right its weight grows to 17.5%. US_credit is the series with the most negative skewness of the sixteen, at -1.45, and the asymmetry is the work of a single month: without its worst month the skewness is -0.10. The optimizer buys that asymmetry because variance cannot see it: Equation 9.2 uses only the first two moments. Changing the risk measure is not enough on its own, as Section 10.3 shows: a tail measure estimated from the historical returns does not see one month either, and only the moment-based estimate of Section 11.1 does. The example is not contrived. It fell out of the standard procedure applied to the standard inputs.

NoteWorked: the closed form of Equation 9.3 against the solver

Equation 9.3 is an explicit formula, so we can evaluate it and compare. The constants are those of Merton (1972).

iota <- rep(1, ncol(Re))
Sinv <- solve(S)

A <- as.numeric(t(iota) %*% Sinv %*% iota)
B <- as.numeric(t(iota) %*% Sinv %*% mu)
C <- as.numeric(t(mu)   %*% Sinv %*% mu)

w_gmv_cf <- setNames(as.numeric(Sinv %*% iota) / A, colnames(Re))
w_tan_cf <- setNames(as.numeric(Sinv %*% mu)   / B, colnames(Re))

round(sort(w_gmv_cf)[1:4], 4)      # the four most negative
#> UK_government     EU_credit     JP_credit     CH_credit 
#>       -0.8367       -0.6701       -0.4776       -0.1736

The closed form wants to short 5 of the sixteen series, most heavily UK_government at -83.7%. The solver was not allowed to, which is the whole of the difference between the two answers.

Two readings. The unconstrained portfolio genuinely has the lower volatility, at 3.95% against 4.13%, because a short position is a real diversification tool. And it reaches that number by holding 15 positions including 5 shorts, which is the behavior Michaud (1989) named and Jagannathan and Ma (2003) showed the constraint suppresses.

The vertex identity of Appendix B can be checked directly, and the maximum Sharpe ratio against \(\sqrt{C}\):

c(gmv_variance_from_A   = 1 / A,
  gmv_variance_measured = as.numeric(t(w_gmv_cf) %*% S %*% w_gmv_cf),
  max_sharpe_sqrt_C     = sqrt(C),

The first two agree to machine precision, which is the algebra working. The last two do not, and the gap is what the long-only constraint costs at the tangency: the unconstrained maximum Sharpe ratio is 11.2% higher than anything reachable without shorting.

9.2.4 Three ways this fails without an error

Each of the following produces an object that looks like a solved portfolio.

WarningThe solver most examples use is the wrong one

Published examples commonly reach for DEoptim. That advice predates the convex solvers in the package and is now not merely slow but wrong: minimum variance under a budget and a sign constraint is a quadratic program, and a differential-evolution search does not solve quadratic programs, it approximates them. Section 13.1 measures the approximation. On a problem with a known answer the metaheuristics stop at roughly two thirds to three quarters of the attainable Sharpe ratio after minutes of search, while ROI reaches all of it in seconds. Use ROI for a quadratic program, and reach for a metaheuristic only when the problem is genuinely non-convex.

The second failure concerns solver names. The modern incantation is optimize_method = c("CVXR", "OSQP"), and the obvious reading of that vector is wrong. Inside optimize.portfolio():

if (length(optimize_method) == 2)
  optimize_method <- optimize_method[2]
else optimize_method <- optimize_method[1]

The first element is discarded and dispatch happens on the second, matched against a hard-coded list of names.

This guard catches a second failure that has nothing to do with solver names. On the CRAN release of PortfolioAnalytics, an exact full_investment constraint is encoded as two opposing inequality rows, which are simultaneously active and linearly dependent. quadprog then reports the problem infeasible and ROI returns NA weights without raising a condition. Measured on this data, that fails in 30 of 312 rolling sixty-month minimum-variance windows, for a constraint set consisting of nothing but \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\iota} = 1\) and \(\boldsymbol{w} \geq \boldsymbol{0}\). The fix is recorded in Appendix D. Until it reaches CRAN, solve_checked() is what stands between the reader and a backtest with holes in it.

The third failure concerns return targets. A target can be attached to a constraint or to the return objective, and the backends disagree about what the second means.

Under ROI the objective’s target is honored. Under CVXR it is read only when optimize.portfolio() is called internally with ef = TRUE, which happens from meanvar.efficient.frontier() and nowhere else. Put the target on the constraint: it is the portable form, and it is the one that means what it says.

9.2.5 The package’s own frontier chart

chart.EfficientFrontier() draws the frontier, the assets, the capital market line and the tangency point in a single call, and it locates the tangency correctly, by taking the highest Sharpe ratio among the frontier points rather than by calling maxSR.

                        main = "", RAR.text = "Sharpe")

Two defaults are worth knowing. rf = 0 is the default, so without an argument the tangent is drawn to a zero rate and nothing is reported. That is harmless here, because the returns are already in excess of the bill, and wrong in most other settings. And match.col = "ES" is the default, so a mean-StdDev frontier drawn without match.col = "StdDev" searches for a column that does not exist.

9.3 What the Frontier Actually Buys

9.3.1 The frontier is a set of answers, not an object

There is no single problem whose solution is the efficient frontier. There are as many problems as there are points, each a separate quadratic program with its own target return, and the frontier is where their answers landed.

Everything a textbook draws on that picture can be produced from PortfolioAnalytics, and none of it is as obvious as the theory makes it look. The frontier comes from create.EfficientFrontier(), the tangency has to be read off it rather than requested, the quadratic-utility portfolios come from a risk objective carrying a risk_aversion argument, and the capital allocation line is not a function the package offers at all. We compute each here.

# Quadratic utility: maximize mu'w - lambda * w'Sigma w. In PortfolioAnalytics
# this is a return objective plus a risk objective carrying `risk_aversion`.
# Note that `risk_aversion` is gamma/2, not gamma -- see appendix B.
qu_weights <- function(lambda) {
  spec <- base_spec |>
    add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
    add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "var", risk_aversion = lambda)
  w <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(Re, spec, optimize_method = "ROI"))
  w[colnames(Re)]
}

Three things in Figure 9.3 are worth stating, because each is a place where the theory is easy and the implementation is not.

The quadratic-utility diamonds lie on the frontier rather than near it. That is the check that the objective was specified correctly: if risk_aversion is passed to the wrong objective, or the return objective is omitted, the solution still returns weights and still plots, but it lands off the curve.

The capital allocation line passes through the origin rather than through a risk-free rate on the vertical axis, because these returns are already in excess of the bill. Plotting total returns instead moves the intercept to the bill’s return and changes which frontier point is tangent. This is the single most common way the picture comes out wrong.

The tangency point is the one place the line touches the curve, and it was found by scanning the frontier for the highest Sharpe ratio rather than by asking for it. maxSR = TRUE exists and does not do this reliably, which is Section 9.2’s subject.

9.3.2 Reading the frontier through its weights

Figure 9.3 says where the answers landed. It does not say why, and the why is visible only in the weights. Each point on the frontier is a separate portfolio, so plotting the whole weight vector against the target return shows which assets enter, which leave, and where each one gives up.

Read Figure 9.4 left to right. At the minimum-variance end the portfolio is almost entirely US_government with small allocations elsewhere. Moving right, the government band shrinks, US_equity swells through the middle of the frontier to 65% at its widest, with US_credit beside it at up to 17%, and by the far right every position has been abandoned in favor of the single highest-return series, EU_equity. That last feature is not a quirk of this data: the right-hand endpoint of a long-only frontier is always a single asset, because maximizing return subject to a budget and no shorting has a corner solution.

9.3.3 The left end is stable and the right end is not

Equation 9.3 depends only on \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\); every point to the right of the vertex depends on \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) as well. Means are estimated far less precisely than variances from the same sample, a point Section 4.2 measured directly: the rolling mean wanders by more than its own level while volatility moves by roughly a third of its own. The theoretical consequence is Best and Grauer (1991); the practical one can be shown with a resampling experiment.

We draw the same thirty-one model years in twelve-month blocks, which preserves the serial dependence a naive bootstrap would destroy (Künsch 1989; Politis and Romano 1994), re-optimize on each draw, and measure how far the answer moves from the full-sample one.

set.seed(1602)

block_index <- function(n, block = 12L) {
  starts <- sample.int(n - block + 1L, ceiling(n / block), replace = TRUE)
  as.vector(outer(0:(block - 1L), starts, "+"))[seq_len(n)]
}

# Half the L1 distance between two long-only weight vectors: the share of the
# portfolio that must change hands to get from one to the other. The guard is
# not decoration -- given NULL, the unguarded arithmetic returns zero, and a
# failed solve would enter the sample as a portfolio that did not move.
turnover <- function(w1, w2) {
  if (is.null(w1) || is.null(w2)) stop("a failed solve is not a turnover of zero")
  sum(abs(w1[names(w2)] - w2)) / 2
}

B <- 200L
boot <- vapply(seq_len(B), function(b) {
  Rb <- xts::xts(zoo::coredata(Re)[block_index(nrow(Re)), ], order.by = zoo::index(Re))
  wg <- extractWeights(solve_checked(Rb, min_var_spec, optimize_method = "ROI"))
  fb <- as.data.frame(unclass(
    create.EfficientFrontier(Rb, ef_spec, type = "mean-StdDev",
                             n.portfolios = 60)$frontier))
  wt <- setNames(as.numeric(fb[which.max(fb$mean / fb$StdDev), wcols]),
                 sub("^w[.]", "", colnames(fb)[wcols]))
  c(gmv = turnover(wg[colnames(Re)], w_gmv),
    tangency = turnover(wt[colnames(Re)], w_tan))
}, numeric(2))

The tangency portfolio moves 3.4 times as far as the minimum-variance portfolio on identical data drawn a different way. In the worst twentieth of draws it asks for a trade of 53% of the portfolio, where the minimum-variance answer never exceeds 16% in any draw.

Nothing changed except the sample. The right end of the frontier is not more aggressive than the left; it is less well determined, which is a different kind of problem, because holding less of it does not make the estimate any better. It is also not free: Section 23.3 puts a price on that turnover, and the strategies that move most are the ones that pay most. This is Michaud (1989) made visible, and it is the argument that motivates the shrinkage methods of Section 15.1 and the robust formulations of Part VIII.

9.3.4 Diversification effort and diversification payoff are unrelated

It is tempting to attribute the minimum-variance portfolio’s low volatility to diversification. Mostly it is not diversification at all.

The cleanest way to see this is to run the same construction on two universes, one built to be heterogeneous and one built to be homogeneous. The sixteen classes span bonds, equities and commodities. The five equity classes are the same asset in five places.

Req <- Re[, grepl("_equity$", colnames(Re))]
Ceq <- stats::cor(Req)
Seq <- stats::cov(Req)

The count of effective bets above is the reciprocal of the Herfindahl index of the weights, the simplest of several measures of how concentrated a portfolio really is (Choueifaty and Coignard 2008; Meucci 2009).

The two universes could hardly be less alike. Average pairwise correlation is 0.34 across the classes and 0.55 within equities. What the optimizer does with them differs accordingly: across the classes it holds 1.47 effective positions out of sixteen, because 82% of the portfolio is US_government. Among equities, where no series has a low volatility, it has no such option and holds 3.49 of five.

One of those is barely a portfolio and the other is a real one. Now compare what each bought. Minimum variance lands 13.6% below the least volatile class series, and 11.4% below the least volatile equity market. The portfolio with 2.4 times the effective positions buys the smaller discount.

Both frontiers in Figure 9.6 terminate at the same point, because the highest-return asset in the mixed universe is an equity market and the optimizer at the right end holds nothing else. What the eleven non-equity series add is entirely at the other end: they extend the frontier leftward from 13.8% down to 4.1% of volatility. Nothing is added above. A floor is added below.

9.3.5 Five hundred universes, one constant

Two universes are suggestive and no more. If the minimum-variance level really is set by what is available rather than by how it combines, the pattern should hold on universes nobody designed.

set.seed(1602)

gmv_vol_of <- function(X) {
  w <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(X,
    portfolio.spec(colnames(X)) |>
      add.constraint("full_investment") |> add.constraint("long_only") |>
      add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var"),
    optimize_method = "ROI"))
  sqrt(as.numeric(t(w) %*% stats::cov(X) %*% w)) * sqrt(12)
}

  })

Across 521 sub-universes drawn from the same sixteen series, the median portfolio lands 12.9% below its own least volatile constituent; the smallest discount is 1% and the largest is 29%. The size of the discount depends on the selection, then. Where the level comes from does not.

#>           0.97673           0.05067           0.98840

The volatility of the least volatile available asset explains 98% of the variation in the minimum-variance level. Average correlation, which is what the word diversification refers to, explains 5%. Both together with the size of the universe reach 99%.

This deserves stating plainly, because the claim that diversification is the only free lunch is usually offered as though the combining did the work. On this evidence it does not. Choosing the universe fixes the answer to within the discount, typically 13% and never more than 29% here, and the optimizer then allocates inside a room whose ceiling was fixed before it was called. That is the first argument in this book for spending effort on which assets are eligible rather than on the machinery that weights them, and Section 15.1 returns to it.

9.3.6 What each constraint costs

A mandate is a sequence of restrictions, and each one moves the frontier south-east. Running the same problem four times makes the cost of each visible rather than asserted.

  grepl("_government$", a) ~ "government",
  grepl("_credit$", a)     ~ "credit",
  TRUE                     ~ "commodity")
# split() returns its groups in alphabetical order, so the limit vectors below
# are built by name rather than positionally. Getting that wrong applies the
# equity limit to the commodity and nothing complains.
grp <- split(seq_len(ncol(Re)), group_of(colnames(Re)))
gmin <- c(commodity = 0.00, credit = 0.05, equity = 0.20, government = 0.10)
gmax <- c(commodity = 0.15, credit = 0.40, equity = 0.60, government = 0.50)
stopifnot(identical(names(grp), names(gmin)))

spec_unconstrained <- portfolio.spec(colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "box", min = -1, max = 1) |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "StdDev")

spec_box <- base_spec |>
  add.constraint(type = "box", min = 0, max = 0.25) |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "StdDev")

spec_group <- base_spec |>
  add.constraint(type = "box", min = 0, max = 0.25) |>
  add.constraint(type = "group", groups = grp,
                 group_min = unname(gmin[names(grp)]),
                 group_max = unname(gmax[names(grp)])) |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "StdDev")

ladder <- list(`1 no short-sale limit` = spec_unconstrained,
               `2 long only`           = ef_spec,
               `3 plus a 25% cap`      = spec_box,
               `4 plus group limits`   = spec_group)

fronts <- lapply(names(ladder), function(nm) {
  e <- create.EfficientFrontier(Re, ladder[[nm]], type = "mean-StdDev",
                                n.portfolios = 100)
  f <- as.data.frame(unclass(e$frontier))
  data.frame(spec = nm, vol = f$StdDev * sqrt(12), ret = f$mean * 12,
             sharpe = f$mean / f$StdDev)
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

The ordering in Table 9.4 is mechanical and the magnitudes are not. Removing the short-sale limit buys a genuinely lower left end, because a short position hedges. The 25 percent cap costs at both ends, because both ends of the long-only frontier were close to a single position. The lowest reachable volatility rises from 4.13% to 5.26%, since the minimum-variance portfolio held 82% in one series, and the highest reachable return falls from 13.41% to 11.08%, since the corner solution of Figure 9.4 is no longer reachable. The group limits then add little at the left end, where the lowest reachable volatility becomes 5.38%, and nothing at the best Sharpe ratio, which stays at 0.908. They bind at the right end, where the capped frontier ended in equities alone and the group ceiling allows them 60 percent: the highest reachable return falls again, to 8.66%.

That last observation is the argument of Section 12.1 in one picture. A constraint that binds only where the unconstrained answer was absurd costs little and buys a portfolio someone will actually hold, which is the Jagannathan and Ma (2003) result seen from the mandate’s side rather than the estimator’s.

9.3.7 And it does not survive contact with the future

Everything above is in sample. Running the same portfolios out of sample, with a sixty-month rolling estimate and monthly rebalancing, takes the tangency portfolio from first place to last. The out-of-sample column is read from the register that Section 25.3 computes, rather than recomputed here, so that the two chapters cannot drift apart.

The tangency portfolio keeps 55% of the Sharpe ratio it promised and finishes last of the three. Minimum variance, which needed no estimate of \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) at all, keeps 104%, and equal weighting, which promised the least, keeps 102%; a share above 100% is possible because the out-of-sample record covers model years 11 to 31 rather than the whole sample. Two rules deliver what the full sample said they would, and the one that estimates the mean does not.

The order of the three depends on the estimation window. With 120 months behind each estimate instead of sixty, the tangency portfolio reaches a Sharpe ratio of 0.70 and finishes ahead of equal weighting at 0.63, though behind minimum variance at 0.91. What does not depend on the window, or on how often the portfolio is rebalanced, is the following. In every cell of the register, with windows of 36, 60 and 120 months and monthly, quarterly and annual rebalancing, minimum variance has the highest Sharpe ratio of the three, and the tangency portfolio keeps the smallest share of what it promised, between 35% and 76%. This is the pattern DeMiguel et al. (2009) report for fourteen rules on seven datasets: none was consistently better than equal weighting out of sample, the failure was attributable to estimation error rather than to any defect in Equation 9.2 itself, and the estimation window a mean-based rule needs before it does better is long, which is the direction of the 120-month result here. Jagannathan and Ma (2003) explain why minimum variance is the exception: dropping the mean removes the input that is estimated worst.

At the sixty-month window the portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio in this chapter has the lowest of the 5 strategies in the register of Section 25.3. The gap between the two columns of Table 9.5 is the subject of the rest of the book.

Key Takeaways

  • Mean-variance optimization has a closed-form solution only when weights are unconstrained. Adding a short-sale constraint turns Equation 9.2 into a quadratic program, which is what every result in this chapter actually solves.
  • The minimum-variance portfolio of Equation 9.3 does not involve expected returns. That makes it the one point on the frontier requiring no return forecast, and it is why it is far more stable than the tangency portfolio.
  • Concentration is not automatically a symptom of bad estimates. A dominant common factor produces concentrated efficient portfolios even with perfect inputs (Green and Hollifield 1992; Clarke et al. 2011).
  • The left end of the frontier is determined by \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) and the right end by \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\), which is estimated far less precisely. A block bootstrap makes the tangency portfolio move several times as far as the minimum-variance portfolio on the same data.
  • The level of minimum variance is set mainly by the least volatile asset available, not by how assets combine. Universe selection matters more than the weighting machinery applied afterwards.
  • In sample the tangency portfolio has the highest Sharpe ratio by construction; out of sample it keeps the smallest share of what it promised at every estimation window. It finishes behind minimum variance throughout, and behind equal weighting at the sixty-month window though not at 120 months (DeMiguel et al. 2009; Jagannathan and Ma 2003).
