10  Risk Measures Beyond Variance

Every optimizer in Section 9.1 measured risk by the variance of the portfolio, and nothing in the data required it: Markowitz (1959) himself proposed the semi-variance, which counts only the returns below the mean, as the measure an investor who fears losses rather than dispersion should prefer. Artzner et al. (1999) turned the question around and asked which properties a number must have before it can be called a measure of risk at all, and found that the industry’s standard, value at risk, lacks the one that matters most for building portfolios. This chapter asks what changes when variance is replaced by a measure that has that property.

We first set out the coherence axioms, value at risk and why it fails them, expected shortfall and the linear program that makes it as easy to optimize as variance, its Cornish-Fisher extension, and the downside and drawdown measures that sit beside them; then show which of these PortfolioAnalytics can solve, with which solver, and where its defaults do something other than what the specification says; then solve one long-only problem on the sixteen class series under four objectives and read what each bought. The allocation is strategic in the sense of Section 3.1: full-sample moments, no timing, and the out-of-sample check read from the register of Section 25.3 rather than recomputed.

The data are the sixteen risky class series of Section 2.2, in excess of the Treasury bill for the reasons of Section 3.1, loaded as Section 9.1 loads them.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)   # sixteen risky classes
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return                                  # the bill
Re <- R - rf                                                       # excess returns
stopifnot(ncol(Re) == 16L, !anyNA(Re), length(rf) == nrow(R))

10.1 Coherence, Tails, and What Variance Assumes

10.1.1 Four properties of a risk measure

Write \(X\) for the random excess return of a position over one period and \(\rho(X)\) for the number a risk measure assigns to it, in the units of a loss. Artzner et al. (1999) call \(\rho\) coherent if it satisfies four axioms: monotonicity, so that a position which pays at least as much as another in every state is not riskier; translation invariance, so that adding a sure amount \(c\) reduces the measured risk by exactly \(c\); positive homogeneity, so that \(\rho(\lambda X) = \lambda\rho(X)\) for \(\lambda > 0\); and subadditivity,

\[ \rho(X + Y) \;\leq\; \rho(X) + \rho(Y). \tag{10.1}\]

The last axiom is the one that matters here. It says that combining two positions can never make the measured risk larger than the sum of the two measured separately, which is what diversification means; a measure that violates it can rank a diversified portfolio as riskier than its concentrated parts, and an optimizer built on it will act on that ranking. Variance is not coherent, since it scales with \(\lambda^2\) rather than \(\lambda\), and the standard deviation fails monotonicity. They are used anyway because, when returns are elliptical, every translation-invariant and positively homogeneous measure is an increasing function of the standard deviation at a given mean (McNeil et al. 2015), so the choice of measure changes no ranking. Whether returns are elliptical is a question Section 4.2 answered for this market: they are not.

10.1.2 Value at risk

The value at risk at level \(\alpha\) is the loss that is exceeded with probability \(\alpha\): writing \(q_\alpha(X)\) for the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the return,

\[ \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(X) = -\,q_\alpha(X), \tag{10.2}\]

with \(\alpha = 0.05\) for the 95% level that this chapter uses throughout, and which PortfolioAnalytics writes as p = 0.95. It is the regulatory standard of Jorion (2007); it is monotone, translation invariant and homogeneous; and it fails Equation 10.1, not only in contrived examples: a quantile ignores everything beyond itself, so two positions whose losses each sit inside the 5% tail can, combined, produce a loss outside it.

NoteWorked: where value at risk fails subadditivity on this data

Equation 10.1 can be tested on every pair of the sixteen series. For an equally weighted pair, homogeneity turns the axiom into \(\rho\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}X + \tfrac{1}{2}Y\right) \leq \tfrac{1}{2}\rho(X) + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho(Y)\), which is one comparison per pair. PerformanceAnalytics reports both as negative numbers by default; invert = FALSE returns the loss as a positive one, the convention of Equation 10.2.

loss_var <- function(x) as.numeric(VaR(x, p = 0.95, method = "historical", invert = FALSE))
loss_es  <- function(x) as.numeric(ES(x,  p = 0.95, method = "historical", invert = FALSE))

pairs <- utils::combn(colnames(Re), 2)

Value at risk fails on 3 of the 120 pairs, CH_credit with CH_government, EU_credit with EU_government, and US_credit with US_government, each a credit series combined with the government bond of its own currency. 10.1.3 Expected shortfall and the linear program

The expected shortfall at level \(\alpha\) is the expected loss given that the loss exceeds the value at risk, Equation 4.5 of Section 4.1, also called conditional value at risk and expected tail loss; PortfolioAnalytics accepts all three names. It is coherent (Acerbi and Tasche 2002) and the smallest coherent measure that dominates value at risk at the same level, which is why it is the tail measure this book uses.

What made expected shortfall usable in an optimizer is a representation due to Rockafellar and Uryasev (2000). For a portfolio \(\boldsymbol{w}\) with return \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{r}\),

\[ \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(\boldsymbol{w}) = \min_{\zeta \in \mathbb{R}} \left\{\, \zeta + \frac{1}{\alpha}\, \mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(-\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{r} - \zeta\right)_+\right] \right\}, \tag{10.3}\]

where \((x)_+ = \max(x, 0)\), and the minimizing \(\zeta\) is the value at risk. The expression inside the braces is convex in \((\boldsymbol{w}, \zeta)\) jointly, so minimizing it over both at once minimizes the expected shortfall over \(\boldsymbol{w}\) without ever computing a quantile. With the expectation replaced by the average over \(T\) observed return vectors \(\boldsymbol{r}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{r}_T\) and one auxiliary variable \(z_t\) per observation, the minimum-ES problem under full investment and no short sales is the linear program

\[ \min_{\boldsymbol{w},\,\zeta,\,\boldsymbol{z}} \; \zeta + \frac{1}{\alpha T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} z_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad z_t \geq -\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{r}_t - \zeta,\; z_t \geq 0,\; \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\iota} = 1,\; \boldsymbol{w} \geq \boldsymbol{0}, \tag{10.4}\]

with \(N + T + 1\) variables, here 389, and one inequality per month. Rockafellar and Uryasev (2002) extend the representation to distributions with atoms, which the empirical distribution is. Every constraint in Equation 10.4 is linear, so the problem is convex and a local minimum is the global one (Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004), as for the quadratic program of Equation 9.2; Section 13.1 is about that distinction. A mean-ES frontier adds the row \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} \geq \bar{\mu}\) and traces \(\bar{\mu}\), and the ratio of mean excess return to expected shortfall, which the package calls STARR, is the tail analog of the Sharpe ratio, maximized by the same normalization that turns the maximum-Sharpe problem into a quadratic program in Section 13.1.

NoteWorked: the expected shortfall of US_credit by hand

The sample version of Equation 4.5 is an average over the tail. With \(T\) months and \(\alpha = 0.05\) the tail holds the \(\alpha T\) worst observations, which is not an integer, and PerformanceAnalytics averages every return strictly below the \(\alpha\)-quantile.

x  <- as.numeric(Re[, "US_credit"])
q  <- stats::quantile(x, 0.05)              # the quantile; its negative is the VaR
tail_months <- x[x < q]
es_hand <- -mean(tail_months)

stopifnot(all.equal(es_hand, loss_es(x)))
c(months = length(x), alpha_T = 0.05 * length(x), in_tail = length(tail_months),
  VaR = as.numeric(-q), ES = es_hand,
  ES_modified = as.numeric(ES(x, p = 0.95, method = "modified", invert = FALSE)))
#>      months     alpha_T     in_tail         VaR          ES ES_modified 
#>   372.00000    18.60000    19.00000     0.02509     0.04470     0.09405

The 19 months below the quantile run from -16.4% to -2.5%, and

\[ \widehat{\mathrm{ES}}_{0.05} = -\frac{1}{19}\sum_{t \in \text{tail}} r_t = -\frac{-0.8493}{19} = 0.0447, \]

against a value at risk of 0.0251: the average month in the tail loses 1.8 times the loss at its edge. The Cornish-Fisher figure of 0.0941 is the estimate Section 4.2 reported as roughly twice the historical one, and the two disagree because the excess-return skewness of -1.36 pushes the expansion further out than 19 observed months reach.

10.1.4 The modified expected shortfall

The historical estimator uses \(\alpha T\) observations, 18.6 of them here, and Yamai and Yoshiba (2005) show that expected shortfall is estimated with a larger standard error than value at risk from the same sample precisely because it depends on the few observations beyond the quantile. The alternative of Favre and Galeano (2002) and Boudt et al. (2008) replaces the sample tail by a normal quantile corrected for skewness and kurtosis through the Cornish-Fisher expansion, Equation 4.6, and the resulting modified expected shortfall is a function of the portfolio’s first four moments. Each is a polynomial in \(\boldsymbol{w}\) through the coskewness and cokurtosis tensors that Section 11.1 sets out, so the modified measure responds to the asymmetry of every asset and every pair, and Boudt et al. (2008) also decompose it into the contribution of each position, which is the quantity Section 14.1 budgets. But it is not convex in \(\boldsymbol{w}\), and no linear program computes its minimum. PortfolioAnalytics reaches it by search, which Section 10.2 shows and Section 13.3 measures. One default from Section 4.2 carries over: ES() in PerformanceAnalytics returns this modified figure unless method = "historical" is asked for.

10.1.5 Downside measures, drawdowns, and two more coherent measures

Three further families deserve a place. The oldest is the safety-first criterion of Roy (1952), which minimizes the probability of falling below a disaster level, and the semi-variance of Markowitz (1959), which Bawa and Lindenberg (1977) generalized to the lower partial moment of order \(n\) around a target \(\tau\),

\[ \mathrm{LPM}_n(\tau) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\tau - \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{r}\right)_+^{\,n}\right], \tag{10.5}\]

whose square root at \(n = 2\) is the downside deviation and the denominator of the ratio of Sortino and Meer (1991). Drawdown measures ask about the path rather than the distribution: Chekhlov et al. (2005) define the conditional drawdown at risk as the average of the worst \(\alpha\) share of drawdowns and show that it, too, is minimized by a linear program of the form of Equation 10.4, with drawdowns in place of losses. The third family is the one Krokhmal (2007) built by replacing the average in Equation 10.3 with a higher norm. At the second order,

\[ \mathrm{CSM}_\alpha(\boldsymbol{w}) = \min_{\zeta} \left\{\, \zeta + \frac{1}{\alpha}\, \sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(-\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{r} - \zeta\right)_+^{2}\right]} \right\}, \tag{10.6}\]

which is coherent, penalizes the shape of the tail beyond its average, and is a second-order cone program rather than a linear one; PortfolioAnalytics names it CSM. The package also offers EQS, an “expected quadratic shortfall” documented only by its code, which minimizes over \(\zeta\) the expression of Equation 10.3 with a squared positive part in place of the loss; which tail it squares is a question Section 10.2 measures rather than assumes.

10.1.6 What the choice of measure assumes

Each measure encodes a belief about what the investor of Section 3.1 dislikes. Variance treats a gain of ten percent and a loss of ten percent as the same event, and by Equation 3.12 that is right only if utility is quadratic or every higher moment is a function of the first two, the two cases Markowitz (1959) identified; Levy and Markowitz (1979) and Markowitz (2014) find the approximation good across most utility functions and return histories, and poor where returns are far from normal. A tail measure asks about one side of the distribution only, and so responds to skewness and kurtosis, which Section 4.3 showed to live in credit rather than in equity: US_credit has the third-best Sharpe ratio of the sixteen and a total-return skewness of -1.45, the most negative of them. Markets appear to price downside risk separately from variance (Ang et al. 2006), so the choice is not only a matter of taste; and it has a cost in estimation, since the historical tail uses a twentieth of the sample and the modified one inherits the sample skewness, which Section 4.2 found to change sign in a third of rolling windows. Section 11.1 takes the comoments as inputs; here they enter only through the measure.

One rule from Section 3.1 governs what follows: the measure a portfolio is judged by must be the measure it was chosen by, and a comparison across objectives has to show every portfolio on every measure so that the mismatch is visible. Section 10.3 does that.

10.2 Implementation

10.2.1 The objectives the package knows

A risk objective in PortfolioAnalytics is named for the function that computes it. "var" or "StdDev" is the variance of Equation 9.1; "ES", with the aliases "ETL" and "CVaR", is Equation 4.5, taking its level and estimator as arguments = list(p = 0.95, method = "historical"); "VaR" is Equation 10.2, which no convex solver accepts, since a quantile is not convex in the weights, so it is reachable only through the search methods of Section 13.1; "CSM" and "EQS" are solved by CVXR only. Four specifications on one constraint set carry the chapter: minimum variance, minimum historical expected shortfall, quadratic utility at risk_aversion = 2, which by the \(\gamma/2\) convention of Section B.5 is \(\gamma = 4\), and minimum modified expected shortfall.

base_spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only")

spec_var <- base_spec |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")
spec_es  <- base_spec |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "ES",
                                       arguments = list(p = 0.95, method = "historical"))
spec_mes <- base_spec |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "ES",
                                       arguments = list(p = 0.95, method = "modified"))
spec_qu  <- base_spec |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "var", risk_aversion = 2)   # lambda = 2, i.e. gamma = 4

The first three are convex and go to ROI: a quadratic program for the two variance objectives, as in Section 9.2, and the linear program of Equation 10.4 for the shortfall.

w_of <- function(opt) extractWeights(opt)[colnames(Re)]      # always index by name

o_var <- optimize.portfolio(Re, spec_var, optimize_method = "ROI")
o_es  <- optimize.portfolio(Re, spec_es,  optimize_method = "ROI")
o_qu  <- optimize.portfolio(Re, spec_qu,  optimize_method = "ROI")
w_var <- w_of(o_var); w_es <- w_of(o_es); w_qu <- w_of(o_qu)
stopifnot(abs(sum(w_es) - 1) < 1e-6, all(w_es >= -1e-8))

W3   <- rbind(`minimum variance` = w_var, `minimum ES` = w_es, `quadratic utility` = w_qu)
keep <- colnames(Re)[apply(W3, 2, max) > 0.005]
round(W3[, keep], 3)
#>                   EU_equity GLOBAL_commodity JP_equity JP_government UK_equity

The risk numbers come back two ways. extractObjectiveMeasures() reports what the solver minimized, and extract_risk() recomputes the mean, the standard deviation and all three tail measures for any weight vector, so a portfolio chosen by one measure can be read on the others.

The two ES figures agree, and both are the value of Equation 10.4, which with \(\alpha T = 18.6\) interpolates between the eighteenth and nineteenth worst months, rather than the sample-tail average of ES(); the two differ at the fourth decimal, 0.0239 against 0.0238.

NoteUnder ROI the method argument is not read

The linear program of Equation 10.4 is the sample formulation, and it is the only formulation ROI has. Asking for method = "modified" on an ES objective and then solving with ROI returns the historical solution without a message.

o_mes_roi <- optimize.portfolio(Re, spec_mes, optimize_method = "ROI")
c(same_weights_as_historical = isTRUE(all.equal(w_of(o_mes_roi), w_es)))
#> same_weights_as_historical 
#>                       TRUE

The modified measure needs a solver that evaluates the objective as a black box, which means differential evolution (Storn and Price 1997; Ardia et al. 2011), particle swarm, simulated annealing or random portfolios, none of which carries an optimality guarantee. We use differential evolution with a small budget, and Section 10.3 shows what the budget bought.

  US_credit = w_mes[["US_credit"]], positions = sum(w_mes > 0.005),

#> modified_ES   US_credit   positions     seconds 
#>     0.02546     0.01200     9.00000    19.90000
WarningA mean plus a risk objective is a ratio problem by default, but only for the tail measures

optimize.portfolio() decides what one return objective plus one risk objective means, and decides differently for different measures. With mean and StdDev the default maxSR = FALSE makes it a quadratic-utility problem at the default risk_aversion = 1, and the tangency portfolio needs maxSR = TRUE. With mean and ES the default is maxSTARR = TRUE, as it is for CSMratio and EQSratio: the package maximizes the ratio unless told not to, and maxSTARR = FALSE turns the specification into a minimum-ES problem in which the return objective is merely reported.

spec_mean_es <- base_spec |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "ES", arguments = list(p = 0.95))
spec_mean_sd <- base_spec |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "StdDev")

starr  <- function(w) { x <- as.numeric(Re %*% w); mean(x) / loss_es(x) }
sharpe <- function(w) { x <- as.numeric(Re %*% w); mean(x) / stats::sd(x) }

asked <- list(
  `mean + ES, defaults`         = optimize.portfolio(Re, spec_mean_es, optimize_method = "ROI"),
  `mean + ES, maxSTARR = FALSE` = optimize.portfolio(Re, spec_mean_es, optimize_method = "ROI",
                                                     maxSTARR = FALSE),
  `mean + StdDev, defaults`     = optimize.portfolio(Re, spec_mean_sd, optimize_method = "ROI"),
  `mean + StdDev, maxSR = TRUE` = optimize.portfolio(Re, spec_mean_sd, optimize_method = "ROI",
                                                     maxSR = TRUE)
)

# What each default actually solved, checked rather than asserted.
w_qu1 <- w_of(optimize.portfolio(Re, base_spec |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "var", risk_aversion = 1), optimize_method = "ROI"))
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(w_of(asked[[2]]), w_es,  tolerance = 1e-4)),
          isTRUE(all.equal(w_of(asked[[3]]), w_qu1, tolerance = 1e-4)))

ratios <- tibble::tibble(
  specification = names(asked),
  solves  = c("maximum STARR", "minimum ES", "quadratic utility, risk_aversion = 1",
              "maximum Sharpe"),
  STARR   = vapply(asked, function(o) starr(w_of(o)),  numeric(1)),
  Sharpe  = vapply(asked, function(o) sharpe(w_of(o)), numeric(1)),

The two default rows of Table 10.1 have 27% of their weight in common: one maximizes a tail ratio and holds mostly US_government, the other a utility whose risk penalty is almost switched off and holds only equity, EU_equity first. Nothing in the returned object says which was solved beyond the name of the objective measure. State the ratio arguments explicitly in every call, in both directions.

10.2.2 The mean-ES frontier

create.EfficientFrontier() with type = "mean-ETL" calls meanetl.efficient.frontier(), which solves Equation 10.4 for the minimum-ES portfolio, for the maximum return, and then for n.portfolios return targets between them. chart.EfficientFrontier() draws it in one call, its default match.col = "ES" locating the horizontal axis; the mean-variance frontier of Section 9.2 is computed beside it on the same constraints.

                                  n.portfolios = 50, optimize_method = "ROI")
ef_sd <- create.EfficientFrontier(Re, spec_mean_sd, type = "mean-StdDev",
                                  n.portfolios = 50, optimize_method = "ROI")

# `unclass()` before `as.data.frame()`, for the reason given in chapter 9.

fr_sd <- as.data.frame(unclass(ef_sd$frontier))

# The direct call returns the same matrix, without the wrapper object.

                                        n.portfolios = 10)
c(es_points = nrow(fr_es), sd_points = nrow(fr_sd), direct_points = nrow(fr_direct),
  min_ES_on_frontier = fr_es$ES[1], min_ES_solved = extractObjectiveMeasures(o_es)$ES)

Two frontiers under two measures are compared on a common axis, and there are two of them. Every coordinate goes into one data frame first, one row per frontier portfolio and per asset, with the realized volatility and expected shortfall of each, so that a point cannot be paired with another portfolio’s return.

weights_of <- function(fr) {
  W <- as.matrix(fr[, grep("^w[.]", colnames(fr))])
  colnames(W) <- sub("^w[.]", "", colnames(W))
  stopifnot(setequal(colnames(W), colnames(Re)))
  W[, colnames(Re)]
}

The skewness of US_credit is the work of one month, October of model year 17 in the credit crisis, a return 8.0 standard deviations below the mean: without it the excess-return skewness is -0.03 rather than -1.36. A 5% tail averages that month with 18 others, so the historical expected shortfall of the series is 2.13 times its standard deviation, rank 8 of the sixteen, where the Cornish-Fisher figure, which is built from the skewness, is 4.49 times, rank 2. The frontier holdings that differ by so much are separated by the few hundredths of a percentage point of risk reported above, which is the statement of McNeil et al. (2015) made on sixteen assets: series that are close to elliptical are ranked the same way by both measures, and the weights on which the two frontiers disagree are weakly determined by either.

ImportantCSM is unavailable to the search methods

Every objective a metaheuristic evaluates passes through constrained_objective(), and that function’s branch for CSM is empty: it sets no function, so the objective contributes nothing to the score (upstream issue #51, recorded in Appendix D). A random-portfolio or differential-evolution run on a CSM objective does not fail; it scores every candidate at zero and returns the first one it looked at.

ImportantTwo things to check before trusting an EQS number

The ratio problems are solved in a normalized form, mean fixed at one and box and group limits scaled by the weight sum, and the weights are rescaled to sum to one afterwards. Earlier builds skipped the rescaling, so that a box or group limit on a ratio problem did not bind in the returned weights; on the build recorded in Appendix D it does, and we measure it rather than assume it. What does not survive the rescaling is the objective value: expected shortfall and CSM are homogeneous and divide through, while EQS is quadratic in the weights, so the figure reported for a ratio solution is not the EQS of the weights reported with it.

The second check concerns the measure itself. In both optimize.portfolio() and extract_risk() the quantity squared is the positive part of the portfolio return, where the ES and CSM branches of the same functions take the positive part of its negative. The number the package calls EQS is therefore the tail average of squared gains, and only on negated returns does it become the tail average of squared losses.

# The empirical version of the minimization in @eq-10-ru for a nonnegative
# series v: the average of its largest alpha share, interpolated at the edge.
tail_mean <- function(v, alpha = 0.05) {
  v <- sort(v, decreasing = TRUE); m <- alpha * length(v); k <- floor(m)
  (sum(v[seq_len(k)]) + (m - k) * v[k + 1]) / m
}
r_es <- as.numeric(Re %*% w_es)
eqs_check <- c(package_EQS            = extract_risk(Re, w_es)$EQS,
               tail_of_squared_gains  = tail_mean(pmax(r_es, 0)^2),
               tail_of_squared_losses = tail_mean(pmax(-r_es, 0)^2),
               package_EQS_on_negated = extract_risk(-Re, w_es)$EQS)
stopifnot(all.equal(eqs_check[["package_EQS"]], eqs_check[["tail_of_squared_gains"]],
                    tolerance = 1e-3))
signif(eqs_check, 4)
#>            package_EQS  tail_of_squared_gains tail_of_squared_losses 
#>              0.0008126              0.0008126              0.0006052 
#> package_EQS_on_negated 
#>              0.0006052

skew_of <- function(w) as.numeric(skewness(as.numeric(Re %*% w)))
stopifnot(skew_of(w_eqs) < skew_of(w_es))        # the order the next sentence states

Minimizing the package’s EQS therefore penalizes large gains, which is why its minimum-risk portfolio above has a more negative skewness than the minimum-ES one, -0.44 against -0.10. Until the sign is corrected upstream, an EQS objective should be posed on -Re, and every EQS figure read from the returned weights rather than from the returned object.

10.3 Four Objectives on One Universe

10.3.1 What each objective bought

The four portfolios of Section 10.2 solve the same long-only, fully invested problem and differ only in what they call risk. Each is reported here on every measure, with the tail measures computed on the excess returns that were optimized and the drawdown on the total-return wealth path.

portfolios <- list(`minimum variance` = w_var,
                   `minimum ES` = w_es,
                   `quadratic utility, lambda = 2` = w_qu,
                   `minimum modified ES (search)` = w_mes)

measures_of <- function(w) {
  x  <- as.numeric(Re %*% w)                                       # excess, what was optimized
  xt <- xts::xts(as.numeric(R %*% w), order.by = zoo::index(R))    # total, for the wealth path
  c(US_credit = w[["US_credit"]], positions = sum(w > 0.005),
    ret = mean(x) * 12, vol = stats::sd(x) * sqrt(12),
    ES = loss_es(x),
    modified_ES = as.numeric(ES(x, p = 0.95, method = "modified", invert = FALSE)),
    utility = (mean(x) - 2 * stats::var(x)) * 12,                   # mu - lambda sigma^2, lambda = 2
    skewness = as.numeric(skewness(x)),
    max_drawdown = as.numeric(maxDrawdown(xt)))
}

Read Table 10.2 down the US_credit column first. The quadratic-utility investor holds 9.8% of the portfolio in the most negatively skewed series in this market, for the reason Section 9.3 gave: a mean-based objective pays for the third-best Sharpe ratio of the sixteen, and variance cannot see what it is buying. The three objectives that carry no mean hold at most 1.2% of it. The skewness column shows what a return objective brings with it: the quadratic-utility portfolio, the rest of which is equity, has a skewness of -0.78 and a drawdown of 44%, while the minimum-ES portfolio, at -0.10 and 12%, is the closest to symmetric of the three convex solutions, and it is 0.15 units of skewness less negative than the minimum-variance portfolio, which holds no credit either. The two agree on their largest position, US_government at 82% and 85%, and on whether to hold 14 of the sixteen series at all: the minimum-ES portfolio gives up EU_equity, with a total-return skewness of -0.59, for JP_government, at 0.61.

The last row is the search’s answer, and the modified-ES column reads it against the others. The differential-evolution portfolio reports a modified expected shortfall of 2.55%, above the 2.41% that the minimum ES portfolio reaches on the same measure without having been asked to. The search did not find its own minimum, which is the behavior Section 13.3 measures on a problem with a known answer, and the reason this chapter reports the convex solutions as the result and the searched one as an illustration.

10.3.2 Each portfolio on every measure

The rule of Section 3.1 says that each portfolio must be reported by the measure it optimized. Table 10.3 does that on the diagonal and shows everything else beside it.

Three of the four bold cells carry the asterisk: the convex solvers returned, for each measure, the portfolio that is best on it, which is what a solved problem means. The fourth does not, and its column is won by minimum ES. The table also prices each choice in the other measures, and on this universe the price is small: the minimum-ES portfolio gives up 0.08 percentage points of annualized volatility against the minimum-variance portfolio and gains 0.07 percentage points of monthly expected shortfall. The tail measure exchanged one holding for another and moved one moment, and that is the honest size of the effect on sixteen class series and 372 months.

10.3.3 Out of sample, read from the register

Everything above uses full-sample moments. The register of Section 25.3 runs the same two risk-only rules on rolling windows, re-estimated monthly, and this chapter reads its lines rather than recomputing them, so that the two chapters cannot disagree.

oos <- utils::read.csv("output/tables/oos-sweep.csv") |>
  dplyr::filter(universe == "classes", frequency == "months", basis == "gross",
                strategy %in% c("min_variance", "min_cvar")) |>
  dplyr::arrange(window, strategy)
stopifnot(nrow(oos) == 6L)
pick <- function(w, s, col) oos[[col]][oos$window == w & oos$strategy == s]

On the sixty-month window the minimum-ES rule realizes an expected shortfall of 2.72% against 2.56% for minimum variance, a difference of 0.16 percentage points in favor of the rule that did not optimize it, and the sign is the same at all three windows. Matching the metric to the objective is not the same as winning on it: a 5% tail estimated from sixty months is 3 observations, the portfolio built on them inherits their sampling error, and Section 26.1 puts that cost in units. Where the minimum-ES rule does differ is in the moments it was not asked about, a skewness of -0.15 against -0.55 and a Sharpe ratio of 0.893 against 0.829, and neither difference survives a longer window: at 120 months minimum variance has the higher Sharpe ratio, 0.905 against 0.728, and the less negative skewness.

The thirty-six-month row is a warning rather than a finding. There the minimum-ES rule posts a Sharpe ratio of 0.944, the highest in Table 10.4, from portfolios each built on a 5% tail of 1.8 observations. A tail of two months is not an estimate of a distribution; it is two months. Section 25.3 puts the error bar of Lo (2002) on every Sharpe ratio in the register: by Equation 25.2 the standard error of this one is 0.22, and the six Sharpe ratios of Table 10.4 lie within 0.22 of one another. Choose the measure for what it sees, report by the measure chosen, and do not read a rank from a tail that holds two observations.

Key Takeaways

  • A risk measure is coherent if it is monotone, translation invariant, positively homogeneous and subadditive (Artzner et al. 1999). Value at risk fails subadditivity on 3 of the 120 pairs in this market; expected shortfall never does (Acerbi and Tasche 2002); variance is not coherent either, but ranks portfolios correctly when returns are elliptical.
  • The representation of Rockafellar and Uryasev (2000), Equation 10.3, turns minimum expected shortfall into the linear program Equation 10.4, convex and solved exactly by ROI; the modified expected shortfall of Favre and Galeano (2002) and Boudt et al. (2008) depends on the third and fourth moments, is not convex, and is reached only by search.
  • In PortfolioAnalytics a mean plus ES, CSM or EQS specification is a ratio problem unless maxSTARR, CSMratio or EQSratio is FALSE, while mean plus StdDev is a quadratic-utility problem unless maxSR = TRUE; ROI ignores method = "modified"; CSM is silently unevaluated by the search methods; and EQS squares the gains.
  • On the sixteen class series the two frontiers are almost the same curve and not the same portfolios: at a matched return their US_credit weights differ by up to 10 percentage points, and in both directions. A 5% historical tail does not register the skewness of that series, which is the work of one month; the Cornish-Fisher estimate does. Of the four objectives only quadratic utility holds it in size, and the minimum-ES portfolio is the closest to symmetric of the convex solutions.
  • Report each portfolio by the measure it optimized, and show all on all measures. Out of sample the minimum-ES rule realizes a higher expected shortfall than minimum variance at every window: a 5% tail on sixty months is three observations, and a thirty-six-month window’s Sharpe ratio rests on two.
