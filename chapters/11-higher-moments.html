11  Higher Moments

What does an investor lose by describing an asset class with two moments when its returns are skewed and fat-tailed? Kraus and Litzenberger (1976) extended the capital asset pricing model to a third moment and showed that investors pay for positive skewness and demand compensation for negative coskewness with the market; Harvey and Siddique (2000) confirmed on the cross-section of equity returns that conditional skewness carries a price of its own, and Dittmar (2002) extended the argument to kurtosis. If the market prices these moments, a portfolio built on \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) alone is leaving something on the table, and the question of this chapter is how much, on the sixteen asset classes of this book.

The allocation is strategic in the sense of Section 3.1: the moments are unconditional, estimated once on the full sample, and nothing is timed. We first extend the expected-utility expansion of Equation 3.12 to four moments and define the comoment tensors that a portfolio’s skewness and kurtosis are built from, then implement them in PortfolioAnalytics, including the structured estimator, the moment interface and a custom objective, and then compare on the sixteen classes what a two-moment investor, a four-moment investor and a minimum-modified-shortfall investor hold.

The data are the sixteen risky class series of Section 2.2, in excess of the Treasury bill as in Section 9.1.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return
Re <- R - rf
stopifnot(ncol(Re) == 16L, !anyNA(Re))
N        <- ncol(Re)
n_months <- nrow(Re)

base_spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only")

The case for the chapter is one series. Section 4.2 computed the shape moments of Equation 4.2 for every class, and US_credit stood out; here we recompute the two numbers that matter and the weight a mean-variance investor gives the series.

# Quadratic utility, gamma/2 as `risk_aversion` (appendix B), solved exactly.
qu_weights <- function(gamma) {
  spec <- base_spec |>
    add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
    add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "var", risk_aversion = gamma / 2)
  w <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(Re, spec, optimize_method = "ROI"))
  w <- pmax(w[colnames(Re)], 0)
  w / sum(w)
}

  weight_at_gamma_5 = w_qu5[["US_credit"]])
#>          skewness   excess_kurtosis weight_at_gamma_5 
#>           -1.4468           12.2419            0.1523

US_credit has a skewness of -1.45, the most negative of the 16 series, and an excess kurtosis of 12.2, the second highest. A mean-variance investor with relative risk aversion \(\gamma = 5\), solving Equation 3.12 on these sixteen series exactly as Section 9.3 did, holds 15.2% of it. The tangency portfolio of Section 9.2 holds less, and the middle of the frontier holds more; we return to both in Section 11.3. The weight is not a mistake of the optimizer. Equation 9.2 sees a volatility of 7.3% a year and nothing else, and the question is what changes once it sees the rest.

11.1 Expected Utility Beyond Two Moments

11.1.1 The four-moment expansion

Take the investor of Equation 3.10 with initial wealth normalized to one and returns measured in excess of the bill, so that terminal wealth is \(W = 1 + r_{\boldsymbol{w}}\) with \(r_{\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{R}^e\) the portfolio’s excess return. Write \(\bar{W} = 1 + \mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}\) for its expectation and \(m_{k,\boldsymbol{w}} = \mathbb{E}[(r_{\boldsymbol{w}} - \mu_{\boldsymbol{w}})^k]\) for the \(k\)-th central moment of the portfolio return. A Taylor expansion of \(U(W)\) around \(\bar{W}\), taken through the fourth order and then averaged, gives

\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[U(W)\right] \approx U(\bar{W}) + \frac{U''(\bar{W})}{2}\, m_{2,\boldsymbol{w}} + \frac{U'''(\bar{W})}{6}\, m_{3,\boldsymbol{w}} + \frac{U''''(\bar{W})}{24}\, m_{4,\boldsymbol{w}} . \tag{11.1}\]

Stopping after the second term is Equation 3.12, and Markowitz (1959) gave the two conditions under which stopping there is exact. Samuelson (1970) gave the reason it is usually close: for distributions that become compact as the holding period shrinks, the \(k\)-th moment is of order \(\Delta t^{k/2}\), so each further term is smaller than the last by a factor that vanishes with the horizon. That is a statement about monthly returns of moderate size, and it is why Levy and Markowitz (1979) and Markowitz (2014) found the two-moment approximation almost exact on historical equity data. It says nothing about a series whose fourth moment is dominated by a handful of months, which is what Section 4.3 found for credit.

The signs of the derivatives are not free. Scott and Horvath (1980) showed that any investor with positive marginal utility, risk aversion and decreasing absolute risk aversion has \(U''' > 0\) and \(U'''' < 0\): a preference for skewness and an aversion to kurtosis follow from the same properties that produce an aversion to variance. Equation 11.1 is therefore a four-moment objective with signs fixed by economics; Jondeau and Rockinger (2006) developed its portfolio-choice form, Athayde and Flôres (2004) the maximum-skewness problem, and Mitton and Vorkink (2007) show that an investor who values the right tail deliberately under-diversifies to keep it.

11.1.2 Coskewness and cokurtosis

The portfolio moments in Equation 11.1 are polynomials in the weights whose coefficients are moments of the joint distribution. Let \(\boldsymbol{r} = \boldsymbol{R}^e - \boldsymbol{\mu}\) be the vector of centered excess returns. The coskewness matrix and the cokurtosis matrix are

\[ \boldsymbol{M}_3 = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\boldsymbol{r}\,\boldsymbol{r}' \otimes \boldsymbol{r}'\right] \;\in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N^2}, \qquad \boldsymbol{M}_4 = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\boldsymbol{r}\,\boldsymbol{r}' \otimes \boldsymbol{r}' \otimes \boldsymbol{r}'\right] \;\in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N^3}, \tag{11.2}\]

with \(\otimes\) the Kronecker product, so that the element of \(\boldsymbol{M}_3\) in row \(i\) and column \((j-1)N + k\) is \(m_{ijk} = \mathbb{E}[r_i r_j r_k]\), and \(\boldsymbol{M}_4\) holds \(m_{ijkl}\) in the same unfolded arrangement. They are the third- and fourth-order analogues of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), flattened into two dimensions so that the portfolio moments are matrix products,

\[ m_{3,\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{M}_3\,(\boldsymbol{w}\otimes\boldsymbol{w}), \qquad m_{4,\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{M}_4\,(\boldsymbol{w}\otimes\boldsymbol{w}\otimes\boldsymbol{w}), \tag{11.3}\]

and the standardized skewness and excess kurtosis of the portfolio are \(s_{\boldsymbol{w}} = m_{3,\boldsymbol{w}} / \sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}}^3\) and \(\kappa_{\boldsymbol{w}} = m_{4,\boldsymbol{w}} / \sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}}^4 - 3\), the portfolio versions of Equation 4.2. This is the representation of Jondeau and Rockinger (2006) and of Boudt et al. (2008), whose modified expected shortfall consumes exactly these objects, and it is the one PerformanceAnalytics and PortfolioAnalytics use.

The price of the representation is its size. By symmetry \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) has \(N(N+1)/2\) distinct elements, \(\boldsymbol{M}_3\) has \(N(N+1)(N+2)/6\) and \(\boldsymbol{M}_4\) has \(N(N+1)(N+2)(N+3)/24\).

params <- c(covariance = N * (N + 1) / 2,
            coskewness = N * (N + 1) * (N + 2) / 6,
            cokurtosis = N * (N + 1) * (N + 2) * (N + 3) / 24)
c(params, observations = n_months)
#>   covariance   coskewness   cokurtosis observations 
#>          136          816         3876          372

With \(N = 16\) the sample coskewness matrix has 816 free parameters and the cokurtosis matrix 3876, against 372 months of data: 10.4 parameters per observation for the fourth moment alone, each estimated from a fourth power that a single month can dominate. Section 7.1 found structure necessary for 136 covariances; here it is unavoidable. Martellini and Ziemann (2010) supplied it: impose a constant-correlation or a single-factor model on the comoments, and shrink the sample tensor toward it as Ledoit and Wolf (2004) shrink a covariance matrix. Under a single factor, \(r_i = \beta_i f + \varepsilon_i\) with \(\varepsilon_i\) independent of \(f\) and of each other, the coskewness reduces to

\[ m_{ijk} = \beta_i \beta_j \beta_k\, \mathbb{E}[f^3] + \mathbb{1}\{i = j = k\}\, \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i^3], \tag{11.4}\]

so that \(2N + 1\) numbers replace 816, and the cokurtosis has an analogous form in the loadings and the second and fourth moments of the factor and of the residuals. Boudt et al. (2015) extend this to \(k\) factors in closed form; it is what PortfolioAnalytics computes under method = "boudt", with the factors taken from principal components of the returns. What the structure assumes is worth stating before it is used: the cross terms \(\mathbb{E}[f^2 \varepsilon_i]\) and \(\mathbb{E}[f \varepsilon_i^2]\) are set to zero, so an asset whose residual is large precisely when the factor is large has its skewness removed by the structure. Section 11.2 shows that this is the case for credit.

11.1.3 The CRRA investor

The utility function the rest of the literature uses, introduced in Section 3.1, is constant relative risk aversion, \(U(W) = W^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)\). Its derivatives are \(U' = W^{-\gamma}\), \(U'' = -\gamma W^{-\gamma-1}\), \(U''' = \gamma(\gamma+1)W^{-\gamma-2}\) and \(U'''' = -\gamma(\gamma+1)(\gamma+2)W^{-\gamma-3}\), with the sign pattern of Scott and Horvath (1980). Substituting into Equation 11.1 gives the objective we write into PortfolioAnalytics below,

\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[U(W)\right] \approx \frac{\bar{W}^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\bar{W}^{-\gamma-1}\, m_{2,\boldsymbol{w}} + \frac{\gamma(\gamma+1)}{6}\,\bar{W}^{-\gamma-2}\, m_{3,\boldsymbol{w}} - \frac{\gamma(\gamma+1)(\gamma+2)}{24}\,\bar{W}^{-\gamma-3}\, m_{4,\boldsymbol{w}} , \tag{11.5}\]

with the portfolio moments from Equation 11.3. Truncated after the second term it is Equation 3.12 up to the wealth factors, which are close to one for monthly returns; the third and fourth terms are what this chapter adds. Brandt et al. (2005) use this fourth-order expansion to make a dynamic CRRA problem tractable, Jondeau and Rockinger (2006) measure when the last two terms move the allocation, and Harvey et al. (2010) treat the same objective with uncertain comoments. Because expected utility has no units an investor recognizes, we report it through the certainty equivalent, the riskless excess return that gives the same utility,

\[ \mathrm{CE}_{\boldsymbol{w}} = \big((1-\gamma)\,\mathbb{E}[U(W)]\big)^{1/(1-\gamma)} - 1 , \tag{11.6}\]

which is in return units and can be annualized and compared across portfolios.

11.1.4 The modified expected shortfall as a four-moment objective

The other four-moment objective, and the one PortfolioAnalytics uses most, is the modified expected shortfall of Favre and Galeano (2002) and Boudt et al. (2008). It takes the Cornish-Fisher quantile of Equation 4.6 with the portfolio’s own \(s_{\boldsymbol{w}}\) and \(\kappa_{\boldsymbol{w}}\) substituted in, and reads the expected shortfall of Equation 4.5 off the corrected normal. Its inputs are therefore \((\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}, \boldsymbol{M}_3, \boldsymbol{M}_4)\) and the weights, nothing else, which is why the estimator of the tensors decides the answer. Section 10.1 treats expected shortfall as a risk measure, its coherence and its historical estimator; here we use only the fact that its modified form is a function of the same four moments as Equation 11.5.

11.1.5 Dependence that no moment describes

Coskewness and cokurtosis summarize a joint distribution; they are not the joint distribution. A copula is: by Sklar’s theorem any joint distribution is its marginals composed with a function on the unit cube that carries the dependence and nothing else (see McNeil et al. 2015, ch. 7). The property of that function a portfolio investor cares about is tail dependence,

\[ \lambda_L = \lim_{q \to 0}\; \Pr\!\left(U_1 \leq q \mid U_2 \leq q\right), \tag{11.7}\]

the probability that one asset is in its worst \(q\) fraction of months given that the other is. Embrechts et al. (2002) showed why correlation cannot carry this: a Gaussian copula has \(\lambda_L = 0\) for every correlation below one, whereas a \(t\) copula has \(\lambda_L > 0\), rising as its degrees of freedom fall. Longin and Solnik (2001) and Ang and Chen (2002) documented on equity markets that dependence is stronger in the lower tail than in the upper, and Patton (2004) measured what modeling the asymmetry is worth out of sample. The cross terms that Equation 11.4 discards are the moment-based shadow of this asymmetry, and Section 11.3 measures both on the same pairs.

11.2 Comoment Tensors and Custom Objectives

11.2.1 The sample tensors and their shape

set.portfolio.moments() computes every moment a specification’s objectives need, in one call, and returns them as a named list. Which moments it computes depends on the objectives, so the same returns and the same method yield different lists for different specifications.

var_spec <- base_spec |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")
mes_spec <- base_spec |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "ES",
                arguments = list(method = "modified", p = 0.95))

mom_var <- set.portfolio.moments(Re, var_spec, method = "sample")
mom_s   <- set.portfolio.moments(Re, mes_spec, method = "sample")

list(variance_objective = names(mom_var), modified_ES_objective = names(mom_s))
#> $variance_objective
#> [1] "mu"    "sigma"
#> 
#> $modified_ES_objective
#> [1] "mu"    "sigma" "m3"    "m4"
vapply(mom_s, function(x) paste(dim(x), collapse = " x "), character(1))
#>          mu       sigma          m3          m4 
#>    "16 x 1"   "16 x 16"  "16 x 256" "16 x 4096"

A variance objective produces mu and sigma; a modified expected shortfall objective adds m3 of dimension \(16 \times 256\) and m4 of \(16 \times 4096\), which are Equation 11.2 computed by PerformanceAnalytics::M3.MM() and M4.MM() with the \(1/T\) convention. The tensors carry no dimnames, so every element is addressed by position, and the first thing to do with them is confirm that the contraction of Equation 11.3 returns the moment of the portfolio’s return series.

r_qu5 <- as.numeric(Re %*% w_qu5)
m3_w  <- PerformanceAnalytics::portm3(w_qu5, mom_s$m3)
m4_w  <- PerformanceAnalytics::portm4(w_qu5, mom_s$m4)

stopifnot(all.equal(m3_w, mean((r_qu5 - mean(r_qu5))^3)),
          all.equal(m4_w, mean((r_qu5 - mean(r_qu5))^4)))

sd_ml <- sqrt(mean((r_qu5 - mean(r_qu5))^2))      # the 1/T standard deviation
stopifnot(m3_w / sd_ml^3 < min(shape$skew[shape$kind == "equity"]))   # as the text says
c(skew_from_tensor = m3_w / sd_ml^3,
  skew_from_series = as.numeric(PerformanceAnalytics::skewness(r_qu5)),
  exkurt_from_tensor = m4_w / sd_ml^4 - 3)
#>   skew_from_tensor   skew_from_series exkurt_from_tensor 
#>            -0.7948            -0.7948             3.3409

portm3() and portm4() are the PerformanceAnalytics implementations of Equation 11.3, and the stopifnot() is the guard that the tensor and the series agree to machine precision. The quadratic-utility portfolio at \(\gamma = 5\) has a skewness of -0.79, more negative than any equity market in Table 4.1, which is the credit position doing what Equation 11.1 says it does.

11.2.2 The structured estimator

method = "boudt" fits a statistical factor model by principal components and builds the tensors from Equation 11.4 and its fourth-order counterpart. The pieces are exported and can be called separately.

fit1 <- statistical.factor.model(Re, k = 1)
class(fit1); names(fit1)
#> [1] "stfm"
#> [1] "factor_loadings"     "factor_realizations" "residuals"          
#> [4] "m"                   "k"                   "N"

mom_b <- set.portfolio.moments(Re, mes_spec, method = "boudt")
stopifnot(all.equal(mom_b$sigma, extractCovariance(fit1)),
          all.equal(mom_b$m3,    extractCoskewness(fit1)),
          all.equal(mom_b$m4,    extractCokurtosis(fit1)))

beta <- setNames(as.numeric(fit1$factor_loadings), colnames(Re))
pc_share <- prcomp(zoo::coredata(Re))$sdev^2
stopifnot(all(grepl("_equity$", names(sort(abs(beta), decreasing = TRUE))[1:5])))
round(sort(beta, decreasing = TRUE)[1:5], 3)
#> EU_equity CH_equity UK_equity US_equity JP_equity 
#>     0.506     0.404     0.400     0.303     0.284

The single factor explains 44% of the total variance and loads most heavily on the equity markets, which is the factor Section 5.2 found; US_credit has a loading of 0.092, and its own residual carries 79% of its variance.

NoteThe number of factors must be a literal

set.portfolio.moments() recovers k from match.call() rather than from its value, so method = "boudt", k = 2 works and k = n_factors with n_factors <- 2 does not: the unevaluated symbol reaches statistical.factor.model(), which fails on k <= 0 with a message about language types.

n_factors <- 2
ok  <- set.portfolio.moments(Re, mes_spec, method = "boudt", k = 2)
bad <- try(set.portfolio.moments(Re, mes_spec, method = "boudt", k = n_factors),
           silent = TRUE)
c(literal = class(ok), variable = class(bad))
#>     literal    variable 
#>      "list" "try-error"

Fit the model with statistical.factor.model(R, k = n_factors), where k is an ordinary argument, and extract the tensors from the fit.

How different the two estimators are is visible in a single element. Take the coskewness of US_equity with US_credit and EU_equity, and the own third moment of US_credit, standardized by the three volatilities so that they read like correlations.

pos   <- function(s) match(s, colnames(Re))
m3_at <- function(M3, i, j, k) M3[i, (j - 1) * N + k]      # element m_ijk of M3
sds   <- apply(zoo::coredata(Re), 2, stats::sd)
std_coskew <- function(M3, a, b, d) {
  i <- pos(a); j <- pos(b); k <- pos(d)
  m3_at(M3, i, j, k) / (sds[i] * sds[j] * sds[k])
}

triple <- tibble::tibble(
  element    = c("US_equity, US_credit, EU_equity", "US_credit, US_credit, US_credit"),
  sample     = c(std_coskew(mom_s$m3, "US_equity", "US_credit", "EU_equity"),
                 std_coskew(mom_s$m3, "US_credit", "US_credit", "US_credit")),
  structured = c(std_coskew(mom_b$m3, "US_equity", "US_credit", "EU_equity"),
                 std_coskew(mom_b$m3, "US_credit", "US_credit", "US_credit"))
)
triple
#> # A tibble: 2 × 3
#>   element                         sample structured
#>   <chr>                            <dbl>      <dbl>
#> 1 US_equity, US_credit, EU_equity -0.432     -0.307
#> 2 US_credit, US_credit, US_credit -1.35      -0.182

frob <- c(M3 = norm(mom_s$m3 - mom_b$m3, "F") / norm(mom_s$m3, "F"),
          M4 = norm(mom_s$m4 - mom_b$m4, "F") / norm(mom_s$m4, "F"))
round(frob, 3)
#>    M3    M4 
#> 0.536 0.468

The structured own third moment of US_credit is 13% of the sample value, and across the whole tensor the two estimates differ by 54% of the sample norm for \(\boldsymbol{M}_3\) and 47% for \(\boldsymbol{M}_4\). Where the difference comes from is the identity \(\mathbb{E}[(\beta f + \varepsilon)^3] = \beta^3\mathbb{E}[f^3] + 3\beta^2\mathbb{E}[f^2\varepsilon] + 3\beta\,\mathbb{E}[f\varepsilon^2] + \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^3]\), of which Equation 11.4 keeps the first and last terms.

The two cross terms carry 87% of the series’ skewness. US_credit is not skewed because the equity factor is skewed, nor because its residual is; it is skewed because its residual is large and negative in the months when the factor is, which is the joint event Section 4.3 identified and the dependence that Equation 11.7 describes. A factor structure with independent residuals cannot represent it, so the estimator built to reduce the variance of the tensor removes the feature of this asset that the tensor was wanted for. That is the bias half of the trade Martellini and Ziemann (2010) describe, and it decides the allocation in Section 11.3.

11.2.3 The moment interface

optimize.portfolio() obtains its moments from one function, momentFUN, whose default is set.portfolio.moments; Section 7.2 and Section 6.2 replaced it for the first two moments, and the same argument carries the tensors. A moment function of our own also removes the need to pass method through ..., which is fragile for the reason the callout above gives.

four_moments <- function(R, portfolio, ...) {
  X <- zoo::coredata(R)
  S <- stats::cov(X)
  list(mu = matrix(colMeans(X), ncol = 1), sigma = S,
       m3 = PerformanceAnalytics::M3.MM(X),
       m4 = PerformanceAnalytics::M4.MM(X),
       mean = colMeans(X), var = S)      # both pairs of names, as chapter 7 requires
}

structured_moments <- function(R, portfolio, ...) {
  fit <- statistical.factor.model(R, k = 1)
  S   <- extractCovariance(fit)
  list(mu = matrix(colMeans(zoo::coredata(R)), ncol = 1), sigma = S,
       m3 = extractCoskewness(fit), m4 = extractCokurtosis(fit),
       mean = colMeans(zoo::coredata(R)), var = S)
}

The contract, read from optimize.portfolio() and constrained_objective(), is this. The function is called once, before the search, with R and portfolio supplied if they are among its formals and any other formal matched by name from the ... of optimize.portfolio(); its own ... is discarded. The list it returns is handed, under DEoptim, random, pso and GenSA, to every objective function by name: each element whose name matches a formal of the objective function is passed as that argument, R and weights are added if the function declares them, and the objective’s arguments list is applied last and overrides anything of the same name. The names mu, sigma, m3 and m4 are therefore not special; they are the formals of StdDev(), VaR() and ES(), and a custom objective can name its inputs as it likes provided the moment function returns them under those names. A missing name fails inside the objective function, ES(method = "modified") with M3 must be a matrix and a function without a default with a missing-argument error; both are reported as a message per evaluation and then surface as a second, unrelated error at the end of the search. The convex solvers read only mu and sigma, the patched behavior recorded in Appendix D.

11.2.4 A custom objective: the CRRA investor

PortfolioAnalytics has no expected-utility objective beyond the quadratic one. It does allow an objective to be any function whose name is passed to add.objective(), so Equation 11.5 is a dozen lines. The function returns the negative of the expansion, because a risk objective is minimized, and takes an order argument so that the same function can be truncated after the second moment for comparison.

crra_utility <- function(R, weights, mu, sigma, m3, m4, gamma = 5, order = 4) {
  w  <- as.numeric(weights)
  Wb <- 1 + sum(w * as.numeric(mu))               # expected terminal wealth, W0 = 1
  m2 <- as.numeric(t(w) %*% sigma %*% w)
  eu <- Wb^(1 - gamma) / (1 - gamma) - gamma / 2 * Wb^(-gamma - 1) * m2
  if (order >= 3)
    eu <- eu + gamma * (gamma + 1) / 6 * Wb^(-gamma - 2) *
      PerformanceAnalytics::portm3(w, m3)
  if (order >= 4)
    eu <- eu - gamma * (gamma + 1) * (gamma + 2) / 24 * Wb^(-gamma - 3) *
      PerformanceAnalytics::portm4(w, m4)
  -eu
}

NoteWorked: polishing the metaheuristic answer

nloptr::slsqp() is a sequential quadratic programming solver that takes bounds and an equality constraint directly. The wrapper below closes over the moments so that the objective is a function of the weights alone.

crra_fn <- function(mom, gamma, order = 4) function(w)
  crra_utility(NULL, w, mom$mu, mom$sigma, mom$m3, mom$m4, gamma, order)

polish <- function(f, w0) {
  w0  <- pmax(as.numeric(w0), 0); w0 <- w0 / sum(w0)
  sol <- nloptr::slsqp(x0 = w0, fn = f, lower = rep(0, N), upper = rep(1, N),
                       heq = function(w) sum(w) - 1,
                       control = list(xtol_rel = 1e-10, maxeval = 2000))
  w <- pmax(sol$par, 0)
  setNames(w / sum(w), colnames(Re))
}
turnover <- function(w1, w2) sum(abs(w1[names(w2)] - w2)) / 2
ce_annual <- function(neg_eu, gamma) 12 * (((1 - gamma) * (-neg_eu))^(1 / (1 - gamma)) - 1)

11.2.6 Conditional comoments

Everything above is unconditional. CCCgarch.MM() produces conditional moments from a constant-conditional-correlation GARCH fit (Bollerslev 1990): a GARCH(1,1) per series, the correlation matrix \(\boldsymbol{R}\) of the standardized residuals and the one-step-ahead volatility forecasts \(\boldsymbol{D}_{t+1}\), assembled as \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{D}_{t+1}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{D}_{t+1}\), with the tensors taken from the standardized residuals rescaled to the forecast volatilities. Jondeau and Rockinger (2003) show that conditional skewness and kurtosis themselves persist, which the CCC structure holds fixed, and Guidolin and Timmermann (2008) model the same features through regimes, the route Section 19.1 takes. The function requires fGarch, which is not in this book’s library, so the call is shown and not run.

conditional <- CCCgarch.MM(Re)
str(conditional, give.attr = FALSE)     # mu, sigma, m3, m4: the same names

The list has the same four names, so it drops into momentFUN unchanged. One property of it matters: a series whose GARCH alpha1 falls below 0.01 is silently given its unconditional volatility for every date, so a mixed universe receives a conditional matrix for some assets and an unconditional one for the rest, with nothing in the output to say which.

11.3 What the Third and Fourth Moments Buy

The application opens with the picture: the sixteen series in the plane that Equation 11.1 cares about and Equation 9.2 does not.

The Jarque-Bera test of Equation 4.4 rejected normality for most of these series; Figure 11.1 shows that the rejections are not of one kind. The equity markets sit at a skewness between -0.59 and -0.28 and an excess kurtosis below 4, the region where Samuelson (1970) and Levy and Markowitz (1979) say two moments suffice. US and UK credit sit at an excess kurtosis of about 12, 3.8 times the highest equity value, and that is where the terms of Equation 11.5 have something to do.

We compare the mean-variance tangency portfolio of Section 9.2, computed as it was there; the quadratic-utility investor of Equation 3.12 at \(\gamma = 5\) and \(\gamma = 10\); the four-moment investor of Equation 11.5 at the same two risk aversions; and the two minimum-shortfall portfolios of Table 11.2.

Read Table 11.3 and Figure 11.2 by pairs. At \(\gamma = 5\) the two-moment investor holds 15% of US_credit and the four-moment investor 1%; US_government, the series with a positive skewness and the lowest excess kurtosis in Figure 11.1, rises from 10% to 27%, taking the whole of the credit weight and 3 percentage points from the two equity markets besides. At \(\gamma = 10\) the same move takes the credit weight from 6% to 0%. The portfolio’s realized skewness improves from -0.79 to -0.65 at \(\gamma = 5\), and its historical shortfall from 7.9% to 7.3%. This is the message of the chapter: the third and fourth moments move the portfolio away from the asset whose variance understates its risk, and toward the one whose variance overstates it.

The size of the effect is as important as its direction. In the four-moment investor’s own units the move from the two-moment to the four-moment portfolio at \(\gamma = 5\) is worth 5 basis points of certainty-equivalent return a year. That is the finding of Levy and Markowitz (1979) and Markowitz (2014) restated on this universe: even with the most skewed class in the set at 15% of the portfolio, the two-moment approximation costs a few basis points, because Equation 11.1 converges quickly for monthly returns of this size. It is also smaller than the 44 basis points the unpolished DEoptim answer left on the table, which is the order of the two errors an implementation has to respect. The shortfall investor is a different case. Under the sample tensors the minimum lands within 6% of minimum variance, because the least volatile asset here, US_government, is also among the least skewed; under the structured tensors it buys 15% of credit, and its realized shortfall of 2.8% exceeds both the 2.4% of the sample-tensor minimum and the 2.5% of minimum variance.

11.3.2 What the tensors are estimated from

Every tensor above was estimated on 372 months, and Section 4.2 found that the rolling skewness of a single series changes sign in a third of sixty-month windows. The equivalent question for a portfolio is how far its skewness moves when the same sample is resampled, and we answer it with the twelve-month block bootstrap of Section 9.3, on the \(\gamma = 5\) quadratic-utility portfolio that holds the credit.

The portfolio skewness that Equation 11.5 prices at -0.79 has a ninety-percent bootstrap interval from -1.21 to -0.31, a factor of 4 between its ends. It is also why the warnings of Section 8.1 apply here with more force than to the mean. 11.3.3 Dependence the tensors are a shadow of

Table 11.1 traced the skewness of credit to the co-movement of its residual with the factor in bad months. Equation 11.7 measures that co-movement directly, without any moment. We transform three pairs to ranks with copula::pobs(), count the months in which both series are in their worst decile, compare the count with what a Gaussian copula at the same correlation implies, and fit a \(t\) copula by maximum likelihood for its degrees of freedom and implied \(\lambda_L\).

tail_dependence <- function(a, b, q = 0.10) {
  U   <- copula::pobs(zoo::coredata(Re[, c(a, b)]))     # ranks: the margins are gone
  rho <- as.numeric(stats::cor(Re[, a], Re[, b]))
  fit <- copula::fitCopula(copula::tCopula(dim = 2), U, method = "ml")
  c(correlation     = rho,
    lower_empirical = mean(U[, 1] <= q & U[, 2] <= q) / q,
    upper_empirical = mean(U[, 1] > 1 - q & U[, 2] > 1 - q) / q,
    lower_gaussian  = copula::pCopula(c(q, q), copula::normalCopula(rho)) / q,
    t_df            = fit@estimate[2],
    t_lambda        = copula::lambda(fit@copula)[[1]])
}
pairs <- list(c("US_equity", "EU_equity"), c("US_equity", "US_credit"),
              c("US_credit", "US_government"))

The empirical columns are conditional probabilities: given that one series is in its worst tenth of months, how often the other is too, and the same in the best tenth. For US_equity / EU_equity and for US_credit / US_government the lower figure exceeds the upper, which is the asymmetry of Longin and Solnik (2001) and Ang and Chen (2002); for US_equity / US_credit the two tails are equal, at 8 months each, so the asymmetry is not a property of every pair. For every pair the lower figure exceeds what a Gaussian copula at the measured correlation implies, which is the failure Embrechts et al. (2002) describe. The fitted degrees of freedom say the same thing in a parameter: for US_credit / US_government the \(t\) copula wants 2.9 degrees of freedom and an asymptotic tail dependence of 0.45, where the Gaussian copula puts zero. A credit position and a government bond position that fall together in their worst months, at a correlation of 0.52, is what a covariance matrix cannot see, what the cross terms of Table 11.1 partly capture, and what a single-factor structure loses entirely. Modeling it directly, as Patton (2004) does, is an allocation method of its own and beyond the strategic setting of this chapter; Section 19.1 reaches the same dependence from the time-series side, where the comoments become properties of a regime rather than of the sample.

Key Takeaways

  • Expected utility expanded to four moments, Equation 11.1, rewards skewness and penalizes kurtosis with signs fixed by Scott and Horvath (1980); the portfolio moments it needs are contractions of the tensors of Equation 11.2, which PerformanceAnalytics::portm3() and portm4() compute.
  • The tensors have 816 and 3876 free parameters on sixteen assets. The single-factor structure of Martellini and Ziemann (2010) and Boudt et al. (2015) behind method = "boudt" reduces them to a few dozen and, on this universe, removes 87% of the skewness of US_credit, which lives in the cross terms the structure sets to zero.
  • A custom objective is any function whose name add.objective() receives. It is fed the moment list by name, must be visible from the global environment, and is skipped without an error when it is not; assert after every solve that it was evaluated.
  • The metaheuristics find the neighborhood of the optimum, not the optimum: on Equation 11.5 the DEoptim answer was 44 basis points of certainty equivalent below the polished one, more than the effect under study. A local solver started from the metaheuristic answer is part of the method.
  • At \(\gamma = 5\) the four-moment investor moves about 14 percentage points from credit to government bonds, worth a few basis points a year: the direction is what the theory predicts, the size is what Levy and Markowitz (1979) predicted. The coskewness that drives it is estimated with an uncertainty that leaves the sign of a single element undetermined.
