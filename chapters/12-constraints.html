12  Portfolio Constraints

How does an investment policy statement, written in prose by a committee, become something a solver can read? Jagannathan and Ma (2003) showed that the answer is less mechanical than it looks: imposing a no-short-sale constraint on a minimum-variance problem is mathematically the same as shrinking the covariance matrix, so a constraint that binds is not a nuisance the optimizer works around but information about which estimates it should not trust. Frost and Savarino (1988) had made the empirical case fifteen years earlier, finding that bounding the weights improved out-of-sample performance because it capped how much of an estimation error any one position could express.

This chapter is about strategic allocation in the sense of Section 3.1: the constraints are the policy, the policy is set once from long-run beliefs, and Brinson et al. (1986) found that it explains most of the variation in what a fund subsequently earns. We first set out what each family of constraint does to the feasible set and what a binding constraint is worth, then implement every constraint type PortfolioAnalytics offers on the sixteen risky classes and record what each does to the two anchor portfolios of Section 9.2, then translate a complete mandate line by line into a specification, solve it, audit it, and price each of its lines.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)   # 16 risky classes
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return                                  # the bill
Re <- R - rf                                                       # excess returns
stopifnot(ncol(Re) == 16L, !anyNA(Re))

12.1 From Policy Statement to Feasible Set

Every constraint is a statement about which weight vectors are admissible. The mean-variance problem of Markowitz (1952), Equation 9.2, is solved over the whole of \(\mathbb{R}^N\); a mandate replaces \(\mathbb{R}^N\) by a feasible set \(\mathcal{C}\), and the constrained problem is

\[ \min_{\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathcal{C}} \; \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \max_{\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathcal{C}} \; \frac{\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}}} . \tag{12.1}\]

Markowitz (1956) solved exactly this problem for any set of linear constraints, and the critical line algorithm of that paper is where the geometry of the constrained frontier was first worked out; Perold (1984) made it computable at scale, and Kolm et al. (2014) survey the constraints practice has added since. The families PortfolioAnalytics recognizes are these:

The first seven rows are intersections of half-spaces, so any number of them together leaves a convex set and Equation 12.1 remains a quadratic program with a unique answer (Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004). The diversification floor is a ball intersected with the simplex, also convex. Only the position limit breaks convexity: the set of portfolios holding at most \(k\) names is a union of faces of the simplex, and Section 13.1 shows what that costs in solver choice. Turnover and transaction costs depend on a starting portfolio and have their own chapter, Section 23.1.

12.1.1 What a binding constraint is worth

Write the linear constraints as \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w} \le \boldsymbol{b}\) and keep the budget separate. The Lagrangian of the minimum-variance problem is

\[ \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w}, \lambda_0, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} - \lambda_0\left(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\iota} - 1\right) + \boldsymbol{\lambda}'\left(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w} - \boldsymbol{b}\right), \tag{12.2}\]

and the conditions of Kuhn and Tucker (1951) characterize the solution: at the optimum \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} = \lambda_0\boldsymbol{\iota} - \boldsymbol{A}'\boldsymbol{\lambda}\), every multiplier satisfies \(\lambda_k \ge 0\), and \(\lambda_k\,(\boldsymbol{a}_k'\boldsymbol{w} - b_k) = 0\) for each row. The last condition, complementary slackness, is the one to hold on to: a constraint that is not binding has a multiplier of zero and could have been left out; a constraint that binds has a positive multiplier, and that multiplier is its shadow price,

\[ \lambda_k = -\,\frac{\partial\, \sigma^{2}_{*}/2}{\partial b_k}, \tag{12.3}\]

the rate at which the optimal variance would fall if the bound were relaxed by one unit (Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004, ch. 5). Solvers do not always return the multipliers, but Equation 12.3 can be recovered by perturbing a bound and re-solving, which is what Section 12.3 does for every line of a mandate.

12.1.2 The Jagannathan–Ma covariance

For the long-only constraint \(\boldsymbol{w} \ge \boldsymbol{0}\), with multipliers \(\boldsymbol{\delta} \ge \boldsymbol{0}\) so that the first-order condition reads \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} = \lambda_0\boldsymbol{\iota} + \boldsymbol{\delta}\), Jagannathan and Ma (2003) observed that the constrained solution is the unconstrained minimum-variance portfolio of Equation 9.3 computed on a modified matrix,

\[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma} - \left(\boldsymbol{\delta}\boldsymbol{\iota}' + \boldsymbol{\iota}\boldsymbol{\delta}'\right), \qquad \boldsymbol{w}_{\text{long-only}} = \frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}} {\boldsymbol{\iota}'\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}} . \tag{12.4}\]

The proof is two lines: \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} - \boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{w}) - \boldsymbol{\iota}(\boldsymbol{\delta}'\boldsymbol{w}) = \lambda_0\boldsymbol{\iota}\), using the budget and complementary slackness, and a vector proportional to \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}\) is the unconstrained answer. Element by element, \(\tilde{\sigma}_{ij} = \sigma_{ij} - \delta_i - \delta_j\): every covariance involving an asset the constraint excludes is reduced by that asset’s shadow price. The assets an unconstrained optimizer shorts are the ones with high estimated covariance to the rest, and the constraint shrinks exactly those covariances, which is why Jagannathan and Ma (2003) could show that the long-only minimum-variance portfolio performs about as well out of sample as one built on a shrunk covariance of the kind Section 7.1 estimates. DeMiguel et al. (2009) generalize the observation: a bound on the norm of the weight vector is a family of shrinkage estimators indexed by the bound, with the long-only constraint as one member. A binding constraint, on this reading, is the mandate correcting the estimator.

12.1.3 What constraints cost in information

The other side of the ledger is due to Grinold and Kahn (2000) and Clarke et al. (2002). The fundamental law of active management, Grinold (1989), says the attainable information ratio is the product of forecasting skill and the number of independent bets, and Clarke et al. (2002) add a third factor, the transfer coefficient,

\[ \text{TC} = \operatorname{corr}\!\left(\sigma_i\, w^{c}_i,\; \sigma_i\, w^{u}_i\right), \tag{12.5}\]

the cross-sectional correlation between the risk-weighted positions the constraints allow and the ones the forecasts called for. It is one when nothing binds and falls with every constraint that does, and Equation 24.4 in Section 24.1 uses it to price a long-only restriction on an active manager. Best and Grauer (1991) supply the mechanism: under bounds, a small change in \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) no longer moves the weights continuously but switches which constraints are active, so the data determine the set of held assets and the bounds pin the weights within it. Michaud (1989) and Green and Hollifield (1992), cited in Section 9.2, describe the unconstrained weights those bounds replace; Black and Litterman (1992) built an estimation method around the observation that nobody would hold them. A tracking-error limit is a constraint of the same family and is treated in Section 24.1 following Jorion (2003).

12.2 Implementation

12.2.1 Two anchors and a bookkeeping function

Everything below is measured against the two anchor portfolios of Section 9.2, recomputed rather than retyped. The tangency portfolio is requested with maxSR = TRUE, which Section 13.2 verified against an independent solve.

S  <- stats::cov(Re); mu <- colMeans(Re); N <- ncol(Re)
p_vol <- function(w) sqrt(as.numeric(t(w) %*% S %*% w) * 12)
p_ret <- function(w) sum(mu * w) * 12
p_sr  <- function(w) p_ret(w) / p_vol(w)

# Weights by name from any solved specification. The assertion is not
# decoration: a solver that fails returns NULL or NA without raising. A
# specification on part of the universe is solved on that part, and the
# series it leaves out come back with a weight of zero.
weights_of <- function(spec, method = "ROI", ...) {
  a <- names(spec$assets)
  stopifnot(all(a %in% colnames(Re)))
  w <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(Re[, a], spec, optimize_method = method, ...))
  stopifnot(!is.null(w), !anyNA(w), all(a %in% names(w)))
  out <- setNames(numeric(ncol(Re)), colnames(Re)); out[a] <- w[a]
  out
}
min_var  <- function(spec) weights_of(spec |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var"))
tangency <- function(spec) weights_of(spec |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "StdDev"), maxSR = TRUE)

stats_of <- function(label, w) tibble::tibble(
  portfolio = label, vol = p_vol(w), ret = p_ret(w), sharpe = p_sr(w),
  held = sum(w > 1e-6), largest = max(w), in_what = names(which.max(w)))

base_spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only")

w_gmv <- min_var(base_spec)
w_tan <- tangency(base_spec)
anchors <- dplyr::bind_rows(stats_of("minimum variance", w_gmv),
                            stats_of("tangency", w_tan))
anchors
#> # A tibble: 2 × 7
#>   portfolio           vol    ret sharpe  held largest in_what      
#>   <chr>             <dbl>  <dbl>  <dbl> <int>   <dbl> <chr>        
#> 1 minimum variance 0.0413 0.0329  0.795     6   0.821 US_government
#> 2 tangency         0.0512 0.0508  0.991     5   0.684 US_government

The minimum-variance anchor places 82% in US_government. That number is the reason this chapter exists, and Section 12.3 returns to it.

NoteWorked: the shadow prices of the long-only constraint, and Equation 12.4 checked

With the long-only anchor in hand, the multipliers can be read off the first-order condition: \(\boldsymbol{\delta} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} - \lambda_0\boldsymbol{\iota}\), and \(\lambda_0 = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\) because \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\delta} = 0\) at the optimum.

lambda0 <- as.numeric(t(w_gmv) %*% S %*% w_gmv)
delta   <- setNames(as.numeric(S %*% w_gmv) - lambda0, colnames(Re))
stopifnot(all(abs(delta[w_gmv > 1e-6]) < 1e-7), all(delta[w_gmv <= 1e-6] > 0))   # complementary slackness

S_tilde <- S - (outer(delta, rep(1, N)) + outer(rep(1, N), delta))       # @eq-12-jm
w_jm    <- as.numeric(solve(S_tilde, rep(1, N)))
w_jm    <- setNames(w_jm / sum(w_jm), colnames(Re))

c(max_abs_difference = max(abs(w_jm - w_gmv)),
  smallest_eigenvalue_ratio = min(eigen(S_tilde)$values) / min(eigen(S)$values))
#>        max_abs_difference smallest_eigenvalue_ratio 
#>       0.00000000000001816       1.00413189110421763
round(sort(delta * 12 * 1e4, decreasing = TRUE)[1:4], 2)      # largest, annualized variance x 1e4
#>     EU_credit     CH_credit     JP_credit CH_government 
#>          6.37          5.73          4.37          4.33

The multipliers are zero on every held series and positive on every excluded one, which is complementary slackness, and the unconstrained portfolio of Equation 9.3 on \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) reproduces the solver’s answer to 2e-14. The largest shadow prices belong to EU_credit, CH_credit, JP_credit. Since \(\delta_i\) is the covariance of series \(i\) with the portfolio less the portfolio’s own variance, these are the series that covary most with what is held, and theirs are the covariances Equation 12.4 shrinks most. The modified matrix stays positive definite, its smallest eigenvalue within 0.41% of that of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\).

12.2.2 Box constraints

A box constraint bounds each weight separately. min and max accept a scalar or a vector named by asset, and a later box constraint replaces an earlier one in the solved problem, which is how a cap is layered over long_only here.

bmax <- setNames(rep(0.25, N), colnames(Re))
bmax["GLOBAL_commodity"] <- 0.10                    # one class capped tighter
spec_box <- base_spec |> add.constraint(type = "box", min = 0, max = bmax)

w_gmv_box <- min_var(spec_box); w_tan_box <- tangency(spec_box)
dplyr::bind_rows(stats_of("minimum variance, 25% cap", w_gmv_box),
                 stats_of("tangency, 25% cap", w_tan_box))
#> # A tibble: 2 × 7
#>   portfolio                    vol    ret sharpe  held largest in_what  
#>   <chr>                      <dbl>  <dbl>  <dbl> <int>   <dbl> <chr>    
#> 1 minimum variance, 25% cap 0.0526 0.0337  0.642     8    0.25 US_credit
#> 2 tangency, 25% cap         0.0698 0.0634  0.908     7    0.25 US_equity

The cap binds hard on the minimum-variance side, where 82% in one series becomes 2 positions at exactly 25%, and volatility rises from 4.13% to 5.26%. On the tangency side the Sharpe ratio falls from 0.991 to 0.908.

12.2.3 Group constraints

A group constraint bounds the sum of weights over a set of assets. The groups are given as a list of index vectors and the limits as two vectors in the same order, and nothing in the language enforces that order. split() returns its groups alphabetically, so the limits are built by name and the order is asserted, exactly as Section 9.3 did.

group_of <- function(a) dplyr::case_when(
  grepl("_equity$", a)     ~ "equity",
  grepl("_government$", a) ~ "government",
  grepl("_credit$", a)     ~ "credit",
  TRUE                     ~ "commodity")
grp  <- split(seq_len(N), group_of(colnames(Re)))
gmin <- c(commodity = 0.00, credit = 0.05, equity = 0.20, government = 0.10)
gmax <- c(commodity = 0.15, credit = 0.40, equity = 0.60, government = 0.50)
stopifnot(identical(names(grp), names(gmin)), identical(names(grp), names(gmax)))

spec_group <- base_spec |>
  add.constraint(type = "group", groups = grp,
                 group_min = unname(gmin[names(grp)]),
                 group_max = unname(gmax[names(grp)]))

opt_group <- optimize.portfolio(Re, spec_group |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "StdDev"),
  optimize_method = "ROI", maxSR = TRUE)
w_tan_grp <- extractWeights(opt_group)[colnames(Re)]
w_gmv_grp <- min_var(spec_group)

extractGroups(opt_group)$group_weights
#>  commodity     credit     equity government 
#>    0.04136    0.16233    0.29630    0.50000

extractGroups() returns the group sums by name; the chart is one call.

chart.GroupWeights(opt_group, grouping = "groups", main = "")

tibble::tibble(group = names(grp), lower = gmin[names(grp)], upper = gmax[names(grp)],
               min_variance = g_gmv, tangency = g_tan) |>
  dplyr::mutate(binds_mv  = abs(min_variance - lower) < 1e-6 | abs(min_variance - upper) < 1e-6,
                binds_tan = abs(tangency - lower) < 1e-6 | abs(tangency - upper) < 1e-6)
#> # A tibble: 4 × 7

NoteOne group constraint per specification

add.constraint() appends, but the solver reads constraints through get_constraints(), which keeps only the last group constraint it meets, and likewise the last box constraint. Regions on top of asset classes therefore cannot be a second group line; the second grouping goes in as a linear exposure constraint with an indicator matrix, shown below. The same rule is why the box constraint above could replace long_only without complaint.

12.2.4 Weight-sum constraints

full_investment is weight_sum with both bounds at one; dollar_neutral sets both to zero. A dollar-neutral minimum-variance portfolio is the zero vector, so the long-short example needs a return term, here the quadratic utility of Section 3.1 with risk_aversion read as \(\gamma/2\).

spec_dn <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "dollar_neutral") |>
  add.constraint(type = "box", min = -0.3, max = 0.3) |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "var", risk_aversion = 10)
w_dn <- weights_of(spec_dn)
round(c(sum = sum(w_dn), gross = sum(abs(w_dn)), longs = sum(w_dn > 1e-6), shorts = sum(w_dn < -1e-6)), 4)
#>    sum  gross  longs shorts 
#>  0.000  2.155  8.000  8.000
Warningweight_sum with no arguments leaves one percent uninvested

weight_sum_constraint() defaults to min_sum = 0.99 and max_sum = 1.01, chosen to give the random-portfolio sampler room. Under a variance objective the solver uses that room:

w_loose <- weights_of(portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "weight_sum") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var"))
sum(w_loose)
#> [1] 0.99

The weights sum to 0.99, which is a cash position nobody asked for. Write full_investment, or write both bounds.

12.2.5 A target return

return_constraint() fixes \(\boldsymbol{\mu}'\boldsymbol{w} = \bar{\mu}\), and the word to notice is fixes. Under the ROI quadratic path the constraint is an equality, so a target below the minimum-variance return drags the answer onto the lower branch of the frontier.

target_of <- function(ann) stats_of(sprintf("target %.1f%%", 100 * ann),
  min_var(base_spec |> add.constraint(type = "return", return_target = ann / 12)))
ret_tbl <- dplyr::bind_rows(stats_of("minimum variance, no target", w_gmv),
                            target_of(0.025), target_of(0.05))
ret_tbl[, c("portfolio", "vol", "ret", "sharpe")]
#> # A tibble: 3 × 4
#>   portfolio                      vol    ret sharpe
#>   <chr>                        <dbl>  <dbl>  <dbl>
#> 1 minimum variance, no target 0.0413 0.0329  0.795
#> 2 target 2.5%                 0.0434 0.025   0.576
#> 3 target 5.0%                 0.0505 0.05    0.991

stopifnot(0.025 < p_ret(w_gmv), p_ret(w_gmv) < 0.05)        # one target on each branch

w_tan_8 <- tangency(base_spec |> add.constraint(type = "return", return_target = 0.08 / 12))
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(w_tan_8, w_tan)))                # the target changed nothing
c(requested_under_maxSR = 0.08, achieved = p_ret(w_tan_8), tangency = p_ret(w_tan))
#> requested_under_maxSR              achieved              tangency 
#>               0.08000               0.05077               0.05077

The 5% target is on the efficient branch: more return than the minimum-variance portfolio for more risk. The 2.5% target delivers less return at 4.34% volatility against 4.13%, a portfolio the minimum-variance anchor dominates. A mandate that says “at least” is a floor, and a floor should be imposed only when it binds, which the audit at the end of this section checks. The last line shows the second thing to know: maxSR = TRUE ignores the constraint altogether, because max_sr_opt() searches over target returns and overrides the one in the specification.

12.2.6 Position limits

A cardinality constraint, at most \(k\) non-zero weights, is the one row of Table 12.1 that is not convex, and Bienstock (1996) shows that solving it exactly is a mixed-integer quadratic program. PortfolioAnalytics accepts it everywhere and honors it in three places.

ImportantA position limit on the quadratic path is dropped without a message 
spec_pos  <- base_spec |> add.constraint(type = "position_limit", max_pos = 5)
w_pos_roi <- min_var(spec_pos)
# the limit has to bind for this to be a test: the unconstrained answer holds more than five
stopifnot(sum(w_gmv > 1e-6) > 5, isTRUE(all.equal(w_pos_roi, w_gmv)))
c(limit = 5, held = sum(w_pos_roi > 1e-6), identical_to_unconstrained = isTRUE(all.equal(w_pos_roi, w_gmv)))
#>                      limit                       held 
#>                          5                          6 
#> identical_to_unconstrained 
#>                          1

Under a variance objective the ROI path has no integer formulation, so the constraint is read and then not used: 6 positions come back under a limit of five. On the build this book pins, a position limit (max_pos) on ROI’s quadratic path is dropped without a message: no error, no warning, and the unconstrained weights come back, exactly as on the CRAN release. ROI honors the limit only on its two mixed-integer paths, maximum return and minimum expected shortfall, which it hands to Rglpk. A patch that refuses the quadratic call is proposed upstream, not in the pinned build (Appendix D). The honest check is pos_limit_fail(), which the sampler uses internally and which takes any weight vector; the full table of which solver honors which constraint is Appendix C.

The two paths on which ROI does honor the limit are linear, written as mixed-integer programs. Minimum expected shortfall shows it on the same specification:

w_es     <- weights_of(base_spec |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "ES"))
w_pos_es <- weights_of(spec_pos  |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "ES"))
stopifnot(sum(w_es > 1e-6) > 5, !pos_limit_fail(w_pos_es, 5, NULL, NULL))   # binds, and is honored
c(held_without_limit = sum(w_es > 1e-6), held = sum(w_pos_es > 1e-6),
  fails_limit = pos_limit_fail(w_pos_es, 5, NULL, NULL))
#> held_without_limit               held        fails_limit 
#>                  6                  5                  0

Under a variance objective the exact route does not exist in the package. DEoptim and random portfolios repair every candidate through rp_transform() until it satisfies the limit; pso and GenSA penalize the objective instead and, on Section 13.3’s measurement, return the whole universe anyway. A small seeded run of the one that works:

set.seed(1602)
# DEoptim prints its final member whatever `trace` says; capture.output() swallows it.
utils::capture.output(
  w_pos_de <- weights_of(spec_pos |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var"),
                         method = "DEoptim", search_size = 2000, itermax = 50,
                         traceDE = 0, trace = FALSE)) |> invisible()

# The screening heuristic of Chang et al. (2000): keep the k largest
# unconstrained holdings and re-solve the quadratic program on them.
keep     <- names(sort(w_gmv, decreasing = TRUE))[1:5]
w_screen <- min_var(portfolio.spec(assets = keep) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |> add.constraint(type = "long_only"))
stopifnot(!pos_limit_fail(w_pos_de, 5, NULL, NULL), p_vol(w_pos_de) > p_vol(w_screen))
dplyr::bind_rows(stats_of("DEoptim, max 5", w_pos_de),
                 stats_of("screen then re-solve, 5", w_screen))[, c("portfolio", "vol", "held")]
#> # A tibble: 2 × 3
#>   portfolio                  vol  held
#>   <chr>                    <dbl> <int>
#> 1 DEoptim, max 5          0.0453     3
#> 2 screen then re-solve, 5 0.0414     5

DEoptim honors the limit and lands at 4.53% volatility. The screening heuristic of Chang et al. (2000), two lines and one quadratic program, reaches 4.14%. Neither is certified optimal; the search is 9.5% worse and far slower, which is Section 13.3’s point about heuristics on this universe.

12.2.7 Leverage exposure

12.2.9 Diversification

diversification_constraint() asks for \(1 - \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{w} \ge d\), one minus the Herfindahl index of the weights. The floor is convex, but the package routes it only to the search-based solvers, where it does two things the documentation does not say.

NoteUnder ROI the constraint is ignored; under the searches it is a band 
spec_div  <- base_spec |> add.constraint(type = "diversification", div_target = 0.7)
w_div_roi <- min_var(spec_div)
stopifnot(diversification(w_gmv) < 0.7, isTRUE(all.equal(w_div_roi, w_gmv)))   # a target that binds, and is not read
c(diversification = diversification(w_div_roi), identical_to_unconstrained = isTRUE(all.equal(w_div_roi, w_gmv)))
#>            diversification identical_to_unconstrained 
#>                     0.3185                     1.0000

The ROI path never reads div_target. The search-based path reads it inside constrained_objective() as a penalty that fires only when the achieved value is outside \([0.95\,d,\; 1.05\,d]\), so a target of 0.7 is satisfied by anything between 0.665 and 0.735, and a portfolio more diversified than the target is penalized too. It is a target, not a floor.

set.seed(1602)

DEoptim returns a diversification of 0.682, inside the band and below the target, at 5.36% volatility. The exact solution of the floor sits at 0.700 with 4.56%. HHI() and diversification() take any weight vector, and Section 14.1 uses them on portfolios that were never optimized for either.

12.2.10 Auditing a weight vector against its specification

Every failure above has the same shape: the constraint is in the object and absent from the answer. The remedy reads the specification and checks the weights against each line, extending the four-line audit() of Section 13.2 to every constraint type.

audit_spec <- function(w, spec, tol = 1e-6) {
  w <- w[names(spec$assets)]
  rows <- lapply(spec$constraints, function(cn) {
    if (!isTRUE(cn$enabled)) return(NULL)
    ok <- switch(cn$type,
      full_investment = , dollar_neutral = , active = , weight_sum = , leverage = , weight =
        sum(w) >= cn$min_sum - tol && sum(w) <= cn$max_sum + tol,
      box = , long_only = all(w >= cn$min - tol) && all(w <= cn$max + tol),
      group = { g <- vapply(cn$groups, function(i) sum(w[i]), numeric(1))
                all(g >= cn$cLO - tol & g <= cn$cUP + tol) },
      position_limit    = !pos_limit_fail(w, cn$max_pos, cn$max_pos_long, cn$max_pos_short),
      leverage_exposure = sum(abs(w)) <= cn$leverage + tol,
      factor_exposure   = { e <- as.numeric(t(cn$B) %*% w)
                            all(e >= cn$lower - tol & e <= cn$upper + tol) },
      return            = sum(mu * w) >= cn$return_target - tol,   # read as a floor
      diversification   = diversification(w) >= cn$div_target - tol,
      NA)
    tibble::tibble(constraint = cn$type, satisfied = ok)
  })
  dplyr::bind_rows(rows)
}

12.3 A Mandate, Line by Line

12.3.1 The policy statement

An endowment’s investment committee writes, in prose: the portfolio is fully invested and long only; equities between 40 and 60 percent; government bonds at least 10 percent; credit at most 40 percent; commodities at most 15 percent; no single class series above 35 percent; expected excess return at least 6 percent a year; at most eight positions. The first six lines are linear and enter directly. The seventh is a floor, imposed only where it binds. The eighth is the non-convex line, and the audit will say whether it matters.

m_min <- c(commodity = 0.00, credit = 0.00, equity = 0.40, government = 0.10)
m_max <- c(commodity = 0.15, credit = 0.40, equity = 0.60, government = 1.00)
stopifnot(identical(names(grp), names(m_min)), identical(names(grp), names(m_max)))
floor_ann <- 0.06

mandate <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>                 # fully invested
  add.constraint(type = "box", min = 0, max = 0.35) |>        # long only; no series above 35%
  add.constraint(type = "group", groups = grp,                # class limits, bound by name

  add.constraint(type = "position_limit", max_pos = 8)        # at most eight positions

# The seven lines the solver reads, on any part of the universe.
build <- function(m_min, m_max, cap, floor, assets = colnames(Re)) {
  g <- split(seq_along(assets), group_of(assets))             # that part's own groups, by name
  stopifnot(all(names(m_min)[m_min > 0] %in% names(g)))       # no class with a floor is emptied
  portfolio.spec(assets = assets) |>
    add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
    add.constraint(type = "box", min = 0, max = cap) |>
    add.constraint(type = "group", groups = g,
                   group_min = unname(m_min[names(g)]), group_max = unname(m_max[names(g)])) |>
    add.constraint(type = "return", return_target = floor / 12)
}
keep8   <- names(sort(w_m_qp, decreasing = TRUE))[1:8]
w_m_gmv <- min_var(build(m_min, m_max, 0.35, floor_ann, assets = keep8))
stopifnot(all(audit_spec(w_m_gmv, mandate_r)$satisfied))      # every line, the eighth included
c(held = sum(w_m_gmv > 1e-6), vol_qp = p_vol(w_m_qp), vol_mandate = p_vol(w_m_gmv))
#>        held      vol_qp vol_mandate 
#>     8.00000     0.06584     0.06594

The repaired portfolio passes every line, and the eighth line costs 0.010 percentage points of volatility for the 2 positions it removes. The screen is a heuristic, so that figure is an upper bound on the price of the line.

12.3.2 Which lines bind, and what each is worth

Complementary slackness says a slack constraint has a shadow price of zero. Equation 12.3 is recovered by tightening each line by one percentage point and re-solving, the finite-difference version of the multiplier; the cardinality line is left out because the solver does not read it, so the baseline is the quadratic program’s answer to the other seven, and the repair above is the price of the eighth.

tighten <- list(
  `equity >= 40%`     = \(m1, m2, cap, fl) { m1["equity"] <- m1["equity"] + 0.01;         build(m1, m2, cap, fl) },
  `equity <= 60%`     = \(m1, m2, cap, fl) { m2["equity"] <- m2["equity"] - 0.01;         build(m1, m2, cap, fl) },
  `government >= 10%` = \(m1, m2, cap, fl) { m1["government"] <- m1["government"] + 0.01; build(m1, m2, cap, fl) },
  `credit <= 40%`     = \(m1, m2, cap, fl) { m2["credit"] <- m2["credit"] - 0.01;         build(m1, m2, cap, fl) },
  `commodity <= 15%`  = \(m1, m2, cap, fl) { m2["commodity"] <- m2["commodity"] - 0.01;   build(m1, m2, cap, fl) },
  `no series > 35%`   = \(m1, m2, cap, fl) build(m1, m2, cap - 0.01, fl),
  `return >= 6%`      = \(m1, m2, cap, fl) build(m1, m2, cap, fl + 0.01))

stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(min_var(build(m_min, m_max, 0.35, floor_ann)), w_m_qp)))   # same seven lines
shadow <- lapply(names(tighten), function(nm) {
  s <- tighten[[nm]](m_min, m_max, 0.35, floor_ann)
  tibble::tibble(line = nm,
                 d_vol_mv  = 100 * (p_vol(min_var(s)) - p_vol(w_m_qp)),
                 d_sr_tan  = p_sr(tangency(s)) - p_sr(w_m_tan))
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

Of the seven lines priced, 3 bind on the minimum-variance objective (equity >= 40%; no series > 35%; return >= 6%) and 2 on the tangency (equity >= 40%; no series > 35%). The rest cost nothing in sample, which is not the same as costing nothing: a slack line is insurance against a future estimate that would make it bind. The most expensive line for the minimum-variance portfolio is return >= 6%, at 1.037 percentage points of volatility per point of tightening; for the tangency it is equity >= 40%, at 0.0023 of Sharpe ratio per point.

12.3.4 Why the constraints come first

Read the left panel of Figure 12.3 from the first rung. Unconstrained minimum variance on this universe is degenerate: it puts 82% into US_government, holds 6 series, and is the answer Green and Hollifield (1992) predict whenever one asset has a volatility far below the rest, which Section 9.2 measured at 4.4 times between the most and least volatile. The 35% cap pins US_government at the cap and spreads the remainder over 6 further series, still 55% in government bonds. Only the class limits produce a portfolio a committee would recognize, with 8 positions and equity at 40% of the total. On this universe the group constraints are not a refinement of the optimization; they are the precondition for a portfolio anyone would hold, and the estimation error Section 8.1 measured is why the constrained answer, not the unconstrained one, is the honest estimate. That the same constraints are where a sleeve structure comes from is the subject of Section 15.1.

Key Takeaways

  • A constraint defines the feasible set, and every family in Table 12.1 except the position limit keeps it convex. Choosing a constraint chooses the solver: a position limit needs a mixed-integer formulation or a heuristic, and Section 13.1 measures what the heuristic is worth.
  • A binding long-only constraint is equivalent to shrinking the covariance matrix by the constraint’s own shadow prices, Equation 12.4, so a constraint that binds is the mandate correcting the estimator rather than a cost to be minimized.
  • Shadow prices are recovered by tightening each line and re-solving. On the mandate here most lines are slack in sample: the equity floor and the 35% cap bind on both objectives and the return floor is the most expensive line for minimum variance. The cardinality limit is slack for the tangency portfolio and violated by the minimum-variance answer, where the quadratic path drops it and a screen has to impose it.
  • PortfolioAnalytics accepts every constraint on every solver and honors only some. On the build this book pins, the ROI quadratic path drops a position limit and a diversification target silently, maxSR = TRUE ignores a return target, and a leverage bound returns a long-only portfolio because gmv_opt_leverage() omits ROI’s variable bounds.
  • Audit every returned weight vector against the specification it came from: extractGroups(), HHI(), diversification(), pos_limit_fail() and the sum of weights, or audit_spec() above, which reads the specification and checks each line.
