14  Risk-Based Allocation

Draft|Code: script · exercises · all chapters|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

Every portfolio so far has been described by how much money sits in each position. That is not the same as how much risk sits in each position, and on this universe the two are far apart: the minimum-variance portfolio of Section 9.1 puts most of its capital in one series and, as this chapter measures, nearly all of its risk there too.

Allocating risk rather than capital is an old idea with a clean definition (Qian 2006; Maillard et al. 2010), a cheap approximation, and an implementation in PortfolioAnalytics that does not work. We set out the decomposition that makes the idea precise, compute the exact answer two ways, and then measure what the package’s own risk-budget objective does with a problem whose optimum it scores correctly and cannot find.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)
Re <- R - sim_riskfree_monthly$return
S  <- stats::cov(Re)
N  <- ncol(Re)

14.1 Allocating Risk Rather Than Capital

14.1.1 The decomposition that makes this precise

Portfolio volatility \(\sigma(\boldsymbol{w}) = \sqrt{\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}}\) is homogeneous of degree one in the weights, so by Euler’s theorem it decomposes exactly into additive contributions,

\[ \sigma(\boldsymbol{w}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathrm{RC}_i, \qquad \mathrm{RC}_i = w_i \frac{\partial \sigma}{\partial w_i} = \frac{w_i\,(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w})_i}{\sigma(\boldsymbol{w})} . \tag{14.1}\]

That exactness is what makes risk budgeting possible at all, and it is the point Qian (2006) makes when he says risk budgets do add up: the shares in 1 are not an approximation or an attribution convention, they sum to the total by construction. Dividing through by \(\sigma\) gives shares summing to one, which is what the code below computes.

risk_contribution <- function(w, S) {
  marginal <- as.numeric(S %*% w)
  (w * marginal) / as.numeric(t(w) %*% S %*% w)
}

w_eq <- setNames(rep(1 / N, N), colnames(Re))
round(sort(risk_contribution(w_eq, S), decreasing = TRUE)[1:5], 4)
#> EU_equity CH_equity UK_equity UK_credit EU_credit 
#>    0.1170    0.0973    0.0942    0.0811    0.0787

Sixteen equally weighted series, and the largest contributes 11.7% of the risk against 6.2% of the capital. Equal capital is not equal risk, and the gap is the entire motivation for what follows.

14.1.2 Equal risk contribution

The equal risk contribution portfolio sets every term of 1 equal,

\[ \mathrm{RC}_i = \frac{\sigma(\boldsymbol{w})}{N} \quad \text{for every } i, \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad w_i (\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w})_i = w_j (\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w})_j \;\; \forall\, i, j . \tag{14.2}\]

Maillard et al. (2010) give its properties: it always exists and is unique for a long-only portfolio, and its volatility sits between the minimum-variance portfolio’s and the equally weighted portfolio’s, in that order. It has no closed form except in special cases, but it is emphatically not a hard problem. Spinu (2013) shows that Equation 14.2 is the first-order condition of the convex program

\[ \min_{\boldsymbol{w} > 0} \; \tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln w_i , \tag{14.3}\]

whose log barrier keeps every weight strictly positive and which any convex solver handles; Bai et al. (2016) give a least-squares formulation with the same property. Keep Equation 14.3 in mind for Section 14.3, because the package solves this problem with a metaheuristic, and the problem is convex.

A simple fixed-point iteration converges in milliseconds.

erc <- function(S, tol = 1e-12, max_iter = 1e5) {
  N <- ncol(S)
  w <- rep(1 / N, N)
  for (i in seq_len(max_iter)) {
    marginal <- as.numeric(S %*% w)
    w_new <- (1 / marginal) / sum(1 / marginal)   # down-weight what contributes most
    if (max(abs(w_new - w)) < tol) break
    w <- w_new
  }
  setNames(w, colnames(S))
}

w_erc <- erc(S)
c(max_deviation_from_equal = max(abs(risk_contribution(w_erc, S) - 1 / N)))
#> max_deviation_from_equal 
#>       0.0000000000003543

Six lines, and the risk contributions are equal to machine precision. That number is worth remembering when Section 14.3 spends twenty thousand function evaluations getting nowhere near it.

round(sort(w_erc, decreasing = TRUE)[1:6], 4)
#>    US_government        US_credit    JP_government GLOBAL_commodity 
#>           0.2210           0.0839           0.0757           0.0607 
#>    UK_government    EU_government 
#>           0.0606           0.0550

US_government still receives the largest allocation, at 22.1%, because it is the least volatile series and equalizing risk means overweighting low-volatility assets. That is a long way from the 82% that minimum variance gives it.

14.1.3 The approximation that is nearly free

Maillard et al. (2010) note a special case worth exploiting. If every pairwise correlation equals a common \(\rho\), then Equation 14.2 is solved exactly by

\[ w_i = \frac{\sigma_i^{-1}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_j^{-1}}, \tag{14.4}\]

inverse-volatility weighting, which needs no matrix inverse and no iteration. Correlations here are not equal, but they are not wild either, so Equation 14.4 should land close.

base <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only")

# Exported, and rarely used. Note that it wants the portfolio object as well as
# the returns, and returns a full optimize.portfolio object rather than a vector.
w_iv <- inverse.volatility.weight(Re, base)$weights
w_iv <- w_iv[colnames(Re)]

c(l1_distance_from_erc = sum(abs(w_iv - w_erc)) / 2,
  weight_correlation   = stats::cor(w_iv, w_erc),
  max_rc_deviation     = max(abs(risk_contribution(w_iv, S) - 1 / N)))
#> l1_distance_from_erc   weight_correlation     max_rc_deviation 
#>              0.11482              0.90957              0.02699

inverse.volatility.weight() is exported by PortfolioAnalytics and is rarely used. It lands within 0.11 of the exact answer in \(L_1\) terms, with a weight correlation of 0.91, for the cost of one division per asset. The approximation of Equation 14.4 is doing almost all the work that Equation 14.2 does.

14.2 Implementation

The package has a purpose-built objective for this. It accepts the problem, scores it correctly, and cannot solve it.

rb_spec <- base |>
  add.objective(type = "risk_budget", name = "StdDev", min_concentration = TRUE)

14.2.1 The objective is right

Before blaming a solver it is worth checking what it was asked to minimize. PortfolioAnalytics exposes its own objective function, so we can score two portfolios directly.

score <- function(w) {
  constrained_objective(w = as.numeric(w), R = Re, portfolio = rb_spec,
                        trace = TRUE, normalize = FALSE)$out
}
c(equal_weight = score(w_eq), true_erc = score(w_erc))
#>         equal_weight             true_erc 
#> 1.150416619246363847 0.000000000000002168

1.150 at equal weights and exactly 0.0 at the solution of Equation 14.2. The objective is not misspecified. It knows precisely which portfolio it wants.

14.2.2 The search does not find it

set.seed(1602)

attempt <- function(method, ...) {
  o <- try(suppressWarnings(
    optimize.portfolio(Re, rb_spec, optimize_method = method,
                       trace = FALSE, ...)), silent = TRUE)
  if (inherits(o, "try-error")) {
    return(tibble::tibble(solver = method, l1_from_erc = NA_real_,
                          max_rc_dev = NA_real_, is_equal_weight = NA))
  }
  w <- extractWeights(o)[colnames(Re)]
  tibble::tibble(solver = method,
                 l1_from_erc = sum(abs(w - w_erc)) / 2,
                 max_rc_dev  = max(abs(risk_contribution(w, S) - 1 / N)),
                 is_equal_weight = isTRUE(all.equal(as.numeric(w), rep(1 / N, N),
                                                    tolerance = 1e-6)))
}

searches <- dplyr::bind_rows(
  attempt("DEoptim", search_size = 20000,  traceDE = 0),
  attempt("DEoptim", search_size = 200000, traceDE = 0),
  attempt("random",  search_size = 20000),
  attempt("pso"),
  attempt("GenSA")
)
#>  [1] 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625
#> [11] 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625
#>  [1] 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625
#> [11] 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625

Of the five, 4 return the equal-weighted portfolio exactly, which is the point every one of them starts from. Ten times the evaluation budget changes nothing. pso does move, and moves further from the answer than standing still would have been.

Set beside the objective values, that is the finding: the search sits at a score of 1.15 for two hundred thousand evaluations while a portfolio scoring 0.0 exists and is reachable by six lines of arithmetic.

The one-line approximation of Equation 14.4 is closer to the answer than the twenty-thousand-evaluation search. That is not a statement about risk parity. It is a statement about reaching for a heuristic on a problem that Section 13.1 would classify as convex, given Equation 14.3, and which a fixed point solves to machine precision.

14.2.3 What the machinery does do

The objective is not useless. Asked for a cap rather than an equalization, it delivers.

set.seed(1602)
capped <- optimize.portfolio(
  Re, base |> add.objective(type = "risk_budget", name = "StdDev", max_prisk = 0.15),
  optimize_method = "DEoptim", search_size = 20000, trace = FALSE, traceDE = 0)
#>  [1] 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625
#> [11] 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625 0.0625

w_cap <- extractWeights(capped)[colnames(Re)]
c(largest_risk_contribution = max(risk_contribution(w_cap, S)), cap = 0.15)
#> largest_risk_contribution                       cap 
#>                     0.117                     0.150

A ceiling on any single risk contribution is a constraint a search can satisfy by rejection: it has only to recognize a violation, not to locate an optimum. The practical rule is to use the risk-budget objective for budgets and bounds, and to compute equal risk contribution directly.

14.2.4 Auditing concentration

HHI() and diversification() are exported and take a weight vector, so any portfolio in this book can be audited for concentration in one line, including one returned by a solver that ignored its constraint (Section 13.2).

effective_bets <- function(w) {
  r <- risk_contribution(w, S); r <- r[r > 0]
  exp(-sum(r * log(r)))                      # the entropy of the risk shares
}

w_mv <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(Re,
  base |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var"), optimize_method = "ROI"))[colnames(Re)]

conc <- tibble::tibble(
  portfolio = c("equal weight", "inverse volatility", "equal risk contribution",
                "minimum variance"),
  HHI  = c(HHI(w_eq), HHI(w_iv), HHI(w_erc), HHI(w_mv)),
  div  = c(diversification(w_eq), diversification(w_iv),
           diversification(w_erc), diversification(w_mv)),
  bets = c(effective_bets(w_eq), effective_bets(w_iv),
           effective_bets(w_erc), effective_bets(w_mv)),
  vol  = c(p_vol(w_eq), p_vol(w_iv), p_vol(w_erc), p_vol(w_mv))
)

14.3 What Risk Parity Buys and Costs

14.3.1 The trade, in its own units

Read the last two rows of Table 14.3 together. Minimum variance achieves 4.13% volatility with 2.1 effective bets out of sixteen. Equal risk contribution achieves 6.43% with exactly 16.

That is the trade in its own units: about 55 percent more volatility for 8 times the number of independent bets. Whether it is worth taking depends on how far the covariance matrix behind the minimum-variance answer can be trusted, and Section 9.3 measured that. Resampling the same history moves the minimum-variance portfolio by about five percent of itself, so its concentration is at least stable.

Asness et al. (2012) give the economic argument for taking the trade anyway: if investors are averse to leverage rather than merely to risk, they overpay for high-beta assets, and a portfolio that equalizes risk contributions and then levers up harvests the difference. Clarke et al. (2013) derive the analytic relationships between risk parity, maximum diversification and minimum variance, and show that the three coincide only under conditions this market does not satisfy.

sweep <- utils::read.csv("output/tables/oos-sweep.csv") |>
  dplyr::filter(universe == "classes", window == 60,
                frequency == "months", basis == "gross")
sr_oos <- function(s) sweep$sharpe[match(s, sweep$strategy)]
to_oos <- function(s) sweep$ann_turnover[match(s, sweep$strategy)]
stopifnot(all(c("inverse_vol", "min_variance", "equal_weight", "max_sharpe")
              %in% sweep$strategy))
# The order the text states, read from the register rather than typed.
stopifnot(sr_oos("min_variance") > sr_oos("equal_weight"),
          sr_oos("equal_weight") > sr_oos("inverse_vol"),
          sr_oos("inverse_vol")  > sr_oos("max_sharpe"),
          to_oos("inverse_vol")  < to_oos("max_sharpe") / 4)

Out of sample, inverse volatility earns a Sharpe ratio of 0.573 against minimum variance’s 0.829, equal weighting’s 0.626 and the tangency portfolio’s 0.546 (Section 25.3), at 20% of the turnover of the mean-variance optimizer, which Section 23.3 converts into the Sharpe ratio each strategy surrenders to its own trading. The risk-based portfolios are not uniformly better: minimum variance leads all four, and inverse volatility sits between equal weighting and the tangency portfolio before costs. They are competitive, cheaper, and easier to explain to whoever has to sign for them.

14.3.2 The covariance is a regime variable

One thing risk parity cannot escape, and it is the same thing that undermines everything else in this book. The number of effective bets is not a property of the portfolio. It is a property of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) moves.

d      <- zoo::index(Re)
in_ep  <- vapply(sim_episodes$episode, function(e) in_episode(d, e),
                 logical(length(d)))
calm   <- rowSums(in_ep) == 0               # outside all four episodes
crisis <- in_ep[, "credit_crisis"]

spectrum <- function(keep) {
  e <- pmax(eigen(stats::cov(zoo::coredata(Re[keep, ])), symmetric = TRUE,
                  only.values = TRUE)$values, 0)
  c(months = sum(keep), pc1_share = e[1] / sum(e), directions = sum(e)^2 / sum(e^2))
}
regime <- rbind(calm = spectrum(calm), crisis = spectrum(crisis))
stopifnot(regime["crisis", "pc1_share"] > regime["calm", "pc1_share"],
          regime["crisis", "directions"] < regime["calm", "directions"] / 2)
round(regime, 3)
#>        months pc1_share directions
#> calm      320     0.369      4.800
#> crisis      7     0.760      1.664

Section 7.3 measured it on these sixteen series, and the measurement repeats here: outside the four episodes of sim_episodes the first principal component carries 37% of the variance and the matrix behaves like 4.8 independent directions; through the credit crisis it carries 76% and the count falls to 1.7, a figure that the short window exaggerates and that calm windows of the same length still do not reach. A portfolio constructed to hold sixteen equal risk contributions in calm months holds about 2 independent bets when it matters.

Risk parity does not solve that. It measures risk more honestly than capital weighting does, and then allocates against an estimate that halves precisely when the allocation is tested. Roncalli (2013) treats the estimation problem at length, and Section 19.1 is where this book confronts it rather than noting it.

14.3.3 Budgets that are not equal

Equal risk contribution is the special case of a more general idea. A risk budget assigns each asset a share \(b_i\) with \(\sum_i b_i = 1\) and asks for weights satisfying

\[ \mathrm{RC}_i = b_i \,\sigma(\boldsymbol{w}) \quad \text{for every } i , \tag{14.5}\]

which reduces to Equation 14.2 when \(b_i = 1/N\). Roncalli (2013) develops the general case, and the convex program of Equation 14.3 generalizes directly by replacing the uniform coefficient on the log barrier with \(b_i\). The fixed point in this chapter generalizes just as easily, dividing the marginal contributions by the budget before renormalizing.

The interest of Equation 14.5 is that it is the natural language of a mandate. A policy statement rarely says hold twenty percent in credit; it says credit may consume no more than a quarter of the risk. Those are different instructions and only the second survives a change in volatility. This is also the form in which the package’s own objective is usable, as the max_prisk result above showed: bounds on Equation 14.5 are checkable by rejection, and a search can satisfy them.

14.3.4 Hierarchical risk parity

The methods in this chapter all require \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) and most require its inverse, which Section 7.1 showed is where the estimate is weakest. López de Prado (2016) proposes an alternative that avoids inversion entirely. Hierarchical risk parity clusters the assets on a distance derived from their correlations, orders them so that similar assets are adjacent, and then allocates by recursive bisection: split the ordered set in two, allocate between the halves in inverse proportion to their variances, and recurse.

Nothing in that procedure inverts a matrix, so it is defined even when \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is singular, which by Equation 7.2 is the normal case at instrument level. The claim is that the resulting portfolios are more stable out of sample than either minimum variance or equal risk contribution, because the quantity being estimated, a clustering, is far coarser than a full inverse.

The method belongs with the sleeve structure of Section 15.1 rather than here, because what it is really doing is imposing a hierarchy on the universe and allocating down it. It is mentioned here because it is the obvious next question once risk contributions are on the table, and because it is the one widely used technique in this area that PortfolioAnalytics does not implement at all.

Key Takeaways

  • Portfolio volatility decomposes exactly into additive risk contributions by
    1. The shares are not an attribution convention; they sum to the total by construction (Qian 2006).
  • Equal capital is not equal risk. Sixteen equally weighted series here put 12% of the risk in one of them, against 6.2% of the capital.
  • The equal risk contribution portfolio of Equation 14.2 always exists, is unique long-only, and sits between minimum variance and equal weighting in volatility (Maillard et al. 2010).
  • It is not a hard problem. Equation 14.3 is a convex program with a log barrier, and a six-line fixed point reaches machine precision. The package nonetheless routes it to a metaheuristic.
  • Of five solvers, 4 hand back the equally weighted portfolio they started from, and a tenfold budget changes nothing. The one-line inverse-volatility approximation of Equation 14.4 is closer to the answer than a twenty-thousand-evaluation search.
  • Use the risk-budget objective for caps and budgets, which a search can satisfy by rejection, and compute equal risk contribution directly.
  • Risk parity buys about 8 times the effective bets for about 55 percent more volatility, and out of sample inverse volatility lands between equal weighting and the tangency portfolio at a fraction of the latter’s turnover.
  • The effective number of bets is a property of the covariance matrix, not of the portfolio, and it falls by more than half going into the credit crisis.

Exercises

  1. Run the fixed-point erc() on a covariance matrix estimated from the months outside the four episodes of sim_episodes only, then apply the resulting weights to the months of the credit crisis. How equal are the realized risk contributions?
  2. inverse.volatility.weight() ignores correlations entirely. Construct a universe where that is catastrophic — two nearly identical series and one independent one — and report the risk contributions it produces.
  3. Repeat Table 14.1 with a max_prisk objective instead of min_concentration. Do the solvers agree with each other? Does the budget bind?
  4. The effective-bets measure uses entropy. Replace it with 1 / sum(rc^2), the inverse Herfindahl of the risk shares, and recompute Table 14.3. Which portfolios change rank, and which measure would you defend to a client?
