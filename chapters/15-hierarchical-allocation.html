15  Hierarchical and Sleeve-Based Allocation

How should an allocation be organized when nobody can hold the whole problem in one head, and what does the organization cost? Sharpe (1981) posed it as the decentralized investment problem, in which a central office sets budgets for specialist managers who each optimize the sleeve they know and must be told what makes the pieces add up, and Brinson et al. (1986) measured how much of an institution’s return that first, top-level decision explains. The two-layer structure has been the working organization of every large allocator since: classes first, then selection within them.

This is the chapter in which the book’s own structure is made explicit. Parts III to V allocate across the sixteen class series of Section 2.2, and Part VI selects instruments inside a sleeve; the two layers are not a convenience of exposition but a decision with a cost, and the cost can be measured. It is a strategic decision in the sense of Section 3.1: the policy structure is set from unconditional assumptions and revisited rarely. We first state the two-stage problem and read it as a shrinkage of the covariance matrix, then implement it with mult.portfolio.spec() and by hand, then ask whether the hierarchy dominates one-stage optimization out of sample on this universe.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)   # 16 risky classes

Re <- R - rf                                                       # excess, @sec-03-return-risk-and-the-investor-theory

15.1 The Two-Stage Problem

15.1.1 Sleeves, and what is decided at each level

Partition the \(N\) assets into \(K\) sleeves and write the weight vector and the covariance matrix in the same blocks, \(\boldsymbol{w} = (\boldsymbol{w}_1', \ldots, \boldsymbol{w}_K')'\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = [\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{jk}]\). The one-stage, or flat, problem is that of Markowitz (1952) as stated in Section 9.1, over all \(N\) assets at once. The two-stage, or nested, problem solves each sleeve on its own block first,

\[ \boldsymbol{v}_k = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{v}} \; \boldsymbol{v}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{kk}\boldsymbol{v} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \boldsymbol{v}'\boldsymbol{\iota} = 1,\; \boldsymbol{v} \geq \boldsymbol{0}, \qquad k = 1, \ldots, K, \tag{15.1}\]

then treats each sleeve’s portfolio as a single asset with return \(r_{k,t} = \boldsymbol{v}_k'\boldsymbol{r}_{k,t}\) and allocates across the \(K\) sleeve returns,

\[ \boldsymbol{b} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{b}} \; \boldsymbol{b}'\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{b} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \boldsymbol{b}'\boldsymbol{\iota} = 1,\; \boldsymbol{b} \geq \boldsymbol{0}, \qquad \Omega_{jk} = \boldsymbol{v}_j'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{jk}\boldsymbol{v}_k . \tag{15.2}\]

The portfolio that results holds, in every class, the sleeve budget times the within-sleeve weight,

\[ \boldsymbol{w}_{\text{nest}} = (b_1 \boldsymbol{v}_1', \ldots, b_K \boldsymbol{v}_K')' . \tag{15.3}\]

The objectives in Equation 15.1 and Equation 15.2 need not be the same; a mandate can ask for minimum variance inside the bond sleeves and a tangency portfolio inside equities, and Section 15.3 does exactly that. What matters is the structure: nothing at stage 1 sees any asset outside its own sleeve, and stage 2 sees the sleeves only through \(K\) series.

Equation 15.3 is feasible for the flat problem, so in sample the nested portfolio can never have a lower variance than the flat minimum. The gap is the price of decentralization. Binsbergen et al. (2008) quantify it as a utility loss and show that it is not small when the sleeve managers optimize against their own objectives rather than the office’s; Elton and Gruber (2004) derive the instructions, a benchmark and a risk target for each sleeve, under which the decentralized solution coincides with the centralized one; and Blake et al. (2013) document the practice, in pension funds that split a balanced mandate into specialist sleeves, gained on sleeve performance and paid in coordination. That the top layer nonetheless carries most of the outcome is the finding of Brinson et al. (1986) and Brinson et al. (1991), which Ibbotson and Kaplan (2000) restate carefully: policy explains about ninety percent of a fund’s variation over time, far less of the variation between funds, and essentially all of the average level of return.

15.1.2 The shrinkage reading

Stage 1 of the nested problem uses only the diagonal blocks of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). It is therefore the flat problem solved on

\[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{B}} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11} & \boldsymbol{0} & \cdots \\ \boldsymbol{0} & \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22} & \\ \vdots & & \ddots \end{pmatrix}, \tag{15.4}\]

the matrix in which every cross-sleeve covariance has been set to zero. In the language of Section 7.1 that is a shrinkage estimator of the form of Equation 7.6 with a target that keeps the blocks and discards the rest, and an intensity of one on the off-block entries and zero elsewhere. The difference from Ledoit and Wolf (2004) is where the intensity comes from. Their \(\lambda\) minimizes expected loss and is estimated from the data; here it is imposed by the mandate, and the justification is economic rather than statistical: the cross-sleeve entries are the ones no sleeve manager is responsible for, and stage 1 declares them unknown. What is known about shrinkage since Stein (1956) transfers directly. Setting parameters to zero helps when their true values are near zero and their estimates are not, because those are the entries the error-maximizer of Michaud (1989) exploits, and hurts when it discards information the portfolio was supposed to use.

Stage 2 restores part of that information, in coarse form. Equation 15.2 uses the \(K \times K\) matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\), which carries \(K(K-1)/2\) cross-sleeve covariances in place of the \(\sum_{j<k} N_j N_k\) that were dropped, and carries them only for the sleeve compositions already fixed. If stage 2 ignores them as well, taking \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) to be diagonal, the top-level solution is inverse-variance weighting across sleeves and the whole two-stage procedure collapses to the flat minimum-variance portfolio of Equation 9.3 computed on \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{B}}\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{B}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota} = \big(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota},\, \ldots,\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{KK}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}\big)' \quad \Longrightarrow \quad w_i = \frac{\sigma_k^{-2}}{\sum_{j} \sigma_j^{-2}}\, v_{k,i} \;\; \text{for } i \in k, \qquad \sigma_k^{2} = \frac{1}{\boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{kk}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}} , \tag{15.5}\]

because the inverse of a block-diagonal matrix is block-diagonal, and \(\sigma_k^2\) is sleeve \(k\)’s own minimum variance. The long-only version separates the same way, given the sleeve budgets, and the worked box below checks it numerically. So the three constructions form a ladder. The flat problem uses every entry of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\); the nested problem uses the diagonal blocks plus \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\); the block-diagonal problem uses the diagonal blocks alone. Where nesting sits on that ladder depends on how much of the cross-block covariance is signal.

S    <- stats::cov(Re)

On the full sample the cross-sleeve entries carry 23% of the squared mass of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), and the average correlation between two series in different sleeves is 0.27 against 0.54 within a sleeve. The blocks are real; the space between them is not empty. Whether the estimate of that space is worth using is the question the out-of-sample race in Section 15.3 answers, and the answer is in keeping with Jagannathan and Ma (2003) rather than with the usual case for shrinkage: a restriction on the weights helps out of sample when it forbids what the estimate gets most wrong, and the cross-sleeve covariances of sixteen classes on sixty months are not that.

Everything in this chapter is the long-only quadratic program of Section 9.2, and the rolling loop of Section 15.3 solves it more than a thousand times, so we call quadprog directly, the solver ROI dispatches to in Section 13.2, and confirm once that the two agree.

gmv_qp <- function(S) {
  n   <- ncol(S)
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S, dvec = rep(0, n),
                            Amat = cbind(rep(1, n), diag(n)),
                            bvec = c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)
  w <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  setNames(w / sum(w), colnames(S))
}

gmv_spec <- function(assets) {
  portfolio.spec(assets = assets) |>
    add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
    add.constraint(type = "long_only") |>
    add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")
}

w_flat <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(Re, gmv_spec(colnames(Re)),

NoteWorked: hierarchical risk parity is a different hierarchy

López de Prado (2016) uses the word hierarchical for a construction that has no sleeves given to it. It estimates a tree from the correlation matrix, orders the assets along that tree so that similar ones are adjacent, and allocates by recursive bisection: split the ordered list in half, divide the budget between the halves in inverse proportion to their variances, and recurse. Nothing is inverted, which is why it was raised in Section 14.3 as the one popular method the package lacks, and why it is defined even when Equation 7.2 makes \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) singular. Raffinot (2018) examines the linkage and the cut; Mantegna (1999) is the origin of the correlation-distance tree in finance, and Tola et al. (2008) the first use of such a tree to filter a covariance matrix before optimizing on it.

D  <- sqrt(0.5 * (1 - stats::cor(Re)))                  # the correlation distance
hc <- hclust(stats::as.dist(D), method = "single")
ord <- colnames(Re)[hc$order]                            # quasi-diagonalization

hrp <- function(S, ord) {
  iv_var <- function(items) {                            # inverse-variance variance
    s <- S[items, items, drop = FALSE]; iv <- 1 / diag(s); iv <- iv / sum(iv)
    as.numeric(t(iv) %*% s %*% iv)
  }
  w <- setNames(rep(1, length(ord)), ord)
  clusters <- list(ord)
  while (length(clusters)) {
    nxt <- list()
    for (cl in clusters) {
      if (length(cl) < 2L) next
      h <- floor(length(cl) / 2); a <- cl[1:h]; b <- cl[(h + 1):length(cl)]
      alpha <- 1 - iv_var(a) / (iv_var(a) + iv_var(b))
      w[a] <- w[a] * alpha; w[b] <- w[b] * (1 - alpha)
      nxt <- c(nxt, list(a), list(b))
    }
    clusters <- nxt
  }
  w[colnames(S)]
}
w_hrp <- hrp(S, ord)
stopifnot(abs(sum(w_hrp) - 1) < 1e-12)

15.1.3 The class series is the sleeve’s benchmark

Each of the sixteen series is an index of the instruments inside it; the equity series are the capitalization-weighted portfolios that Section 21.1 verifies against the instrument level, and US_government stands in for a portfolio of Treasuries. That is what makes the top layer a policy: it is set on investable proxies, in the manner of Sharpe (1992), and the weights it produces are handed down as benchmarks. The sleeve manager then holds instruments and is judged, in Section 24.1, on the return in excess of that benchmark and the tracking error it took. The class series is the sleeve’s benchmark, not its result. Setting the policy on a manager’s realized returns would reward each sleeve for the past alpha it will not repeat, carry that selection into the backtest of Section 25.1, and leave nothing against which the manager could be judged; Roll (1977) is the older statement that the benchmark is a choice with consequences, and Grinold and Kahn (2000) the standard account of what is measured once it has been made.

15.1.4 Hedging is a sleeve decision

The sleeve structure also settles a question that has no answer at the level of the whole portfolio. Section 4.3 found that hedging a Japanese government bond removes most of its volatility and hedging Japanese equity removes almost none, and Campbell et al. (2010) show why the optimal hedge differs systematically between bonds and equities: currencies covary differently with each. A hedge is therefore a property of the sleeve rather than of the country, and it changes what a sleeve holds.

fx <- sim_fx_monthly
hedged <- R
for (a in colnames(R)) {
  reg <- sub("_.*$", "", a)
  if (reg %in% unique(fx$region)) {
    f <- fx$fx_return[fx$region == reg][match(zoo::index(R), fx$date[fx$region == reg])]
    stopifnot(!anyNA(f))
    hedged[, a] <- R[, a] - f                  # hedged = unhedged - fx, @sec-04
  }
}
He <- hedged - rf

sleeve_vol <- function(X, a) {
  w <- gmv_qp(stats::cov(X[, a]))
  c(vol = sqrt(as.numeric(t(w) %*% stats::cov(X[, a]) %*% w)) * sqrt(12),
    largest = max(w), bets = eff_bets(w))
}
hedge_tab <- dplyr::bind_rows(lapply(c("government", "credit", "equity"), function(k)
  tibble::tibble(sleeve = k,
                 unhedged = sleeve_vol(Re, sleeves[[k]])[["vol"]],
                 hedged   = sleeve_vol(He, sleeves[[k]])[["vol"]],
                 bets_unhedged = sleeve_vol(Re, sleeves[[k]])[["bets"]],
                 bets_hedged   = sleeve_vol(He, sleeves[[k]])[["bets"]])))
gov <- hedge_tab[hedge_tab$sleeve == "government", ]
stopifnot(gov$hedged < gov$unhedged, gov$bets_hedged > gov$bets_unhedged)

Hedging the government sleeve takes its minimum variance from 4.67% to 2.63% and spreads it from 1.2 to 2.1 effective positions, because foreign bonds become holdable once their currency is removed. Hedging the equity sleeve moves its volatility from 13.84% to 13.50%. Perold and Schulman (1988) and Black (1989) are the two classic and opposed general answers; the sleeve view makes the general answer unnecessary, and Section 22.1 takes the bond side of it up where the currency decision and the duration decision turn out not to be separable.

15.2 Implementation

PortfolioAnalytics has a data structure for this problem and almost no documentation of it. mult.portfolio.spec() wraps a top-level specification, add.sub.portfolio() attaches a sleeve specification together with the settings used to optimize it, and optimize.portfolio() recognizes the result. What it then does is not stated anywhere outside the source, so this section reads the source and checks it against a by-hand construction.

proxy.ret <- na.omit(do.call(cbind, ret))

after which optimize.portfolio() continues with R <- proxy.ret and portfolio <- portfolio$top.portfolio. Every sleeve is run through optimize.portfolio.rebalancing(), the rolling backtest of Section 25.2, not through a single optimization; the sleeve’s returns are the realized returns of that backtest; and the top level is optimized once, on the full history of those realized sleeve returns.

ImportantThe defaults do not run, and one argument is silently discarded

add.sub.portfolio() defaults rebalance_on to NULL, which optimize.portfolio.rebalancing() passes to xts::endpoints(), which fails with “missing value where TRUE/FALSE needed”. A mult.portfolio.spec built with the defaults cannot be optimized. Two further things about the settings. training_period = NULL becomes thirty-six observations. And the length of the window is trailing_periods, the argument add.sub.portfolio() names; passing rolling_window instead, the name the rebalancing function itself uses, looks as if it works and does not, because the sub-portfolio stores trailing_periods = NULL alongside it and the rebalancing function’s compatibility shim overwrites rolling_window with that NULL. The result is an expanding window that nothing reports. Both facts were established by reproducing the proxy series by hand, below.

15.2.3 The same thing by hand

W <- 60L
proxy_by_hand <- function(a) {
  X <- zoo::coredata(Re[, a, drop = FALSE]); n <- nrow(X); out <- numeric(n)
  w <- rep(1 / length(a), length(a))                  # the back-fill: equal, then drifting
  for (t in seq_len(W)) {
    out[t] <- sum(w * X[t, ]); w <- w * (1 + X[t, ]) / (1 + out[t])
  }
  for (t in (W + 1):n)                                # then rolling minimum variance
    out[t] <- sum(gmv_qp(stats::cov(X[(t - W):(t - 1), ])) * X[t, ])
  out
}

In sample the order is fixed by construction and Table 15.2 only measures the gaps. Nesting gives up 0.050 of Sharpe ratio against the flat solution and buys nothing visible in return: the nested portfolio holds 1.69 effective positions against the flat portfolio’s 1.47, with 76% in one series. The sleeve structure did not diversify the answer, because Equation 15.2 is still a minimum-variance problem and still buys the sleeve with the lowest variance; what changes that is a constraint on the budgets, which is Section 12.1’s subject and the sleeve cap 50% row of the opt.list above.

15.3.2 Out of sample

The claim that matters is the one Section 8.1 makes about every method in this book: a restriction that costs variance in sample can buy it back out of sample if what it forbids is mostly estimation error. We run the four constructions on a sixty-month rolling window, monthly, from the same first out-of-sample month as the register of output/tables/oos-sweep.csv, so that the flat line can be checked against it.

FIRST_OOS <- 121L                                # the register's first out-of-sample month
stopifnot(FIRST_OOS == which(model_year(zoo::index(Re)) == 11L)[1])   # first month of model year 11
oos    <- FIRST_OOS:nrow(Re)
rf_oos <- rf[oos]
Xm <- zoo::coredata(Re); Rm <- zoo::coredata(R)

race <- function(W) {
  paths <- lapply(oos, function(t) {
    X  <- Xm[(t - W):(t - 1), ]                    # the W months before t, nothing after
    Sw <- stats::cov(X)
    v  <- within_gmv(Sw, sleeves)
    sr <- sapply(names(v), function(k) X[, sleeves[[k]], drop = FALSE] %*% v[[k]])
    Sb <- Sw; Sb[!same] <- 0
    rbind(flat           = gmv_qp(Sw),
          nested         = unroll(gmv_qp(stats::cov(sr)), v),
          `block-diagonal` = gmv_qp(Sb),
          `equal weight` = rep(1 / N, N))
  })
  strategies <- rownames(paths[[1]])
  ret  <- sapply(strategies, function(s) vapply(seq_along(oos), function(i)
    sum(paths[[i]][s, ] * Rm[oos[i], ]), numeric(1)))
  # turnover against drifted end-of-period weights, as the register defines it
  turn <- sapply(strategies, function(s) {
    eop <- rep(0, N); out <- numeric(length(oos))
    for (i in seq_along(oos)) {
      w <- paths[[i]][s, ]; r <- Rm[oos[i], ]
      out[i] <- 0.5 * sum(abs(w - eop)); eop <- w * (1 + r) / (1 + sum(w * r))
    }
    mean(out) * 12
  })
  list(ret = ret, turnover = turn,
       sleeve_budget = sapply(seq_along(oos), function(i)
         tapply(paths[[i]]["nested", ], sleeve_of(colnames(Re)), sum)))
}

oos_stats <- function(r) {                       # the register's definitions
  n  <- length(r)
  ar <- prod(1 + r)^(12 / n) - 1
  af <- prod(1 + rf_oos)^(12 / n) - 1
  v  <- stats::sd(r) * sqrt(12)
  c(ann_return = ar, ann_vol = v, sharpe = (ar - af) / v)
}

The hierarchy does not dominate one-stage optimization out of sample on this universe. That is worth pausing on, because the nested rule estimates fewer covariances than the flat one, and the finding of DeMiguel et al. (2009) that the book has met at every turn is that rules which estimate less tend to do better out of sample. At sixty months the nested construction earns a Sharpe ratio of 0.593 against the flat portfolio’s 0.828, a gap of 0.235, and the flat portfolio also has the lower realized volatility, 4.13% against 4.22%. The nested portfolio finishes behind equal weighting as well. Shortening the window to thirty-six months, which makes every estimate worse and should favor the construction that estimates fewer covariances, does not reverse the order, and neither does lengthening it to one hundred and twenty. The block-diagonal construction trails the nested one at every window, which places the two on the ladder of Section 15.1: what stage 2 recovers through \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is worth 0.055 of Sharpe ratio at sixty months, and what stage 1 discards is worth more.

That is the shrinkage reading confirmed from the other side. Ledoit and Wolf (2004) shrink because the sample matrix is badly conditioned, and on sixteen series and sixty months it is not: Section 7.1 found no rank-deficient window in thirty-one model years at class level. The cross-sleeve covariances are 23% of the matrix and, at this dimension, mostly information. The case for a hierarchy that discards them is the case Section 7.2 made for holding fewer things, and it arrives when the flat matrix cannot be estimated at all, which is the situation of Section 21.1 and not of this chapter. Here the structure is a governance decision, and the table prices it.

Figure 15.3 shows the gap opening steadily rather than in any one episode: the largest share any named episode of sim_episodes contributes to it is 12%, in the rates shock. The sleeve budgets show why it does not close. Averaged over the out-of-sample period the nested construction gives the government sleeve 84% of the portfolio and the equity sleeve 13%. Inside the government sleeve, stage 1 chose the composition on government covariances alone; the flat problem chose its government holdings for how they sit against equities, and that is the part of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) stage 1 never sees. Turnover, which Section 23.3 prices, is 30% a year for the nested construction against 33% for the flat one, so costs do not change the ranking.

15.3.3 The policy view

A policy is not usually minimum variance everywhere. The structure of Equation 15.1 and Equation 15.2 lets each level carry its own objective, and the combination a mandate most often asks for is a tangency portfolio where returns are believed to be forecastable and minimum variance where they are not. The long-only tangency portfolio is one quadratic program, minimum variance at unit expected return, rescaled to a full budget; it is the point Equation 9.5 picks out, and rather than scanning a frontier for it as Section 9.2 does we solve it directly and check it against a scan of forty frontier points on the sleeve series.

tan_qp <- function(S, mu) {
  stopifnot(any(mu > 0))
  n   <- ncol(S)
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S, dvec = rep(0, n),
                            Amat = cbind(mu, diag(n)), bvec = c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)
  w <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  setNames(w / sum(w), colnames(S))
}

# the check: no long-only frontier portfolio has a higher Sharpe ratio

  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S_sl, dvec = rep(0, n_sl),
                            Amat = cbind(rep(1, n_sl), mu_sl, diag(n_sl)),
                            bvec = c(1, target, rep(0, n_sl)), meq = 2)
  sr_of(sol$solution)
}, numeric(1))

Table 15.4 is what a strategic asset allocation looks like as a document, the policy portfolio that Ang (2014) places at the center of institutional practice: 68% to the government sleeve, 29% to equity, and inside each a regional split the sleeve manager inherits. The equity manager of Section 21.1 receives the five regional weights in the equity rows as a benchmark and selects instruments against it; the fixed-income manager of Section 22.1 receives the government rows and a hedging instruction with them. The policy earns an in-sample Sharpe ratio of 0.954 against 0.991 for the flat tangency portfolio, and after Section 9.3 neither number should be read as a forecast. Key Takeaways

  • The nested problem of Equation 15.1 and Equation 15.2 is the structure every large allocator uses, and Sharpe (1981) posed the question it raises: what the top level must tell the sleeves so that the parts add up. In sample it can never dominate the flat problem, because Equation 15.3 is feasible for it.
  • Stage 1 is the flat problem on the block-diagonal matrix of Equation 15.4: a shrinkage estimator whose intensity is set by the mandate rather than estimated. With inverse-variance budgets at the top, the two constructions coincide exactly, by Equation 15.5.
  • mult.portfolio.spec() runs each sleeve through the rolling backtest of optimize.portfolio.rebalancing(), back-fills the training period with a drifting equal-weight sleeve, and optimizes the top level once on the result. Its defaults do not run, rolling_window is silently discarded in favor of trailing_periods, a one-asset sleeve cannot be optimized, and the proxy series are visible only with trace = TRUE.
  • On sixteen classes and a sixty-month window the hierarchy loses out of sample, at every window length tried, and the block-diagonal construction loses by more. At this dimension the cross-sleeve covariances are information rather than noise; the case for discarding them arrives at instrument level, where the flat matrix cannot be estimated.
  • The estimated hierarchy of hierarchical risk parity groups fixed income by currency rather than by class, which is the hedging decision of Campbell et al. (2010) seen from the data: a hedge is a property of the sleeve, and it changes what the bond sleeves hold while leaving the equity sleeve alone.
  • The class series is the sleeve’s benchmark. The policy is set on investable proxies and handed down; the sleeve manager is judged against it.
