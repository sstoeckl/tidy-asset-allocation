16  Signals and Return Forecasts

Draft|Code: script · exercises · solutions · all chapters|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

Can the excess return on an asset class be forecast, and does a forecast that fits the past help an investor who must act on it before the future arrives? Welch and Goyal (2008) put the first question to every predictor the literature had proposed for the equity premium and found that hardly any of them would have helped an investor in real time: the regressions fit in sample and failed out of sample. Campbell and Thompson (2008) replied that the same predictors do help, by a small but economically meaningful margin, once the forecast is constrained by what theory says its sign must be, and between them the two papers fixed the terms on which every later claim of predictability is judged: an out-of-sample \(R^2\) against the historical mean, and the certainty-equivalent gain of an investor who uses the forecast.

This is the first tactical chapter of the book in the sense of Section 3.1: the inputs are conditioned on information available at the time rather than estimated unconditionally, and the question is whether the conditioning is worth anything. We first set out the predictive regression and its two \(R^2\) statistics, the test of one forecast against another, and the economic-value criterion; then say which signals this universe can carry and build them from the shipped data, momentum from the return histories and the term spread, the credit spread and a turbulence index from the bond curve factors and the cross-section; then test each signal in sample and out of sample, one at a time and combined through an elastic net; and finally scale the forecasts into a monthly tilt around the equal-weight policy and price it after costs.

The data are the sixteen risky class series of Section 2.2, in excess of the Treasury bill for the reasons of Section 3.1, and the assertion that the bill’s dates are the return dates is the first of several in this chapter that exist because a signal one month out of place produces a plausible table.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)   # sixteen risky classes
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return                                  # the bill
Re <- R - rf                                                       # excess returns
N  <- ncol(Re); dates <- zoo::index(R)
stopifnot(N == 16L, !anyNA(Re), all(sim_riskfree_monthly$date == dates))

16.1 Predictability, In Sample and Out

16.1.1 The predictive regression

Write \(r^e_{t+1}\) for the excess return on one series over month \(t+1\) and \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) for a vector of \(K\) signals observed by the end of month \(t\). The predictive regression is

\[ r^e_{t+1} = a + \boldsymbol{b}'\boldsymbol{x}_t + \varepsilon_{t+1}, \tag{16.1}\]

and its in-sample \(R^2\) is the share of the variance of \(r^e\) that the fitted values account for over the whole sample,

\[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t}\hat{\varepsilon}_{t+1}^2} {\sum_{t}\left(r^e_{t+1} - \bar{r}^e\right)^2}, \tag{16.2}\]

with \(\bar{r}^e\) the full-sample mean. Three things about Equation 16.1 are settled before any signal is chosen. The dating is the whole of the discipline: \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) must be known when the position for \(t+1\) is taken, and Section 16.2 asserts that alignment in code rather than trusting it. The \(R^2\) will be small, because Section 4.1 showed, following Merton (1980), that a mean is known only to within \(\sigma/\sqrt{\tau}\) over \(\tau\) years, and a conditional mean that moves with \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) is a fraction of a number that is itself barely visible: one percent a month is a strong result in this literature. And the slope is biased when the signal is persistent and its innovations are correlated with the return’s, as for every valuation ratio and most yields; Stambaugh (1999) derived the bias and Cochrane (2008) showed that the same persistence constrains what the joint system of return and signal can say. Inference uses the Newey and West (1987) standard errors of Equation 5.5, and with sixteen series and four signals the sixty-four regressions below are a multiple-testing problem of the kind Harvey et al. (2016) describe, so a \(t\)-statistic of two begins a case rather than ending one.

16.1.2 The out-of-sample \(R^2\)

An in-sample fit estimates \(a\) and \(\boldsymbol{b}\) on the whole history and scores the fitted values on the same history. An investor cannot do that. At the end of each month \(t\) she has the data through \(t\), estimates Equation 16.1 on them, and forecasts \(\hat{r}^e_{t+1}\); the alternative that uses no signal is the historical mean through \(t\), \(\bar{r}^e_t\), the strategic input of Section 6.1 re-estimated each month. Campbell and Thompson (2008) score the two sequences of forecasts against each other over the out-of-sample months \(t = T_0, \dots, T-1\) by

\[ R^2_{\text{OS}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t=T_0}^{T-1}\left(r^e_{t+1} - \hat{r}^e_{t+1}\right)^2} {\sum_{t=T_0}^{T-1}\left(r^e_{t+1} - \bar{r}^e_t\right)^2}, \tag{16.3}\]

which is positive when the signal’s forecast errors are smaller than the mean’s and negative when they are larger. Unlike Equation 16.2 it has no floor at zero, and a negative value is the common case: Welch and Goyal (2008) report it for most predictors over most periods, Goyal et al. (2024) find on data through 2021 that almost nothing proposed since fares better, and McLean and Pontiff (2016) show that the cross-sectional predictors which did work decay after publication. The burn-in \(T_0\) is a choice, and the window can roll or expand; an expanding window uses everything the investor knows, and it is what we use. Campbell and Thompson (2008) also restricted the forecast by theory, setting a negative forecast of the equity premium to zero, and found that the restriction turns most of their negative \(R^2_{\text{OS}}\) positive; exercise 2 applies it here.

16.1.3 Testing one forecast against another

Two forecasts of the same series are compared on their squared errors. Write \(e^0_{t+1} = r^e_{t+1} - \bar{r}^e_t\) for the mean’s error, \(e^1_{t+1}\) for the model’s, and \(d_{t+1} = (e^0_{t+1})^2 - (e^1_{t+1})^2\) for the loss differential. Diebold and Mariano (1995) observed that under the null of equal accuracy \(d\) has mean zero, so the test is a \(t\)-test on its mean with a standard error that allows the losses to be serially correlated and heteroskedastic,

\[ \text{DM} = \frac{\bar{d}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bar{d})}}, \tag{16.4}\]

with the variance estimated as in Equation 5.5, asymptotically standard normal. When the two models are nested, as a predictive regression and the historical mean are, the larger model estimates parameters whose true value is zero under the null and pays for them in forecast variance, so its squared errors exceed the mean’s even when the signal is useless and Equation 16.4 is biased against it. Clark and West (2007) correct this by adding back the term the estimation noise contributes,

\[ \text{CW} = \frac{\overline{d_{t+1} + \left(\bar{r}^e_t - \hat{r}^e_{t+1}\right)^2}} {\sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\cdot)}}, \tag{16.5}\]

read against the normal distribution as a one-sided test. It is the statistic reported below whenever a signal is set against the mean, with Equation 16.4 beside it to show what the correction changes.

16.1.4 Why a small \(R^2\) can matter

An \(R^2_{\text{OS}}\) of half a percent looks like nothing, and it is not. Kandel and Stambaugh (1996) showed that the decision of a mean-variance investor responds to a predictive relation far weaker than any statistical test would accept, because what the investor cares about is not the fit but the position the forecast changes. The link is short. Under Equation 3.12 an investor with risk aversion \(\gamma\) facing one risky asset with conditional mean \(\mu_t\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) holds \(w_t = \mu_t / (\gamma\sigma^2)\) and earns an expected excess return of \(\mathbb{E}[\mu_t^2]/(\gamma\sigma^2)\). Using the unconditional mean \(\mu\) instead earns \(\mu^2/(\gamma\sigma^2) = S^2/\gamma\), with \(S = \mu/\sigma\) the Sharpe ratio; the difference is \(\operatorname{Var}(\mu_t)/(\gamma\sigma^2)\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(\mu_t)/\sigma^2\) is the \(R^2\) of the forecast. So, as Campbell and Thompson (2008) write it,

\[ \Delta\,\mathbb{E}[r^e] \approx \frac{R^2_{\text{OS}}}{\gamma}, \qquad \frac{\Delta\,\mathbb{E}[r^e]}{S^2/\gamma} \approx \frac{R^2_{\text{OS}}}{S^2}. \tag{16.6}\]

The proportional gain is the \(R^2\) relative to the squared Sharpe ratio, and monthly Sharpe ratios are small numbers. Take \(R^2_{\text{OS}} = 0.5\%\) a month, an annual Sharpe ratio of \(0.5\) so that \(S = 0.5/\sqrt{12} = 0.144\) and \(S^2 = 0.0208\), and \(\gamma = 5\): the proportional gain is \(0.005 / 0.0208 = 24%\) of the unconditional expected excess return, and in return units \(0.005 / 5 = 10\) basis points a month, 1.2 percentage points a year. That is what a predictive \(R^2\) no referee would find impressive is worth to an investor who acts on it without constraint, and it is why in-sample significance and out-of-sample profit are different questions: the first asks whether \(\boldsymbol{b}\) is distinguishable from zero, the second whether acting on \(\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}\), estimated with error and passed through a position rule and a cost model, beats not acting. Pesaran and Timmermann (1995) made the same point with switching rules run in real time.

The criterion that answers the second question is the certainty equivalent of Section 3.1. For a strategy whose realized excess returns have mean \(\hat{\mu}_p\) and variance \(\hat{\sigma}^2_p\) over the out-of-sample months,

\[ \text{CE}_p = \hat{\mu}_p - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\hat{\sigma}^2_p, \qquad \Delta = \text{CE}_{\text{forecast}} - \text{CE}_{\text{mean}}, \tag{16.7}\]

is the riskless excess return the investor would accept in place of each, and \(\Delta\) is the fee she would pay to switch from the strategy built on the mean to the one built on the forecast. It is computed from realized returns, gross or net of costs, and it decides Section 16.3. What it does not do is score the forecast itself, independently of a position rule. That measure, the risk-adjusted forecast error that Section 8.3 introduced and Section 26.1 derives, expresses a forecast’s error as the Sharpe ratio it costs (Salcher et al. 2026); whether a forecaster should be trained on that loss rather than on squared error is the question of Section 27.1.

16.1.5 Which signals this universe can carry

The literature’s signals fall into three families, and the shipped data support two of them.

Momentum needs nothing but the return history. Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) documented that stocks which rose over the past three to twelve months continue to rise over the next, and the construction that became standard skips the most recent month to avoid short-term reversal: the signal at \(t\) is the return over months \(t-11\) to \(t-1\),

\[ \text{mom}_{i,t} = \prod_{k=1}^{11}\left(1 + R_{i,t-k}\right) - 1 . \tag{16.8}\]

Moskowitz et al. (2012) showed that the sign of an asset’s own past twelve-month excess return forecasts its next month across fifty-eight futures on equity indices, bonds, currencies and commodities, the asset-class level of this book rather than the stock level, and Asness et al. (2013) found value and momentum premia in every class and region they examined, negatively correlated with each other. Value is the family this universe cannot support: it needs a fundamental, a book value or an earnings stream, that the market does not carry, so the chapter builds momentum without its partner and the value side waits for Section 21.1.

Macro state signals are the family Welch and Goyal (2008) tested: the dividend yield, the term spread, the default spread, the short rate, inflation. Fama and French (1989) showed that the term spread and the default spread forecast the excess returns on stocks and bonds together, tracking business conditions, and both are in the shipped data. sim_curve_weekly, described in Section 2.2, carries the level, slope and curvature of a Nelson and Siegel (1987) curve per region in the dynamic form of Diebold and Li (2006), plus an investment-grade and a high-yield credit spread per region. In that parametrization the slope factor is the short end minus the long end, so the term spread of Fama and French (1989) is minus the slope, and the IG spread is their default spread. These are the same predictors, built inside the universe: the bond block was simulated from them, so a signal that forecasts the bond and credit series here is forecasting the state that generated them, the state Section 22.1 decomposes the bond returns on. Whether it forecasts the equity series is the open question.

Cross-sectional dispersion is the third family, and it is the one Section 5.3 already built. The turbulence index of Chow et al. (1999) and Kritzman and Li (2010), the Mahalanobis distance of a month’s return vector from its trailing mean in Equation 5.6, measures how unusual the pattern across the sixteen series is; Stöckl (2022b) show that dispersion measured within a cross-section forecasts the premia earned on it, and Stöckl (2022a) link the same construction to parameter uncertainty and to the aggregate premium. It is a signal about the whole market rather than about one series, so it enters every regression with the same value and a series-specific slope.

The real-world versions of the macro signals are one call away for a reader with a network connection: tidyfinance serves the Welch and Goyal (2008) predictor file, whose tms and dfy columns are the term and default spreads built below; the dates are any range of the predictor file’s own, real calendar.

gw <- tidyfinance::download_data_macro_predictors(
  dataset = "macro_predictors_monthly",
  start_date = "2000-01-01", end_date = "2020-12-31")   # an illustrative range
gw[, c("date", "tms", "dfy", "dp")]

Nothing below depends on it; every signal is built from the shipped data so that the chapter reproduces offline.

16.2 Building and Testing the Signals

16.2.1 Signals at month end, lagged once

Momentum first. Equation 16.8 is a ratio of two lagged values of the total-return index, and the assertion checks it against the product it is meant to be.

P   <- cumprod(1 + R)                                   # total-return index
mom <- xts::lag.xts(P, 1) / xts::lag.xts(P, 12) - 1     # months t-11 .. t-1; month t skipped
k <- 100L
stopifnot(all.equal(as.numeric(mom[k, ]),
                    unname(apply(1 + zoo::coredata(R)[(k - 11):(k - 1), ], 2, prod) - 1)))

The curve is weekly and the returns are monthly, so each month end takes the last weekly observation on or before it, never after. The sign convention is then checked against the data rather than assumed: the short rate in the Diebold and Li (2006) parametrization is level plus slope, so level minus term spread must track the bill.

cv <- sim_curve_weekly |>
  dplyr::filter(series %in% c("US_Level", "US_Slope", "US_IG_Spread", "US_HY_Spread")) |>
  tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = series, values_from = value) |>
  dplyr::arrange(date)
i_last <- findInterval(dates, cv$date)          # last weekly row on or before each month end
# Weekly dates are six to eight days apart, so the matched week is at most
# seven days before the month end and never after it.
stopifnot(all(cv$date[i_last] <= dates), all(dates - cv$date[i_last] < 8))

curve <- xts::xts(cbind(level = cv$US_Level[i_last],
                        term  = -cv$US_Slope[i_last],           # long minus short, in pp
                        ig    = 100 * cv$US_IG_Spread[i_last],  # pp
                        hy    = 100 * cv$US_HY_Spread[i_last]),
                  order.by = dates)

cor_short <- stats::cor(as.numeric(curve$level - curve$term),
                        100 * sim_riskfree_monthly$rate_annual)
stopifnot(cor_short > 0.9)
round(cor_short, 3)
#> [1] 0.975

The correlation between the curve’s implied short rate and the bill is 0.975, and the assertion would have failed the render at a sign error. The turbulence index is Section 5.3’s call, unchanged: a sixty-month trailing window, so the index for month \(t\) is computed from the moments of months \(t-60\) to \(t-1\) and the return vector of \(t\) itself.

turb <- uncertaintymeasures::OSturbulence(R, rolling = TRUE, roll.obs = 60)$turb
colnames(turb) <- "turb"
common <- merge(curve, turb)
stopifnot(identical(zoo::index(common), dates))

Every signal now sits at the month end it was observed. Shifting the whole block forward by one month puts the signal observed at \(t\) on the row of the return it predicts, \(t+1\), and the assertion states that the shift did what it was meant to do.

common_lag <- xts::lag.xts(common, 1)
mom_lag    <- xts::lag.xts(mom, 1)
rev_lag    <- xts::lag.xts(Re, 1)                       # last month's own excess return
stopifnot(all.equal(as.numeric(common_lag[k + 1, ]), as.numeric(common[k, ])),
          all.equal(as.numeric(mom_lag[k + 1, ]),    as.numeric(mom[k, ])),
          is.na(common_lag[1, "term"]))

# For one series: the excess return in month m beside every signal as it stood
# at the end of m - 1. This is the frame @eq-16-predictive is estimated on.
pairs_of <- function(s) {
  data.frame(date = dates, y = as.numeric(Re[, s]),
             mom = as.numeric(mom_lag[, s]), rev = as.numeric(rev_lag[, s]),
             zoo::coredata(common_lag))
}
first_row <- function(v) min(which(!is.na(pairs_of("US_equity")[[v]])))
c(momentum = first_row("mom"), curve = first_row("term"), turbulence = first_row("turb"))
#>   momentum      curve turbulence 
#>         14          2         61

The first row carries no signal, the momentum column begins in month 14 and turbulence, which needs sixty months of history, in month 61.

16.2.2 In sample: sixty-four regressions

Each series is regressed on each of the four signals over the whole sample, one signal at a time, with the standard errors of Equation 5.5 at six lags as in Section 5.2.

sig4 <- c(mom = "12-1 momentum", term = "term spread", ig = "IG spread", turb = "turbulence")

nw_t <- function(m, j = 2L) {
  nw <- sandwich::NeweyWest(m, lag = 6, prewhite = FALSE)
  unname(stats::coef(m)[j] / sqrt(nw[j, j]))
}

ins <- lapply(colnames(Re), function(s) {
  d <- pairs_of(s)
  lapply(names(sig4), function(v) {
    dd <- d[!is.na(d[[v]]), ]
    m  <- stats::lm(y ~ x, data = data.frame(y = dd$y, x = dd[[v]]))
    tibble::tibble(series = s, signal = v, slope = unname(stats::coef(m)[2]),
                   t = nw_t(m), r2 = summary(m)$r.squared, n = nrow(dd))
  }) |> dplyr::bind_rows()
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

ins_sum <- ins |>
  dplyr::group_by(signal) |>
  dplyr::summarise(positive = sum(slope > 0), t_above_2 = sum(abs(t) > 2),
                   t_above_3 = sum(abs(t) > 3), median_r2 = median(r2), max_r2 = max(r2))
ins_sum
#> # A tibble: 4 × 6
#>   signal positive t_above_2 t_above_3 median_r2  max_r2
#>   <chr>     <int>     <int>     <int>     <dbl>   <dbl>
#> 1 ig           14         5         0   0.00420 0.0520 
#> 2 mom           8         0         0   0.00129 0.00474
#> 3 term         12         7         0   0.00915 0.0214 
#> 4 turb         11         1         0   0.00299 0.0186

Read Table 16.1 by column. The term spread is the strongest signal in the table: its slope is positive for 12 of the sixteen series, among them every one of the 10 bond and credit series, 7 of its \(t\)-statistics exceed two and none exceeds three, the largest being CH_government at 2.93. That is Fama and French (1989) on modeled data: a steep curve forecasts high excess returns on bonds. The IG spread is second, with 5 above two, again on the credit and bond series where the state was built in. Turbulence clears two on 1 series and its median \(R^2\) is 0.30%. Momentum clears two nowhere: its slope is negative for 3 of the five equity series, positive for the government series, and its largest \(R^2\) is 0.47%. The premium Moskowitz et al. (2012) document in futures is not in these class indices at a monthly horizon; they found it across fifty-eight instruments with a sign rule, not one series at a time with a slope. The largest \(R^2\) anywhere in the table is 5.2%, which by Equation 16.6 is not a small number. The question is whether any of it is available in real time.

16.2.3 Out of sample: an expanding window from model year 11

The out-of-sample scheme is an expanding window with a burn-in, and rsample::rolling_origin() describes the same sequence of splits in the vocabulary of tidymodels, so the count of splits is asserted to match.

burn <- 120L
oos  <- seq.int(burn + 1L, nrow(Re))       # forecast months 121 .. 372
stopifnot(model_year(dates[oos[1]]) == 11L)   # the register's out-of-sample start

ols_fc <- function(d, v, m) {
  tr <- which(!is.na(d[[v]]) & seq_len(nrow(d)) < m)          # strictly before m
  b  <- stats::lm.fit(cbind(1, d[[v]][tr]), d$y[tr])$coefficients
  unname(b[1] + b[2] * d[[v]][m])
}

splits <- rsample::rolling_origin(data.frame(m = seq_len(nrow(Re))),
                                  initial = burn, assess = 1, cumulative = TRUE)
stopifnot(nrow(splits) == length(oos))
c(forecast_months = length(oos), first = fmt_model(dates[oos[1]]))
#>            forecast_months                      first 
#>                      "252" "January of model year 11"

The burn-in is the first 120 months; forecasts begin in January of model year 11 and run to the end of the sample, 252 months, which are the months the register of Section 25.3 scores. At each month \(m\) in that range the regression is fit on every pair dated before \(m\) where the signal exists, the forecast is made for \(m\), and the historical mean of the excess return through \(m-1\) is recorded beside it.

One signal at a time is what Welch and Goyal (2008) tested and what a practitioner rarely does. With seven predictors per series, the four above plus last month’s return, the high-yield spread and the level of the curve, least squares on sixty to three hundred months fits noise, and the remedy is to shrink. The elastic net of Zou and Hastie (2005) minimizes the squared error plus a penalty \(\lambda\left[\alpha\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_1 + (1-\alpha)\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_2^2/2\right]\), which at \(\alpha = 1/2\) both shrinks coefficients toward zero and sets some exactly to zero; Gu et al. (2020) use it as the first of their machine-learning forecasters for the cross-section of stock returns, and Coqueret and Guida (2020) give the algorithm and its glmnet implementation, which we do not reproduce. What matters here is the tuning, because it is where the future leaks in: \(\lambda\) is chosen by five-fold cross-validation on contiguous blocks of the training window, in time order rather than shuffled, re-tuned once a year, and the coefficients are refit every month on the data through \(m-1\). Two values of \(\lambda\) are kept, the one that minimizes cross-validated error and the most penalized one within a standard error of it, because the choice turns out to matter.

en_vars <- c("mom", "rev", "term", "ig", "hy", "level", "turb")

enet_fc <- function(d) {
  ok  <- stats::complete.cases(d[, en_vars])
  lam <- c(NA_real_, NA_real_)
  t(vapply(oos, function(m) {
    tr <- which(ok & seq_len(nrow(d)) < m)
    X  <- as.matrix(d[tr, en_vars]); y <- d$y[tr]
    if ((m - oos[1]) %% 12L == 0L) {                      # re-tune once a year
      fold <- ceiling(seq_along(tr) / (length(tr) / 5))   # five contiguous blocks
      cvf  <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(X, y, alpha = 0.5, foldid = fold)
      lam <<- c(cvf$lambda.min, cvf$lambda.1se)
    }
    fit <- glmnet::glmnet(X, y, alpha = 0.5)
    as.numeric(stats::predict(fit, newx = as.matrix(d[m, en_vars]), s = lam))
  }, numeric(2)))
}

The loop below produces, for every series and every out-of-sample month, the historical mean, the four single-signal forecasts, their equal-weighted combination, which Rapach et al. (2010) showed is more stable than any of its parts, and the two elastic-net forecasts.

fc <- lapply(colnames(Re), function(s) {
  d <- pairs_of(s)
  out <- data.frame(date = dates[oos], series = s, y = d$y[oos],
                    mean = vapply(oos, function(m) mean(d$y[seq_len(m - 1L)]), numeric(1)))
  for (v in names(sig4)) out[[v]] <- vapply(oos, function(m) ols_fc(d, v, m), numeric(1))
  out$comb <- rowMeans(out[, names(sig4)])
  en <- enet_fc(d); out$enet_min <- en[, 1]; out$enet_1se <- en[, 2]
  out
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()
stopifnot(nrow(fc) == N * length(oos), !anyNA(fc))

Equation 16.3, Equation 16.5 and Equation 16.4 are then three short functions.

models <- c(names(sig4), "comb", "enet_min", "enet_1se")
model_label <- c(sig4, comb = "combination",
                 enet_min = "elastic net, lambda.min", enet_1se = "elastic net, lambda.1se")

r2_os <- function(y, f, f0) 1 - sum((y - f)^2) / sum((y - f0)^2)
cw_t  <- function(y, f, f0) nw_t(stats::lm(I((y - f0)^2 - (y - f)^2 + (f0 - f)^2) ~ 1), 1L)
dm_t  <- function(y, f, f0) nw_t(stats::lm(I((y - f0)^2 - (y - f)^2) ~ 1), 1L)

oos_tbl <- fc |>
  tidyr::pivot_longer(dplyr::all_of(models), names_to = "model", values_to = "f") |>
  dplyr::group_by(series, model) |>
  dplyr::summarise(r2os = r2_os(y, f, mean), cw = cw_t(y, f, mean), dm = dm_t(y, f, mean),
                   .groups = "drop") |>
  dplyr::mutate(model = factor(model, levels = models))

oos_sum <- oos_tbl |>
  dplyr::group_by(model) |>
  dplyr::summarise(positive = sum(r2os > 0), median_r2os = median(r2os), best = max(r2os),
                   cw_above = sum(cw > 1.645), dm_above = sum(dm > 1.645))

Three findings, in decreasing order of comfort.

The term spread survives. Its \(R^2_{\text{OS}}\) is positive on 10 of the sixteen series, its median is +0.52%, and it beats the mean by the Clark-West test on 6 of them, with CH_credit at +2.5%. The uncorrected statistic of Equation 16.4 finds 0, which is the nesting bias of Clark and West (2007) in one column: the regression carries two estimated parameters whose noise the mean does not pay for, and without the correction that noise is scored against the signal. The IG spread, second in sample, keeps a positive \(R^2_{\text{OS}}\) on 3 series and turbulence on 1; the in-sample fit of the first was concentrated in credit and of the second in a few crisis months, and an expanding window estimated before those months cannot see either. Turbulence’s worst case is JP_equity at -8.1%.

The combination helps and does not rescue. Averaging the four forecasts, the rule of Rapach et al. (2010), is positive on 7 series with a median of -0.11%, between its best and its worst constituent, which is what an average does.

The elastic net loses. At the cross-validated minimum it beats the mean on 1 series with a median \(R^2_{\text{OS}}\) of -1.95%; at the more penalized lambda.1se the median rises to -0.57% and the count to 5, still short of the single term spread. This is what Gu et al. (2020) report for the same model on the cross-section of stocks, where the elastic net is the weakest of their forecasters and its gains come from thousands of stocks and hundreds of signals, not from seven signals on sixteen series. The more the model is allowed to fit, the more it fits the burn-in’s noise, and the rule that shrinks hardest is the one that comes closest to the mean it is trying to beat. The lesson of Section 8.1, that the cost of an input is set by how well it can be estimated, applies to a conditional mean as it applied to an unconditional one.

16.3 The Economic Value of a Tilt

16.3.1 A tilt around the policy

The strategic policy is the equal-weight portfolio of Equation 8.3, built through the package’s own equal.weight() as in Section 8.2. It is the anchor because it estimates nothing, so that every difference between it and the tilted portfolio is attributable to the forecast, and because the constrained minimum-variance portfolio of Section 9.2 holds most of its weight in one series and leaves no room to tilt.

base_spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only")
w_policy <- extractWeights(equal.weight(Re, base_spec))[colnames(Re)]
stopifnot(all.equal(unname(w_policy), rep(1 / N, N)))

The tilt converts a forecast into a weight in three steps. The forecast for month \(t+1\) is divided by the series’ trailing sixty-month volatility, both known at \(t\), so that a forecast of one percent means something different on a government bond than on an equity market; the cross-sectional mean is removed, because under full investment only a relative view can be expressed; and the result is scaled by \(\kappa\) and clipped to a band of \(\pm\delta\) around the policy weight,

\[ s_{i,t} = \frac{\hat{r}^e_{i,t+1}}{\hat{\sigma}_{i,t}} - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\hat{r}^e_{j,t+1}}{\hat{\sigma}_{j,t}}, \qquad w_{i,t} = w^{\text{policy}}_i + \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\kappa\,s_{i,t},\,-\delta,\,\delta\right), \tag{16.9}\]

re-centered so that the weights sum to one. With \(\delta = 1/(2N)\) no weight can fall below zero or above three halves of the policy weight, and \(\kappa\) is set so that a forecast Sharpe ratio of one half a year fills the band. The weights for month \(t+1\) are set at the end of month \(t\) from forecasts made at \(t\), which is the dating Equation 16.1 was built on.

vol60 <- zoo::rollapply(Re, 60, stats::sd, align = "right", fill = NA)
band  <- 0.5 / N
kappa <- band / (0.5 / sqrt(12))              # a forecast Sharpe ratio of 0.5 a year fills the band

tilt_weights <- function(model) {
  F <- fc |> dplyr::select(date, series, f = dplyr::all_of(model)) |>
    tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = series, values_from = f)
  Fm <- as.matrix(F[, colnames(Re)])
  stopifnot(identical(colnames(Fm), colnames(Re)), nrow(Fm) == length(oos))
  W <- t(vapply(seq_len(nrow(Fm)), function(i) {
    m  <- oos[i]
    s  <- Fm[i, ] / as.numeric(vol60[m - 1L, ])       # forecast in volatility units, known at m - 1
    s  <- s - mean(s)                                  # relative view
    tl <- pmin(pmax(kappa * s, -band), band)
    w  <- w_policy + tl - mean(tl)
    w  <- pmax(w, 0); w / sum(w)
  }, numeric(N)))
  dimnames(W) <- list(as.character(dates[oos]), colnames(Re))
  stopifnot(all(abs(rowSums(W) - 1) < 1e-12), all(W >= 0))
  W
}

W_policy <- matrix(1 / N, length(oos), N, dimnames = list(as.character(dates[oos]), colnames(Re)))
W_term   <- tilt_weights("term")
c(mean_abs_tilt = mean(abs(W_term - 1 / N)),
  share_at_band = mean(abs(abs(W_term - 1 / N) - band) < 1e-9))
#> mean_abs_tilt share_at_band 
#>       0.01526       0.00000

The term-spread tilt moves the average weight by 1.5 percentage points against a band of 3.1, and the band binds on none of the weights. The forecasts are modest and so is the tilt.

NoteapplyFUN() and trailingFUN() do not roll anything

The two exported utilities whose names suggest they were written for this chapter were not. applyFUN(R, weights, FUN) evaluates one risk or return function, by name, for a weight vector or for every row of a weight matrix on the whole of R; it is the helper behind chart.Scatter(), which uses it to place random portfolios on the risk-return plane. trailingFUN(R, weights, n, FUN, FUNargs) evaluates FUN once on the last n + 1 rows of R, not the last n, and returns one number. Neither produces a time series. Rolling estimation in this book is zoo::rollapply(), as in Section 5.3 and the vol60 line above, or the explicit loop of enet_fc(). Both are run here so that the behavior is measured rather than described.

# applyFUN: one metric, many static weight vectors, the full return history.
applyFUN(Re, weights = rbind(policy = w_policy, average_tilt = colMeans(W_term)),
         FUN = "StdDev")
#> [1] 0.02326 0.02322

16.3.2 Rebalancing, turnover and costs

The portfolio is rebalanced monthly. Its accounting is written out rather than handed to Return.portfolio(), because that function back-fills equal weights before the first supplied weight vector, the trap Section 23.2 documents, and because the drifted weights are needed for the cost. By the end of month \(t+1\) each weight has drifted to \(w^{-}_{i,t+1} = w_{i,t}(1 + R_{i,t+1}) / (1 + r_{p,t+1})\); the trade back to the target has the turnover of Equation 23.1 and costs what Equation 23.2 charges, at the per-class one-way rates of pm_costs_default() that Section 23.3 uses on the register, mapped to each series by its class suffix.

cost_rate <- pm_costs_default()[sub(".*_", "", colnames(Re))]
stopifnot(!anyNA(cost_rate)); names(cost_rate) <- colnames(Re)

run_path <- function(W) {
  Rm <- zoo::coredata(R)[oos, colnames(Re), drop = FALSE]
  stopifnot(identical(colnames(W), colnames(Re)), nrow(W) == nrow(Rm))
  r       <- rowSums(W * Rm)                                  # month t+1, weights set at t
  drifted <- W * (1 + Rm) / (1 + r)                            # weights before the trade
  trade   <- abs(W[-1, , drop = FALSE] - drifted[-nrow(W), , drop = FALSE])
  tibble::tibble(date = dates[oos], gross = r,
                 turnover = c(rowSums(trade) / 2, 0),
                 cost     = c(as.numeric(trade %*% cost_rate), 0)) |>
    dplyr::mutate(net = gross - cost)
}

# Sharpe ratio as the register computes it: compounded, in excess of the bill.
sharpe_realized <- function(r, rf) {
  n <- length(r)
  (prod(1 + r)^(12 / n) - prod(1 + rf)^(12 / n)) / (stats::sd(r) * sqrt(12))
}
gamma <- 5
summarise_path <- function(p, label) {
  rf_o <- rf[oos]
  ce <- function(r) { e <- r - rf_o; 12 * mean(e) - gamma / 2 * 12 * stats::var(e) }
  tibble::tibble(
    portfolio    = label,
    ret          = prod(1 + p$gross)^(12 / nrow(p)) - 1,
    vol          = stats::sd(p$gross) * sqrt(12),
    sharpe_gross = sharpe_realized(p$gross, rf_o),
    turnover     = 12 * mean(p$turnover),
    cost_drag    = 12 * mean(p$cost),
    sharpe_net   = sharpe_realized(p$net, rf_o),
    max_dd       = as.numeric(PerformanceAnalytics::maxDrawdown(xts::xts(p$net, order.by = p$date))),
    ce_net       = ce(p$net))
}

tilts <- c(mom = "momentum", term = "term spread", comb = "combination",
           enet_min = "elastic net, lambda.min", enet_1se = "elastic net, lambda.1se")
paths <- c(list(policy = run_path(W_policy)),
           lapply(names(tilts), function(k) run_path(tilt_weights(k))) |> setNames(names(tilts)))
res <- dplyr::bind_rows(Map(summarise_path, paths, c("equal weight (policy)", unname(tilts)))) |>
  dplyr::mutate(ce_gain = ce_net - ce_net[1])

16.3.3 Reading the result

Of the five forecasts, 2 leave the investor better off after costs, and only one of them by more than a few basis points: the term spread, the one signal that survived Table 16.2. Its tilt earns a net Sharpe ratio of 0.650 against the policy’s 0.622, a certainty-equivalent gain of 25 basis points a year at \(\gamma = 5\), with a slightly shallower drawdown and turnover of 21% against 14%, which the per-class rates price at 5 basis points a year. The combination is a wash at +5 basis points. Momentum loses 21 basis points, and the least-penalized elastic net turns the portfolio over 67% a year, almost 5 times the policy’s, to lose 20 net. A model that fits noise trades on it, and Section 23.3 shows the same monotone relation between how much a strategy estimates and how much it pays to trade.

The gain that exists is small against its own uncertainty. The standard error of an annualized Sharpe ratio estimated over 252 months is, by the formula of Lo (2002) under independence, about 0.22; the term tilt’s advantage of 0.028 is well inside it, and the paired test of Section 25.3, which exploits the correlation of the two series, would be sharper but not by enough to turn this into evidence. The honest reading is the one Campbell and Thompson (2008) gave for the equity premium: the signal that theory motivates, the term structure that Fama and French (1989) tied to business conditions, is worth a little to an investor who uses it with restraint, and the signals that data alone motivate are worth nothing after costs. Sharpe (1987) described tactical allocation as the strategic policy re-solved with current information; on this universe the re-solving is worth a few tens of basis points where the information is real and costs about as much where it is not.

Two things this chapter did not do are done elsewhere. It built the forecasts from data; a view that comes from an opinion and must become a return vector before an optimizer can use it is the subject of Section 17.1. And it scaled each forecast into a weight by a rule chosen by hand, Equation 16.9; Section 18.1 skips the forecast and estimates the map from signal to weight on the investor’s objective directly, in the manner of Brandt et al. (2009), while Section 19.1 lets the whole policy switch with a detected state rather than tilt with a continuous signal. Whether any of the three should be judged on squared forecast error at all is the question Section 26.1 takes up.

Key Takeaways

  • A predictive regression is judged out of sample, against the historical mean, by the \(R^2_{\text{OS}}\) of Equation 16.3, and against that nested benchmark by the Clark-West correction of Equation 16.5 to the Diebold-Mariano test, because the mean does not pay the estimation noise a regression does (Welch and Goyal 2008; Campbell and Thompson 2008; Diebold and Mariano 1995; Clark and West 2007).
  • A small \(R^2\) is not a small effect. By Equation 16.6 the proportional gain to a mean-variance investor is \(R^2_{\text{OS}} / S^2\), so half a percent a month against an annual Sharpe ratio of one half is a quarter of the expected excess return (Kandel and Stambaugh 1996).
  • This universe supports momentum from returns, the term and credit spreads from sim_curve_weekly, and turbulence from the cross-section; it does not support value, which needs fundamentals it does not carry.
  • Of four signals on sixteen series, the term spread is the only one with out-of-sample evidence, positive on 10 series and significant on 6; momentum, the IG spread and turbulence lose to the mean out of sample, and a regularized seven-signal model loses on 11 to 15 of the sixteen, the more of them the less it shrinks (Gu et al. 2020).
  • A tilt of at most half the policy weight pays after costs on the term spread, by 25 basis points a year of certainty equivalent, inside the sampling error of the Sharpe ratio; the combination earns a few basis points, and momentum and the elastic net trade more and lose.
  • applyFUN() scores static weight vectors on the full history and trailingFUN() evaluates a function once on the last n + 1 rows. Neither rolls; rolling estimation is zoo::rollapply() or an explicit loop.
