17  Expressing Views

An allocator has an opinion: European credit will lag, commodities will pay, Japanese equity is cheap. How does that opinion become a weight? Black and Litterman (1992) answered that it should not become one directly, because an optimizer handed a hand-written return vector produces the extreme, unstable portfolios that Michaud (1989) called error maximization; the opinion should instead be blended with an equilibrium prior in proportion to how confidently it is held, and the optimizer given the blend. That construction, the Black-Litterman model, is the standard answer, and this chapter sets it beside two others that ask less of the allocator: views on the probabilities of historical scenarios (Meucci 2008), and views that are only a ranking (Almgren and Chriss 2006).

This chapter is tactical in the sense of Section 3.1: a view is conditioning information, and every portfolio below is a deviation from the strategic policy that Section 6.1 anchored on the equilibrium vector. We first state the three formulations as mathematics, then implement each on the sixteen risky class series, and finally express one opinion three ways and compare what each formulation committed to.

The data are the sixteen risky class series of Section 2.2, in excess of the Treasury bill for the reasons of Section 3.1.

17.1 A View as a Bayesian Update

17.1.1 The Black-Litterman posterior

Write \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) for the vector of expected excess returns and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) for their covariance, as in Section 9.1. Black and Litterman (1991) treat \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) as unknown and center its prior on the equilibrium vector of Equation 6.6, read from market weights by the reverse optimization of Sharpe (1974),

\[ \boldsymbol{\mu} \sim N\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}},\, \tau\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right), \qquad \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}} = \delta\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mkt}}, \tag{17.1}\]

where \(\tau\) scales the prior’s uncertainty relative to the returns. A view is a statement about \(K\) linear combinations of \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\), collected in a \(K \times N\) pick matrix \(\boldsymbol{P}\), with a stated value \(\boldsymbol{q}\) and an error whose covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) says how sure the allocator is:

\[ \boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\mu} = \boldsymbol{q} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim N(\boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{\Omega}). \tag{17.2}\]

A row of \(\boldsymbol{P}\) with a single one is an absolute view on one asset; a row with \(+1\) and \(-1\) is a relative view on a spread. Prior and likelihood are normal, so the posterior is normal, with mean and covariance

\[ \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{BL}} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}} + \tau\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}' \left(\boldsymbol{P}\tau\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}' + \boldsymbol{\Omega}\right)^{-1} \left(\boldsymbol{q} - \boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}}\right), \qquad \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = \tau\boldsymbol{\Sigma} - \tau\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}' \left(\boldsymbol{P}\tau\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}' + \boldsymbol{\Omega}\right)^{-1} \boldsymbol{P}\tau\boldsymbol{\Sigma}. \tag{17.3}\]

Satchell and Scowcroft (2000) give the Bayesian derivation, Kolm and Ritter (2017) the cleanest statement of what is conditioned on what, and Cheung (2010) and Walters (2014) the bookkeeping the original paper left out. Two features of Equation 17.3 decide everything below. The posterior mean moves away from the prior only in the directions \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}'\), so a view on one asset moves every asset that covaries with it. And \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) is the covariance of the mean, not of returns, and is never to be handed to a risk objective, the fourth point of the callout in Section 6.2.

Two parameters have no data behind them. \(\tau\) is the more discussed and the less important: He and Litterman (1999) set it at 0.05, Idzorek (2007) at 0.025, Walters (2014) surveys a range from \(1/T\) upward, and Meucci (2010) observes that if \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is proportional to \(\tau\) then \(\tau\) cancels from the posterior mean, which is the convention here. \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is the parameter that matters. He and Litterman (1999) take it diagonal with \(\omega_k = \boldsymbol{p}_k'\tau\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{p}_k\), so a view on a volatile spread is held less tightly; Idzorek (2007) asks for a confidence between zero and one and solves for the \(\omega_k\) that delivers that fraction of the full-confidence tilt; Meucci (2010) gives the closed form of the same idea, used throughout this chapter:

\[ \boldsymbol{\Omega} = \frac{1-c}{c}\;\boldsymbol{P}\tau\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}', \qquad c \in (0, 1). \tag{17.4}\]

Substituting Equation 17.4 into Equation 17.3, the bracketed matrix becomes \(\boldsymbol{P}\tau\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}'/c\), \(\tau\) cancels, and the posterior value of each view portfolio is a weighted average of prior and view:

\[ \boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{BL}} = (1-c)\,\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}} + c\,\boldsymbol{q}. \tag{17.5}\]

At \(c = \tfrac12\) allocator and market are weighted equally; as \(c \to 1\) the view is imposed and as \(c \to 0\) ignored. The confidence is therefore the size of the position rather than a nuisance parameter, and Section 17.2 traces it.

What the optimizer does with \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{BL}}\) is the result He and Litterman (1999) wrote the model to obtain. An investor with the market’s risk aversion holds \(\boldsymbol{w} = (\delta\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\), and substituting Equation 17.3 with Equation 17.4 gives

\[ \boldsymbol{w}_{\text{BL}} = (\delta\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{BL}} = \boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mkt}} + \boldsymbol{P}'\boldsymbol{\Lambda}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \frac{c}{\delta} \left(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}'\right)^{-1} \left(\boldsymbol{q} - \boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eq}}\right). \tag{17.6}\]

The posterior portfolio is the market plus a position in each view portfolio, and nothing else: an asset in no view keeps its market weight, however strongly it covaries with the assets that are. This is what distinguishes Black-Litterman from writing a number into \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) by hand: the update through \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}'\) moves the means of correlated assets by exactly enough that the optimizer sees no spread to exploit between them. Bertsimas et al. (2012) reach the same construction from inverse optimization, and Section 17.3 shows what happens without it. Under a long-only constraint Equation 17.6 holds until a weight reaches zero, after which the portfolio is the quadratic program of Section 12.1.

17.1.2 Views on scenarios: entropy pooling

Black-Litterman is a view on the mean under normality, and Meucci (2008) removes both restrictions. Take as the prior not a distribution but \(J\) scenarios \(\boldsymbol{r}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{r}_J\) with probabilities \(p_1, \dots, p_J\), here the 372 months of the sample with uniform weight. A view is any constraint on expectations under new probabilities \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}\): a mean, a variance, a probability of loss, a ranking. Among all probability vectors that satisfy it, the posterior is the one closest to the prior in relative entropy,

\[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{p}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}} \sum_{j=1}^{J} x_j \left(\ln x_j - \ln p_j\right) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_j x_j = 1, \quad \boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{h}, \quad \boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{x} \le \boldsymbol{f}, \tag{17.7}\]

where each row of \(\boldsymbol{H}\) or \(\boldsymbol{F}\) holds the scenario values of one view function. The objective is the divergence of Kullback and Leibler (1951); choosing the least-divergent distribution consistent with stated moments is the maximum-entropy principle of Jaynes (1957); Csiszár (1975) proved the solution exists and is unique; and Robertson et al. (2005) brought the same device to macroeconomic forecasting. The solution is an exponential tilt,

\[ \tilde{p}_j \propto p_j \exp\!\left(-\boldsymbol{\theta}'\boldsymbol{H}_{\cdot j} - \boldsymbol{\lambda}'\boldsymbol{F}_{\cdot j}\right), \qquad \boldsymbol{\lambda} \ge \boldsymbol{0}, \tag{17.8}\]

with one multiplier per view, so the optimization runs over \(K\) numbers rather than \(J\) probabilities. Equation 17.8 is also why entropy pooling reproduces Black-Litterman when the scenarios are normal and the views are on means (Meucci 2006, 2008): a tilt in the direction of a view portfolio shifts every other asset by its covariance with that portfolio. Where the scenarios are not normal the two part, and Section 17.3 measures by how much.

A posterior that concentrates on a few months has thrown the rest of the history away, and the diagnostic is the effective number of scenarios,

\[ N_{\text{eff}} = \exp\!\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{J} \tilde{p}_j \ln \tilde{p}_j\right), \tag{17.9}\]

the construction Meucci (2009) uses for the effective number of bets in Section 9.3: \(J\) under the uniform prior, falling toward one as the mass concentrates. For a view that moves one mean by \(k\) standard errors, the divergence of the tilt is \(k^2/2T\) to second order, so

\[ N_{\text{eff}} \approx T \exp\!\left(-\frac{k^2}{2T}\right), \tag{17.10}\]

which says where the entropy step bites: a view within a few standard errors costs almost nothing, and one tens of standard errors away leaves a fraction of the history. Confidence enters by opinion pooling, \(\boldsymbol{p}_c = (1-c)\,\boldsymbol{p} + c\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}\) (Meucci 2008), which gives the posterior mean the form of Equation 17.5.

17.1.3 Views as an ordering: the centroid

Both formulations ask for a number, and Salcher et al. (2026) argue that a number is often more than an allocator honestly has: what survives estimation error in a cross-section of means is their ordering, and a rule that commits to the ranking alone loses less out of sample than one that commits to magnitudes. Almgren and Chriss (2006) formalize the ranking as a view. If the allocator believes \(\mu_{\pi(1)} \ge \dots \ge \mu_{\pi(N)}\) for a permutation \(\pi\), the mean vectors consistent with that belief form a cone \(Q\), and for any convex feasible set the portfolio that maximizes expected return over \(Q\) maximizes \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{c}\), where

\[ \boldsymbol{c} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\boldsymbol{\mu} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu} \in Q\right], \qquad c_{\pi(j)} \approx \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{N + 1 - j - \alpha}{N - 2\alpha + 1}\right), \quad \alpha = 0.4424 - 0.1185\,N^{-0.21}, \tag{17.11}\]

is the centroid of the cone under a spherically symmetric distribution, with the closed-form approximation of the same paper. It is the vector of expected order statistics of \(N\) standard normals: symmetric about zero, wider-spaced at the ends, carrying no level and no scale. Herold (2003) works the same problem with qualitative forecasts, and Grinold (1989) supplies the reason a ranking can be worth money: the value of a signal is its correlation with realized returns, which a correct ordering delivers without any magnitude. What a centroid does not say is how large the position should be.

Two functions from Section 6.3 are reused unchanged: tangency_lo() solves the long-only tangency problem as one quadratic program, and make_moments() hands a mean vector the package did not compute to optimize.portfolio().

tangency_lo <- function(mu, S) {
  if (max(mu) <= 0) return(setNames(rep(NA_real_, length(mu)), names(mu)))
  N <- length(mu)
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S, dvec = rep(0, N),
                            Amat = cbind(mu, diag(N)), bvec = c(1, rep(0, N)), meq = 1)
  w <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  setNames(w / sum(w), names(mu))
}
make_moments <- function(mu, S) {
  force(mu); force(S)
  function(R, portfolio, ...) list(mu = mu, sigma = S, m3 = NULL, m4 = NULL, mean = mu, var = S)
}
spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only") |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "StdDev")

17.2.1 Black-Litterman with two views

Two views, one relative and one absolute: United States credit will outperform European credit by two percentage points a year, and commodities will earn four percent a year above the bill. Each is a row of \(\boldsymbol{P}\) and an entry of \(\boldsymbol{q}\), in the monthly units of the data.

P <- matrix(0, 2, N, dimnames = list(c("credit_spread", "commodity"), colnames(Re)))
P["credit_spread", "US_credit"]     <-  1
P["credit_spread", "EU_credit"]     <- -1
P["commodity",     "GLOBAL_commodity"] <- 1

stopifnot(identical(colnames(P), colnames(Re)), identical(names(q), rownames(P)))

prior_views <- as.numeric(P %*% mu_eq)
stopifnot(prior_views[1] < 0)      # the prior puts US_credit below EU_credit, as the text explains

The prior disagrees with the first view by 5.4 percentage points, because under the proxy weights European credit covaries more with the market than United States credit; the second view is 0.1 points from the prior. Equation 17.3 with Equation 17.4 is a few lines, and writing them is the only way to have a confidence parameter, because black.litterman() has none.

bl_post <- function(mu, S, P, q, conf, tau = 1) {
  stopifnot(identical(colnames(P), names(mu)), nrow(P) == length(q))
  Omega   <- (1 - conf) / conf * P %*% (tau * S) %*% t(P)          # @eq-17-omega
  M       <- solve(P %*% (tau * S) %*% t(P) + Omega)
  mu_post <- mu + as.numeric((tau * S) %*% t(P) %*% M %*% (q - P %*% mu))   # @eq-17-posterior
  S_mu    <- tau * S - (tau * S) %*% t(P) %*% M %*% P %*% (tau * S)
  list(mu = setNames(mu_post, names(mu)), S_mu = S_mu, Omega = Omega)
}

post <- bl_post(mu_eq, S, P, q, conf = 0.5)
bl   <- black.litterman(Re, P = P, Mu = mu_eq, Views = q)

stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(unname(post$mu), as.numeric(bl$BLMu))),          # the package at c = 1/2
          isTRUE(all.equal(unname(post$S_mu), unname(bl$BLSigma))),
          isTRUE(all.equal(bl_post(mu_eq, S, P, q, 0.5, tau = 0.05)$mu, post$mu)),  # tau cancels
          isTRUE(all.equal(as.numeric(P %*% post$mu), 0.5 * prior_views + 0.5 * unname(q))))  # @eq-17-pooling
round(cbind(prior = prior_views, view = q, posterior = as.numeric(P %*% post$mu)) * 12, 4)
#>                 prior view posterior
#> credit_spread -0.0336 0.02   -0.0068
#> commodity      0.0387 0.04    0.0394

The four assertions carry the theory: black.litterman() is Equation 17.3 at \(c = \tfrac12\), since its fixed \(\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}'\) is Equation 17.4 at that confidence; changing \(\tau\) leaves the posterior mean untouched; and each view portfolio lands at the midpoint of prior and view.

Noteblack.litterman() with views: three things to check before believing it

17.2.2 Through set.portfolio.moments()

The route that needs no custom function is method = "black_litterman", which set.portfolio.moments() passes on to black.litterman(). The arguments must travel through optimize.portfolio(), because a direct call recovers them with match.call() and sees symbols rather than values, as Section 6.2 showed; with views the route works and carries one more trap.

WarningWith views, BLSigma is no longer a rescaled \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\)

With the prior alone, set.portfolio.moments(method = "black_litterman") hands the optimizer \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}/2\), which a tangency portfolio does not notice. With views it hands \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) of Equation 17.3, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) minus a rank-\(K\) correction in the directions of the view portfolios, and the tangency portfolio is solved on the wrong risk: the commodity weight comes out at 23% against 10% on \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), a gap of 13 points in one weight, and nothing warns. The remedy is momentargs: set.portfolio.moments() fills only the moments it is not given, so momentargs = list(sigma = S) keeps the posterior mean, restores the return covariance, and agrees with the hand computation to 1e-08.

17.2.3 Entropy pooling on the 372 months

The prior is uniform over the 372 months; the series is US_equity; the views are that its mean will be two percentage points a year above the sample mean and that its volatility will be twenty percent below the sample volatility. A view on a mean is a row of \(\boldsymbol{H}\) holding the series; a view on a volatility is a row holding its square, with \(\boldsymbol{h}\) set to the second moment the view implies.

Aeq and beq have no default, so a view expressed only as inequalities still needs Aeq = matrix(1, 1, J), beq = 1 for the probabilities to sum to one; leave them out and the error names a missing argument, not the constraint. The prior must sum to one within \(10^{-5}\). Every call runs nloptr’s derivative checker and reports as an R message, hence the suppressMessages() above; on a squared series the checker sometimes reports one error from its finite-difference tolerance while the optimization converges and hits the constraint, which the stopifnot() confirms. The result p_ is a \(J \times 1\) matrix, not a vector. And a view the scenarios cannot satisfy, a mean beyond the range of the data or a volatility near zero, does not fail as infeasible: the multipliers diverge and the function stops with Sum of revised probabilities is greater than 1!, the symptom rather than the cause.

Where the step bites is a matter of distance, and Equation 17.10 says how to measure it.

A mean view five standard errors from the sample, already 14 percentage points a year, keeps 359 of 372 months; forty keep 60 and put 111 times the prior mass on the single best month. Equation 17.10 tracks the measured number until the tilt reaches the skewed tail, where the normal approximation understates what the data can still supply. In the units an allocator states them in, the volatility view is the expensive one: a twenty percent cut costs 9 months where two points on the mean cost 0.3, because Equation 4.3 made the variance the input a sample knows well and Equation 6.1 the mean the one it does not. In standard errors of the respective estimate, two points on the mean are 0.7 standard errors away and the twenty percent cut 5.5, and Equation 17.10 prices a view by that distance alone.

17.3 One Opinion, Three Formulations

The opinion is that United States credit is expensive and Japanese equity is cheap: as a portfolio, long JP_equity and short US_credit. Each formulation receives it in its own language: a relative view three percentage points a year above the equilibrium spread at a confidence of one half; the same spread as an entropy view on the historical scenarios, pooled at the same confidence; and a bucket ranking with US_credit at the bottom, JP_equity at the top, and no opinion about the fourteen between.

Read 2 across. Black-Litterman and entropy pooling put the tracking error within 0.01 points of each other, at 0.69% and 0.70%, and both move JP_equity from 3.8% to about 7.2%. The first touches nothing else, which is Equation 17.6: the unconstrained tilt is \(\boldsymbol{P}'\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\), and the long-only constraint does not bind. The second moves 9 other weights by more than half a point, CH_government the most at 3.0 points, the difference between an exponential tilt on 372 skewed months and a normal update; it is small here because the spread series has a skewness of -0.19, and on the volatility view above it was not.

The centroid committed to the least and moved the portfolio the most: a turnover of 20% against 3%, US_credit sold to zero, and 10 other weights changed by more than half a point. The reason is not the ranking but the way it entered \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\). Adding \(\kappa\boldsymbol{c}\) to the prior lowered US_credit’s mean and left US_government’s where it was, and the optimizer read that as an opinion about the spread between two series that move together: the unconstrained tilt \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\kappa\boldsymbol{c}\) is a large short in credit hedged with a large long in government bonds, which the long-only constraint clips into what Figure 17.4 shows. Black-Litterman moved US_government’s mean by -0.12 points along with credit’s, so no such spread appeared. A view is coherent with the covariance matrix only when the shift in \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) is \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) times a portfolio, and the centroid’s shift is not; the remedy is to pass the ranking through Equation 17.3 as absolute views on the two named assets, which exercise 3 asks for. The lesson of He and Litterman (1999) is that the point of Black-Litterman is less the Bayesian update than the guarantee that the answer is the market plus the views.

Two readings from Section 8.1 close the comparison. A view is an estimate: the spread has an annualized volatility of 20.0%, so the standard error of its mean over 31 model years is, by Equation 6.1, 3.6 percentage points, and the three-point deviation the opinion asserted is less than one standard error of what the entire sample could say about it; the entropy program priced it at 0.4 effective months of 372. And a ranking commits to less: it is invariant to the magnitude errors that Chopra and Ziemba (1993) found an order of magnitude more costly than errors in variances, which is the case Salcher et al. (2026) make for it, and on these numbers the formulation that asked for a rank matched the two that asked for a number until the rank was written into \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) without the covariance. Whether the ranking is right is a separate question from whether it was expressed well, and only the second is settled here.

None of this is out of sample, and it cannot be. A view is dated, held at a moment about a horizon, and a backtest of views needs a record of what the allocator believed month by month, which no dataset supplies and this book does not invent. The chapter therefore stops at in-sample comparisons of what each formulation buys. Where the view is generated from data, a momentum ranking or a signal of Section 16.1, a history of views exists by construction, and Section 16.3 is where such a view is run through the rolling machinery of Section 25.3. Section 18.1 takes the alternative route of skipping \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) altogether, and Section 19.1 reads a regime as a view on which scenarios apply, Equation 17.7 with the probabilities set by a state rather than an opinion.

Key Takeaways

  • Black-Litterman is Equation 17.3: a normal prior on \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) centered on the equilibrium of Equation 6.6, views as linear statements with error, and a posterior that moves every asset by its covariance with the view portfolios (Black and Litterman 1992; Satchell and Scowcroft 2000).
  • With \(\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \tfrac{1-c}{c}\boldsymbol{P}\tau\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{P}'\) the scale \(\tau\) cancels and each view lands at \((1-c)\) times the prior plus \(c\) times the view (Meucci 2010). The confidence is the size of the position; black.litterman() fixes it at one half.
  • The posterior tangency portfolio is the market plus a position in each view portfolio, Equation 17.6, and assets in no view keep their weight (He and Litterman 1999). A view written into \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) by hand lacks that property and becomes a hedged spread trade.
  • Entropy pooling replaces the distribution with 372 scenarios and the view with a constraint on their probabilities (Meucci 2008). A mean view within a few standard errors costs nothing; a volatility view discards the months of the liquidity shock and the credit crisis and re-rates everything with a left tail.
  • A ranking is a direction, and the centroid of Equation 17.11 is its expected value (Almgren and Chriss 2006). It has no level and no scale, ac.ranking() assigns it a wrong one, and the size of the position is a choice the allocator has to make explicitly.
