18  Parametric Portfolio Policies

Draft|Code: script · exercises · solutions · all chapters|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

What if an investor skipped the forecast altogether and wrote the weight of each asset directly as a function of what can be observed about it? Brandt et al. (2009) proposed exactly that: a portfolio policy with a handful of coefficients on asset characteristics, estimated by maximizing the utility the policy would have delivered, with no expected return and no covariance matrix computed along the way. The appeal is arithmetic. A mean-variance problem on \(N\) assets consumes \(N\) means and \(N(N+1)/2\) variances and covariances, whose estimation error Section 8.1 priced; a policy on \(K\) characteristics has \(K\) parameters however large \(N\) is.

We first set out the policy, its estimator and the standard errors that say whether a tilt is real, with the long-only and transaction-cost variants and the version that times a few asset classes on state variables; then write the policy in about forty lines of R and estimate it twice, across the 123 equities of the modeled market on size and momentum and across the sixteen classes on momentum, the term spread and the credit spread; and finally re-estimate both on an expanding window and hold them out of sample against their benchmarks, gross and net of costs. On the horizon axis of Section 3.1 the chapter is tactical in its positions, which move every month with characteristics observed at the time, and strategic in its rule: the coefficients are estimated from the unconditional history and then held fixed, so that conditioning information enters through the characteristics and never through re-estimated moments.

The data are the two panels of Section 2.2, the sixteen risky class series and the 123 equities of the instrument table. They enter as total returns rather than in excess of the bill: both policies are fully invested in risky assets, and the utility function below is defined on gross wealth.

18.1 Weights as a Function of Characteristics

18.1.1 The policy and its estimator

Let \(\bar{w}_{i,t}\) be the weight of asset \(i\) in a benchmark portfolio at the end of month \(t\), and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i,t}\) a vector of \(K\) characteristics of the asset observed at that date, standardized across the \(N_t\) assets then available to mean zero and unit standard deviation. The policy of Brandt et al. (2009) is linear in them:

\[ w_{i,t} = \bar{w}_{i,t} + \frac{1}{N_t}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}'\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i,t}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, N_t . \tag{18.1}\]

Three features carry the method. The coefficients \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) are the same for every asset and every month, so the policy knows characteristics, not assets. Because the standardized characteristics sum to zero across assets, so do the tilts, and the weights sum to one whenever the benchmark’s do. And the division by \(N_t\) keeps the active position from growing with the number of assets. The benchmark is the capitalization-weighted or the equal-weighted portfolio, and \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = \boldsymbol{0}\) returns it.

The coefficients maximize the average utility the policy would have earned over the sample,

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\; \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} u\big(r_{p,t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big), \qquad r_{p,t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} w_{i,t}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,r_{i,t+1}, \qquad u(r) = \frac{(1+r)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}, \tag{18.2}\]

with \(r_{i,t+1}\) the simple return of Equation 2.1 and \(u\) the power utility of Section 3.1 with relative risk aversion \(\gamma\). The whole distribution of the portfolio return enters, not only the two moments of Section 9.1. Nothing is forecast: Equation 18.2 never computes \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) or \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\). Estimating the decision rule from the investor’s optimality condition, bypassing the return distribution, goes back to Brandt (1999), who did it nonparametrically for one risky asset; Aït-Sahalia and Brandt (2001) used the idea to ask which predictors matter for the portfolio as opposed to the return, and Brandt (2010) surveys the line of work.

The dimension is what the method buys. For the 123 equities the plug-in rule of Equation 8.2 needs 123 means and 7626 variances and covariances from 372 months; the policy needs two coefficients. Michaud (1989) named what the first route does with estimation error, Kan and Zhou (2007) priced it, DeMiguel et al. (2009) showed that it loses to equal weighting out of sample, and Jagannathan and Ma (2003) that constraining the weights acts as shrinkage. A parametric policy is the far end of that road: weights may differ across assets only as far as characteristics do.

Equation 18.1 is linear in \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\), and so therefore is the portfolio return:

\[ r_{p,t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = r_{b,t+1} + \boldsymbol{\theta}'\boldsymbol{a}_{t+1}, \qquad \boldsymbol{a}_{t+1} = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i,t}\,r_{i,t+1}, \tag{18.3}\]

where \(r_{b,t+1}\) is the benchmark’s return and \(a_{k,t+1}\) the return of a zero-investment portfolio that is long the assets with a high value of characteristic \(k\) and short those with a low one. The policy is a position in \(K + 1\) portfolios, the benchmark and one long-short portfolio per characteristic, and \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) is the weight vector over the last \(K\). Hjalmarsson and Manchev (2012) solve the mean-variance version of that problem in closed form, and Brandt and Santa-Clara (2006) had made the same move in the time series, taken up below.

18.1.2 Standard errors

Because \(u\) is concave and \(r_p\) linear in \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\), the objective is concave and the estimate solves the first-order condition

\[ \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\boldsymbol{h}_{t+1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) = \boldsymbol{0}, \qquad \boldsymbol{h}_{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = u'\big(r_{p,t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)\,\boldsymbol{a}_{t+1}, \tag{18.4}\]

\(K\) moment conditions in \(K\) unknowns: at the optimum the marginal-utility-weighted return of every characteristic portfolio is zero. That makes \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\) an exactly identified method-of-moments estimator in the sense of Hansen (1982), with asymptotic covariance matrix

\[ \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) = \frac{1}{T}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{G}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}\hat{\boldsymbol{G}}^{-1}, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{G}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{h}_{t+1}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}'}, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{V}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\boldsymbol{h}_{t+1}\boldsymbol{h}_{t+1}' . \tag{18.5}\]

The middle matrix takes the scores to be serially uncorrelated; when they are not, the estimator of Newey and West (1987) in Equation 5.5 replaces it. A bootstrap that resamples whole months, in blocks to preserve serial dependence (Künsch 1989), is the check that does not lean on the asymptotics. The \(t\)-statistic of \(\hat{\theta}_k\) tests whether characteristic \(k\) belongs in the portfolio, which is not the question of whether it forecasts returns: by Equation 18.4 a characteristic whose portfolio earns nothing on average still enters if it pays in the months in which marginal utility is high.

18.1.3 Long-only weights, and costs

Equation 18.1 shorts freely. A long-only mandate can be imposed as Brandt et al. (2009) do, by truncating the weights at zero and renormalizing,

\[ w^{+}_{i,t} = \frac{\max(0, w_{i,t})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_t}\max(0, w_{j,t})} , \tag{18.6}\]

and it costs two things. The weights are no longer linear in \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\), so Equation 18.3 is lost; and \(\max(0, \cdot)\) has a kink, so the objective is not differentiable wherever a weight crosses zero. The gradient that a quasi-Newton optimizer and Equation 18.5 rely on does not exist there, and estimation falls back on a derivative-free search and the bootstrap. The alternative, which we use at class level, keeps the smooth objective and restricts the parameters instead: \(w_{i,t}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \ge 0\) for every asset and month of the estimation sample is a set of linear inequalities in \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\). Ammann et al. (2016) treat leverage constraints on the policy in the same spirit.

Trading costs enter the objective rather than being subtracted afterwards. With one-way rates \(c_i\), the net return of Equation 23.2 is

\[ r^{\text{net}}_{p,t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = r_{p,t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) - \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} c_i\,\big|w_{i,t}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) - w^{h}_{i,t}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|, \qquad w^{h}_{i,t} = w_{i,t-1}\,\frac{1 + r_{i,t}}{1 + r_{p,t}}, \tag{18.7}\]

where \(w^{h}\) is the weight the previous position has drifted to, and maximizing the utility of \(r^{\text{net}}\) shrinks most the coefficients on the characteristics that change fastest. Section 23.1 showed that under proportional costs the optimal policy is a no-trade region around the target (Constantinides 1986), or a partial adjustment toward it when the target moves (Gârleanu and Pedersen 2013). Brandt et al. (2009) put that region into the policy as one more parameter: the portfolio stays where it has drifted while it is close to the target and trades to the edge of the region otherwise. DeMiguel et al. (2020) show that costs also change which characteristics matter, because trades in different characteristics net against each other and more of them are worth holding jointly than one at a time.

18.1.4 Timing across a few asset classes

With sixteen assets there is little cross-section to learn from, and the information is in the time series. Brandt and Santa-Clara (2006) write the weight of each asset as a linear function of state variables \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\) and observe that the dynamic problem becomes a static one on an augmented set of assets, the managed portfolios \(z_{k,t}\,r_{i,t+1}\): Equation 18.3 with a signal in place of the characteristic. Their unrestricted version has \(N \times K\) coefficients. We keep the parsimony of Equation 18.1 by giving each signal one coefficient and a fixed pattern of loadings \(g_{k,i}\) across classes that sums to zero,

\[ w_{i,t} = \bar{w}_{i} + \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_k\,z_{k,t}\,g_{k,i}, \tag{18.8}\]

so that a positive \(\theta_k\) moves weight toward the classes loading on signal \(k\) when the signal is high. The signals are those Section 16.1 built and tested: the term spread, loading on the government series, and the credit spread, loading on the credit series, the two state variables of Fama and French (1989); and each class’s own 12-1 momentum, a characteristic in the ordinary sense, which Asness et al. (2013) document across asset classes.

What separates all of this from Section 16.3 is the loss function. There the forecasts were chosen by squared error and turned into weights by a rule set by hand, Equation 16.9; here the map from signal to weight is estimated on the investor’s own objective, costs and constraints included. It is the cleanest case in this book of an estimator trained on the decision it serves, and Section 27.1 generalizes it to forecasters with many more parameters, where the linear index \(\boldsymbol{\theta}'\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\) gives way to the nonlinear ones of Gu et al. (2020).

Brandt et al. (2009) use the three characteristics of the cross-sectional literature, size and book-to-market (Fama and French 1992) and momentum (Jegadeesh and Titman 1993). The modeled market supports two of them: size comes from sim_marketcap_weekly and momentum from the returns, while value needs a book value the market does not carry and waits for Section 21.1.

18.2 Estimation of Two Policies

18.2.1 The equity panel

The benchmark is the capitalization-weighted portfolio of 1: the weight held over a month is the last capitalization observed on or before the end of the month before, a weight an investor could have held. Size is the logarithm of the same number and momentum the 12-1 return of Equation 16.8. Both are standardized each month within region rather than across all 123 names. 1 showed that a global size sort in this market sorts on region and that the region-neutral construction carries the more reliable premium; standardizing within region makes every tilt sum to zero region by region, so the policy selects stocks and leaves the regional allocation to the benchmark.

18.2.2 The policy in forty lines

ppp_weights <- function(theta, X, wbar, long_only = FALSE) {
  stopifnot(length(theta) == dim(X)[3], identical(dimnames(X)[1:2], dimnames(wbar)))
  tilt <- 0
  for (k in seq_along(theta)) tilt <- tilt + theta[k] * X[, , k]
  w <- wbar + tilt / ncol(wbar)                                # @eq-18-policy
  if (long_only) { w <- pmax(w, 0); w <- w / rowSums(w) }      # @eq-18-longonly
  w
}

crra <- function(r, gamma) (1 + r)^(1 - gamma) / (1 - gamma)

# Row t of W is held over month t. The trade into it is measured from where
# last month's weights drifted to, is charged to month t, and the portfolio
# starts flat: the accounting of the register.
path_returns <- function(W, Rn, cost) {
  gross <- rowSums(W * Rn)
  hold  <- W * (1 + Rn) / (1 + gross)
  trade <- abs(W - rbind(0, hold[-nrow(W), , drop = FALSE]))
  cbind(gross = gross, net = gross - as.numeric(trade %*% cost),   # @eq-18-cost
        turnover = rowSums(trade) / 2)                             # @eq-23-turnover
}

ppp_utility <- function(theta, X, wbar, Rn, cost = NULL, gamma = 5) {
  W <- ppp_weights(theta, X, wbar)
  r <- if (is.null(cost)) rowSums(W * Rn) else path_returns(W, Rn, cost)[, "net"]
  mean(crra(r, gamma))                                         # @eq-18-estimator
}

# simplex = TRUE restricts theta to the set on which no weight of the
# estimation sample is negative: linear inequalities, one per month and asset.
ppp_estimate <- function(X, wbar, Rn, cost = NULL, simplex = FALSE,
                         start = rep(0, dim(X)[3])) {
  f   <- function(th) -ppp_utility(th, X, wbar, Rn, cost, gamma)
  ctl <- list(reltol = 1e-12)
  par <- if (!simplex) {
    stats::optim(start, f, method = "BFGS", control = ctl)$par
  } else {
    stats::constrOptim(start, f, function(th) numDeriv::grad(f, th, method = "simple"),
                       ui = matrix(X, ncol = dim(X)[3]) / ncol(wbar), ci = -as.numeric(wbar),
                       mu = 1e-6, outer.eps = 1e-12, control = ctl)$par
  }
  setNames(par, dimnames(X)[[3]])
}

path_returns() follows the accounting of the register in Section 25.3; ppp_estimate() maximizes Equation 18.2 with the BFGS method of optim(), or with constrOptim() under the restriction that keeps the weights in the simplex.

18.2.3 Standard errors two ways

Equation 18.5 needs the scores month by month and the Hessian of the objective, and numDeriv supplies both from ppp_weights() without any algebra. The bootstrap resamples months in blocks of twelve, as in Section 9.3, and uses Equation 18.3: each draw is a two-parameter problem on three return series, so the panel is never rebuilt.

u_t <- function(th) crra(rowSums(ppp_weights(th, X_eq, wbar_eq) * Rn_eq), gamma)
H   <- numDeriv::jacobian(u_t, theta_eq)                       # T x K scores, @eq-18-foc
G   <- numDeriv::hessian(function(th) mean(u_t(th)), theta_eq)
V_theta     <- solve(G) %*% (crossprod(H) / nrow(H)) %*% solve(G) / nrow(H)   # @eq-18-avar
se_sandwich <- setNames(sqrt(diag(V_theta)), names(theta_eq))

r_b <- rowSums(wbar_eq * Rn_eq)                                # benchmark return
A   <- vapply(names(theta_eq), function(k) rowSums(X_eq[, , k] * Rn_eq),
              numeric(nrow(Rn_eq))) / ncol(Rn_eq)              # characteristic portfolios
stopifnot(all.equal(as.numeric(r_b + A %*% theta_eq),
                    unname(rowSums(ppp_weights(theta_eq, X_eq, wbar_eq) * Rn_eq))))

set.seed(1602)
block_index <- function(n, block = 12L) {
  starts <- sample.int(n - block + 1L, ceiling(n / block), replace = TRUE)
  as.vector(outer(0:(block - 1L), starts, "+"))[seq_len(n)]
}
boot <- t(vapply(seq_len(400L), function(b) {
  i <- block_index(length(r_b))
  stats::optim(theta_eq, function(q) -mean(crra(r_b[i] + A[i, ] %*% q, gamma)),
               method = "BFGS")$par
}, numeric(2)))
se_boot <- setNames(apply(boot, 2, stats::sd), names(theta_eq))

The two standard errors agree to within 0.07. The size coefficient is -4.05 with a \(t\)-statistic of -3.10: the policy moves weight from the large names of each region to the small ones, and the size portfolio of Equation 18.3, long large and short small, loses 1.8% a year. The momentum coefficient is positive at 1.95, 2.0 standard errors from zero, and it is the instructive one, because the momentum portfolio earns 0.5% a year with a \(t\)-statistic of 0.63: nothing. A return forecaster would drop the characteristic. The policy keeps it because Equation 18.4 weighs returns by marginal utility: the momentum portfolio has a correlation of -0.23 with the benchmark, pays in the months in which the size-tilted portfolio loses, and its marginal-utility-weighted mean at \(\theta_{\text{mom}} = 0\) is 1.6% a year. That momentum has no premium of its own is a property of this market and not of the method: the idiosyncratic components of the equities were drawn independently over time (Section 2.2), so a stock’s past return says nothing about its future one beyond what the replayed factors carry. In sample the policy lifts the certainty equivalent from 7.2% to 12.5% a year, a number Section 18.3 will not repeat. It does so with leverage: 46% of all weights are negative and the short side averages 103% of wealth.

18.2.4 The timing policy across sixteen classes

At class level the benchmark is the equal-weight policy portfolio of Section 16.3, for the reason given there: it estimates nothing, so every difference from it is attributable to the policy. The two curve signals are standardized with what was known at the time, the mean and standard deviation of their own history through \(t\), and clipped at two so that one crisis month does not set the scale of the tilt. Each is multiplied by a standardized indicator of the classes it loads on, which is Equation 18.8.

grp <- sub(".*_", "", colnames(R))               # government, credit, equity, commodity
cv <- sim_curve_weekly |>
  dplyr::filter(series %in% c("US_Slope", "US_IG_Spread")) |>
  tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = series, values_from = value) |>
  dplyr::arrange(date)
j_last <- findInterval(dates, cv$date)
stopifnot(all(cv$date[j_last] <= dates), all(dates - cv$date[j_last] < 8))

expanding_z <- function(x, first = 12L, clip = 2) {
  z <- vapply(seq_along(x), function(t)
    if (t < first) NA_real_ else (x[t] - mean(x[1:t])) / stats::sd(x[1:t]), numeric(1))
  pmax(pmin(z, clip), -clip)
}

NoteThe policy itself is outside PortfolioAnalytics

optimize.portfolio() searches over weight vectors; a parametric policy searches over the coefficients of a function that returns weight vectors, and the package has no objective, constraint or solver interface for that. Nor do its rolling utilities help: Section 16.3 showed that applyFUN() and trailingFUN() roll nothing. Three things can be reused once the weights exist. Return.portfolio() turns a weight path into returns, with the back-fill trap of Section 23.2 waiting for any path that starts after the data do. turnover() measures a trade, on a different scale from Equation 23.1: it divides the summed absolute changes by \(N\), not by two. And the optimizers remain the comparison: the register of Section 25.3 is constrained re-optimization on the same months, and Section 18.3 reads it.

18.3 The Policies Out of Sample

18.3.1 The coefficients through time

Both policies are now run as an investor would have run them. The first estimate uses the months through December of model year 10; the coefficients are re-estimated at the end of every model year on all data to that date; and the weights of each month are set from the latest estimate and the characteristics of the month end before, so the out-of-sample period is that of the register, from observation 121 on. Each policy is estimated twice, on the utility of gross returns and on that of net returns, at 10 basis points one way for every equity and at the per-class rates of pm_costs_default() for the classes.

refit <- seq(burn, Tn - 1L, by = 12L)      # month ends at which theta is re-estimated
oos   <- held > burn
stopifnot(held[oos][1] == burn + 1L, all(model_year(dates[refit + 1L]) == 11:31))

theta_path <- function(X, wbar, Rn, cost = NULL, simplex = FALSE) {
  TH <- matrix(0, length(refit), dim(X)[3],
               dimnames = list(as.character(dates[refit]), dimnames(X)[[3]]))
  for (j in seq_along(refit)) {
    k <- held <= refit[j]                  # nothing after the estimation date
    TH[j, ] <- ppp_estimate(X[k, , ], wbar[k, ], Rn[k, ], cost, simplex,
                            start = if (j > 1 && !simplex) TH[j - 1, ] else rep(0, dim(X)[3]))
  }
  TH
}

The size coefficient is negative at every one of the 21 estimation dates, between -5.9 and -2.1. The momentum coefficient is not stable: it starts at -1.4, is negative at the first 4 dates and ends at 1.8. Putting costs into the objective shrinks momentum to 71% of its size on average and size to 97%: the faster-moving characteristic pays more to be held, as Equation 18.7 says it should. The class coefficients keep their signs throughout and move within narrow bands, but that stability is the restriction’s as much as the data’s, since the simplex caps them.

Both policies finish ahead of their benchmarks out of sample, and neither does so by a margin that the sample can certify. Inside the equity sleeve the policy earns a net Sharpe ratio of 0.92 against the capitalization-weighted benchmark’s 0.61 and a certainty equivalent of 9.0% a year against 4.8%, after paying for a turnover of 333% a year. The gap of 0.32 is large as these things go. It is also 1.4 times the standard error of a single Sharpe ratio over 252 months, which Equation 25.2 puts at 0.22, and the paired test that Section 25.3 names for two strategies on the same months (Jobson and Korkie 1981; Memmel 2003) gives a \(z\)-statistic of 1.78: suggestive, not significant at five percent. The policy is also not the benchmark’s kind of portfolio. It is short 121% of wealth on average, and its drawdown of 52% is deeper than the benchmark’s 47%. What it earns is the size tilt, the one characteristic whose coefficient was stable in Figure 18.1.

Costs in the objective do what Equation 18.7 promises and no more. Turnover falls from 333% to 274%, and the net Sharpe ratio is 0.920 against 0.923 for the policy that ignored costs when it was estimated: at 10 basis points the trades the cost-aware policy avoids were worth about what they cost.

Across the sixteen classes the timing policy raises the net Sharpe ratio from 0.62 to 0.73 and the certainty equivalent by 95 basis points a year, with a shallower drawdown and 3.3 times the turnover; the truncation was needed in 6 of 252 months. The gap of 0.11 is 0.5 standard errors by Equation 25.2, and the paired \(z\)-statistic is 1.87, higher than the raw gap suggests because the policy’s returns have a correlation of 0.97 with the benchmark’s. Set beside the register, 1 of its 5 strategies has a higher net Sharpe ratio than these three coefficients re-estimated once a year, among them minimum variance at 0.90, which estimates a covariance matrix and no mean.

The class-level gain is larger than the few tens of basis points that Section 16.3 found for the term-spread tilt, and it comes from the same information plus momentum. What the policy adds is that nobody chose the scale of the tilt: it was estimated, on the objective, with the mandate as a constraint. Whether the next step, a policy with many signals and a flexible functional form, earns more than it overfits is the subject of Section 27.1, and Section 21.1 returns to the equity policy with the long-only mandate that a sleeve usually carries.

Key Takeaways

  • A parametric policy writes each weight as the benchmark weight plus a linear function of standardized characteristics, Equation 18.1, and estimates the coefficients by maximizing realized utility: 2 parameters for 123 equities in place of 7749 moments (Brandt et al. 2009).
  • The estimate solves a moment condition, so it has a sandwich covariance matrix (Hansen 1982), and a block bootstrap over months agrees with it. The test is whether a characteristic belongs in the portfolio, not whether it forecasts returns: momentum earns nothing here and still enters, as a hedge.
  • Truncating at zero imposes a long-only mandate at the price of differentiability; restricting \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) so that no in-sample weight is negative keeps the objective smooth, and the restriction is set by the most extreme months.
  • Out of sample the equity policy earns a net Sharpe ratio of 0.92 against 0.61 and the class policy 0.73 against 0.62; the paired \(z\)-statistics are 1.8 and 1.9, short of significance.
  • The size coefficient keeps its sign at every re-estimation and the momentum coefficient does not; costs in the objective shrink the faster-moving characteristic more and cut the equity policy’s turnover while leaving its net Sharpe ratio where it was.
  • PortfolioAnalytics has no parametric policy. Return.portfolio() reproduces the returns of a weight path that starts where the data do, and turnover() divides by \(N\) rather than by two.
