19  Regime-Dependent Allocation

What if the right portfolio depends on the state of the world? Hamilton (1989) gave that question its statistical form, a series whose parameters switch with an unobserved Markov chain, and Ang and Bekaert (2002) carried the form into portfolio choice, with an international investor who faces a normal regime and a bear regime of lower means, higher volatilities and higher correlations. The descriptive chapters supplied the reason to ask. In Section 4.3 the tails of this market arrived as a handful of episodes in which correlations rose, and in Section 14.3 the effective number of bets fell in the same months.

This is a tactical chapter in the sense of Section 3.1: the allocation is conditioned on a state inferred at the time, and deviates from a policy estimated without one. We first state the regime-switching model, the filter that infers its state and the mixture moments an investor faces one month ahead; then date the regimes of the modeled market with hindsight, detect them without it in two ways, a turbulence threshold and a two-state hidden Markov model, and show what PortfolioAnalytics offers for switching between portfolio specifications; and finally ask whether a regime-switching allocation is ahead of a static one out of sample once the regime has to be detected rather than dated.

The data are the sixteen risky class series of Section 2.2, in excess of the Treasury bill for the reasons of Section 3.1.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)   # sixteen risky classes
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return                                  # the bill
Re <- R - rf                                                       # excess returns
N  <- ncol(Re); dates <- zoo::index(R); X <- zoo::coredata(Re)
ep <- sim_episodes                                                 # the four named episodes
stopifnot(N == 16L, !anyNA(Re), all(sim_riskfree_monthly$date == dates))

19.1 Regimes, Filters, and Mixtures

19.1.1 The regime-switching model

Let \(s_t \in \{1, \dots, K\}\) be an unobserved state and \(\boldsymbol{r}_t\) the vector of excess returns. In the model of Hamilton (1989), applied to returns,

\[ \boldsymbol{r}_t \mid s_t = s \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_s, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_s), \qquad \Pr(s_{t+1} = j \mid s_t = i) = p_{ij}, \tag{19.1}\]

with the \(p_{ij}\) collected in a transition matrix \(\boldsymbol{P}\) whose rows sum to one. A regime lasts \(1/(1 - p_{ss})\) periods on average, and the chain spends a long-run share \(\bar{\pi}_s\) of its time in state \(s\), where \(\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}'\boldsymbol{P} = \bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}'\). Turner et al. (1989) fitted a two-state version to excess stock returns, with a mean and a variance that both switch, and Ang and Timmermann (2012) survey what the model has been used for since.

19.1.2 The Hamilton filter

The state is never observed, so everything rests on the probability of each state given the returns seen so far. Write \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t|\tau}\) for the vector with elements \(\Pr(s_t = j \mid \boldsymbol{r}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{r}_\tau)\) and \(\boldsymbol{\eta}_t\) for the vector of regime densities \(f(\boldsymbol{r}_t \mid s_t = j)\). The filter of Hamilton (1989) alternates two steps. The prediction step moves the probabilities one period through the chain,

\[ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{t+1|t} = \boldsymbol{P}'\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t|t}, \tag{19.2}\]

and the update step reweights them by how well each regime explains the new observation,

\[ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{t|t} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t|t-1} \odot \boldsymbol{\eta}_t} {\boldsymbol{\iota}'\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t|t-1} \odot \boldsymbol{\eta}_t\right)}, \tag{19.3}\]

where \(\odot\) is the elementwise product. The denominator is the density of \(\boldsymbol{r}_t\) given the past, so the sum of its logarithms is the log-likelihood. It is maximized by the EM algorithm of Dempster et al. (1977), which for a hidden Markov chain is the procedure of Baum et al. (1970) and was adapted to regime-switching models by Hamilton (1990): given state probabilities, the means, variances and transition frequencies are weighted sample moments; given those, the probabilities are recomputed.

The probabilities EM needs use the whole sample. They are the smoothed probabilities \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t|T}\), obtained from the filtered ones by the backward recursion of Kim (1994),

\[ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{t|T} = \boldsymbol{\xi}_{t|t} \odot \left[\boldsymbol{P}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t+1|T} \oslash \boldsymbol{\xi}_{t+1|t}\right)\right], \tag{19.4}\]

with \(\oslash\) elementwise division. The difference between \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t|t}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t|T}\) is the subject of this chapter.

19.1.3 One-step-ahead moments as a mixture

An investor standing at \(t\) does not know \(s_{t+1}\). With \(\boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{\xi}_{t+1|t}\), next month’s return is a mixture of the regime distributions, with mean and covariance

\[ \bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = \sum_{s} \pi_s \boldsymbol{\mu}_s, \qquad \bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \sum_{s} \pi_s \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_s + \sum_{s} \pi_s \left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_s - \bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right) \left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_s - \bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right)'. \tag{19.5}\]

The covariance is not the average of the regime covariances. The second sum, the between-regime term, is the risk of not knowing which regime next month belongs to, and it is largest for the assets whose means differ most across regimes. A mixture of normals is also not normal. For a single series whose regimes share a mean, the variance is \(\bar{\sigma}^2 = \sum_s \pi_s \sigma_s^2\) and the excess kurtosis is

\[ \bar{K} = 3\left(\frac{\sum_s \pi_s \sigma_s^4}{\left(\sum_s \pi_s \sigma_s^2\right)^2} - 1\right) \;\geq\; 0, \tag{19.6}\]

strictly positive whenever the regime variances differ, and skewness appears as soon as the low-mean regime is also the high-variance one (Timmermann 2000). Regimes therefore produce the fat tails and the asymmetry of Section 4.3 from Gaussian ingredients, which Rydén et al. (1998) found sufficient for most stylized facts of daily returns. They produce them jointly, because all assets change regime together: the co-moments that Section 11.1 carries as tensors are here the by-product of two covariance matrices, and Ang and Chen (2002) find that a regime-switching model reproduces the higher correlations of falling markets documented by Longin and Solnik (2001) better than the alternatives they test.

19.1.4 Two ways to use the state

Given \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) there are two rules. The first treats Equation 19.5 as the inputs of Section 9.1 and holds the tangency portfolio of Equation 9.5 computed from \(\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) and \(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\); its weights move continuously with the probabilities. The second holds one of \(K\) portfolios, each built for one regime, and switches when the most probable regime changes,

\[ \boldsymbol{w}_t = \boldsymbol{w}^{(\hat{s}_{t+1})}, \qquad \hat{s}_{t+1} = \arg\max_s \, \pi_s . \tag{19.7}\]

The two agree when one probability is one and differ in between, where the mixture rule holds a compromise and the switching rule a bet. Clarke and Silva (1998) showed for two states how a state-dependent mix enlarges the opportunity set of a static one. Ang and Bekaert (2002) solve the dynamic problem of an investor with constant relative risk aversion and find the cost of ignoring regimes small for an all-equity investor and substantial once a risk-free asset can be held, because the bear regime is then a reason to leave the market; Ang and Bekaert (2004) turn that into a mean-variance rule and report that it outperforms static allocations out of sample. Guidolin and Timmermann (2007) need four regimes to describe stocks and bonds jointly, Guidolin and Timmermann (2008) add preferences over skewness and kurtosis, and Tu (2010) finds that a Bayesian investor who ignores regimes gives up more than two percent a year in certainty-equivalent terms. The out-of-sample evidence on simple switching rules in Bulla et al. (2011) and Nystrup et al. (2015), both of which leave equities for safer assets on a filtered state, is favorable.

All of these condition on a state estimated from the data, and the estimate is where the difficulty lies.

19.1.5 Dating and detecting

A regime can be established in two ways that are easily confused. Dating assigns each month to a regime with the whole sample in view: by a rule that locates peaks and troughs, as in Pagan and Sossounov (2003), by naming episodes after the fact, or by the smoothed probabilities of Equation 19.4. Detecting assigns month \(t\) using only what was known at \(t\): the filtered probabilities of Equation 19.3, computed with parameters that were themselves estimated on data through \(t\). A turning point needs observations on both sides to be recognized, so detection lags dating, and Chauvet and Piger (2008) measure that lag for business-cycle turning points in months.

A backtest of a regime strategy has three places to leak. Smoothed probabilities at \(t\) contain returns after \(t\). Filtered probabilities computed with full-sample parameters contain them less visibly, because the parameters that describe the stress regime were learned from the very months the filter then appears to recognize. And the regime definition itself, the number of states and the series they are fitted to, is usually settled after looking at the sample. The first two are coding errors of the kind Section 16.2 guards against by lagging and asserting, and we assert them away below. The third is the design problem of Section 25.3 and cannot be asserted away.

19.1.6 Turbulence as a model-free detector

A regime model has parameters to estimate before it says anything. The turbulence index of Equation 5.6 has none beyond a trailing mean and covariance matrix: it measures how unusual this month’s return vector is by the standards of the recent past. Chow et al. (1999) used it to separate a calm from a turbulent subsample and blended the two covariance matrices according to the probability of, and the aversion to, each regime, which is the first sum of Equation 19.5 with \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) chosen by the investor. Kritzman and Li (2010) documented that turbulence is persistent and that risk is poorly rewarded while it lasts, the two properties a detector needs, and Kritzman et al. (2012) fitted hidden Markov models to turbulence and to macroeconomic series and reported out-of-sample gains from tilting on the filtered state. A threshold on the index is the simplest detector there is: stress when \(d_t\) exceeds a quantile of its own past.

19.2.2 A detected regime: the turbulence threshold

The index is the one of Section 5.3, from the same call. Before thresholding it we check what a value is made of: the package returns a monotone transform of Equation 5.6, \(\sqrt{d_t}/N\), computed from month \(t\) and the sixty months before it.

turb <- uncertaintymeasures::OSturbulence(R, rolling = TRUE, roll.obs = 60)$turb
tb   <- as.numeric(turb)
stopifnot(length(tb) == nrow(R), all(zoo::index(turb) == dates))

first_oos <- 121L                                    # the register's first out-of-sample month
past <- zoo::coredata(R)[(first_oos - 60L):(first_oos - 1L), ]
d    <- stats::mahalanobis(zoo::coredata(R)[first_oos, ], colMeans(past), stats::cov(past))
stopifnot(all.equal(tb[first_oos], sqrt(d) / N))

A month is flagged when the index exceeds the 75th percentile of its own history, the convention of Kritzman and Li (2010), with the percentile computed on an expanding window that ends the month before. The flag for month \(t\) is known at the end of month \(t\) and decides the portfolio held in \(t + 1\).

turb_flag <- function(tb, q = 0.75, burn = 24L) {
  threshold <- vapply(seq_along(tb), function(t) {
    past <- tb[seq_len(t - 1L)]; past <- past[!is.na(past)]
    if (length(past) < burn) NA_real_ else stats::quantile(past, q, names = FALSE)
  }, numeric(1))
  tb > threshold
}
flag_turb <- turb_flag(tb)

# No look-ahead: reverse everything after `cut`, and the flags through `cut` must not move.
cut    <- first_oos + 119L
tb_alt <- replace(tb, (cut + 1L):length(tb), rev(tb[(cut + 1L):length(tb)]))
stopifnot(identical(turb_flag(tb_alt)[seq_len(cut)], flag_turb[seq_len(cut)]),
          !identical(turb_flag(tb_alt), flag_turb))

19.2.3 A detected regime: a two-state hidden Markov model

Equation 19.1 on all sixteen classes with two states has 306 parameters, two mean vectors, two covariance matrices and two transition probabilities, against 372 months. We fit it instead to one summary series, the equal-weighted excess return of the sixteen, where the same model has six. No hidden-Markov package is needed: the filter of Equation 19.2 and Equation 19.3 is a loop, the smoother of Equation 19.4 another, and EM alternates them with weighted moments.

hamilton <- function(y, th) {
  n <- length(y); filt <- pred <- matrix(NA_real_, n, 2); loglik <- 0
  dens <- cbind(stats::dnorm(y, th$mu[1], th$sigma[1]), stats::dnorm(y, th$mu[2], th$sigma[2]))
  xi <- c(0.5, 0.5)                                  # prior for the first month
  for (t in seq_len(n)) {
    pred[t, ] <- xi                                  # xi_{t|t-1}
    joint     <- xi * dens[t, ]
    loglik    <- loglik + log(sum(joint))
    filt[t, ] <- joint / sum(joint)                  # the update step
    xi        <- as.numeric(filt[t, ] %*% th$P)      # the prediction step
  }
  list(filtered = filt, predicted = pred, loglik = loglik)
}

kim <- function(h, P) {                              # smoothed probabilities
  sm <- h$filtered
  for (t in (nrow(sm) - 1L):1L)
    sm[t, ] <- h$filtered[t, ] * as.numeric(P %*% (sm[t + 1L, ] / h$predicted[t + 1L, ]))
  sm
}

fit_hmm <- function(y, max_iter = 200L, tol = 1e-6) {
  far <- abs(y - stats::median(y)); hi <- far > stats::quantile(far, 0.8)
  th  <- list(mu = c(mean(y[!hi]), mean(y[hi])), sigma = c(stats::sd(y[!hi]), stats::sd(y[hi])),
              P = rbind(c(0.95, 0.05), c(0.10, 0.90)))
  floor_sd <- stats::sd(y) / 4; ll_old <- -Inf; n <- length(y)
  for (iter in seq_len(max_iter)) {
    h  <- hamilton(y, th); sm <- kim(h, th$P)                                # E-step
    J  <- (t(h$filtered[-n, ]) %*% (sm[-1L, ] / h$predicted[-1L, ])) * th$P  # expected transitions
    th$P     <- J / rowSums(J)                                               # M-step
    th$mu    <- colSums(sm * y) / colSums(sm)
    th$sigma <- pmax(sqrt(colSums(sm * outer(y, th$mu, "-")^2) / colSums(sm)), floor_sd)
    if (h$loglik - ll_old < tol) break
    ll_old <- h$loglik
  }
  o <- order(th$sigma)                               # state 2 is the high-volatility state
  list(mu = th$mu[o], sigma = th$sigma[o], P = th$P[o, o], loglik = h$loglik, iter = iter)
}

y        <- rowMeans(X)                              # equal-weighted excess return
fit_full <- fit_hmm(y)
smoothed <- kim(hamilton(y, fit_full), fit_full$P)[, 2]
round(c(mu = 12 * fit_full$mu, sigma = sqrt(12) * fit_full$sigma, stay = diag(fit_full$P)), 3)
#>    mu1    mu2 sigma1 sigma2  stay1  stay2 
#>  0.049  0.053  0.061  0.142  0.984  0.931

The volatility floor is there because the likelihood of a Gaussian mixture is unbounded when one state collapses onto a single observation. On the full sample the two states differ in volatility, 6.1% against 14.2% a year, and hardly in mean; the high-volatility state lasts 15 months on average and the other 63.

With long-run shares \(\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}} = (0.81, 0.19)\) and regime volatilities of \(\sigma_1 = 1.77\) and \(\sigma_2 = 4.10\) percent a month, the scalar version of Equation 19.5 gives

\[ \bar{\sigma}^2 = \underbrace{0.81 \times 1.77^2 + 0.19 \times 4.10^2}_{\text{within}} + \underbrace{0.0002}_{\text{between}} = 5.68 , \]

a volatility of 2.38 percent a month against 2.33 in the sample, and Equation 19.6 gives

\[ \bar{K} = 3\left(\frac{0.81 \times 1.77^4 + 0.19 \times 4.10^4} {5.68^2} - 1\right) = 2.63 . \]

Two Gaussian regimes account for an excess kurtosis of 2.6 of the 5.3 in the sample. They account for none of its skewness of -0.90: the fitted regime means are almost equal, so the between-regime term is 0.003% of the variance. What this model has found is a volatility regime rather than a bear market.

Those estimates used every month. In real time the parameters are re-estimated each December on an expanding window, the filter is run with them through each month of the following year, and nothing after month \(t\) enters the probability for \(t\).

hmm_realtime <- function(y, start) {
  refit <- seq(start, length(y), by = 12L)
  fits  <- lapply(refit, function(e) fit_hmm(y[seq_len(e)]))
  prob  <- t(vapply(start:length(y), function(t) {
    th <- fits[[max(which(refit <= t))]]             # the latest fit, on data through its own date
    xi <- hamilton(y[seq_len(t)], th)$filtered[t, ]
    c(filtered = xi[2], predicted = as.numeric(xi %*% th$P)[2])
  }, numeric(2)))
  list(refit = refit, fits = fits, month = start:length(y), prob = prob)
}
rt <- hmm_realtime(y, start = first_oos - 1L)

# No look-ahead: the same procedure on a truncated sample returns the same probabilities.
cut_h <- first_oos + 23L

                    rt$prob[rt$month <= cut_h, ]))
live     <- rt$month                                 # months in which both detectors exist
flag_hmm <- rep(NA, length(y)); flag_hmm[live] <- rt$prob[, "filtered"] > 0.5
detect <- lapply(seq_len(nrow(ep)), function(i) {
  m   <- intersect(which(in_episode(dates, ep$episode[i])), live)

  tibble::tibble(episode = ep$label[i], months = length(m),
                 turb_lag = lag(flag_turb), turb_share = mean(flag_turb[m]),

                 smoothed = mean(smoothed[calm_live] > 0.5))

Figure 19.1 and Table 19.2 are the chapter’s argument in small. With hindsight the model is clean: the smoothed probability is above one half in 100% of the credit-crisis months and in 16% of the months outside any episode. In real time it is not. Before the credit crisis the sample contains no high-volatility regime to learn from, so the second state is fitted to whatever divides a calm sample: EM stops at its iteration limit in 6 of the 22 refits, and the filtered probability is above one half in 26% of the months before that crisis against 1% for the smoothed one. A regime cannot be detected by a model that has not yet seen it. Once it has, the filter recognizes the liquidity shock 1 month after it begins and the rates shock after 5; the turbulence threshold, which needs no stress history, flags the credit crisis in its first month and the other two after 1 and 2. Neither sees the equity bear market as a whole, a slow decline with bonds rising that is unusual in direction and not in size. Outside the episodes the threshold flags 15% of months and the filter 35%.

19.2.4 Regime portfolios in PortfolioAnalytics

PortfolioAnalytics ships the machinery for the switching rule. combine.portfolios() turns a list of specifications into one portfolio.list object; regime.portfolios() attaches a regime series to such a list; and optimize.portfolio.rebalancing() then solves, at each rebalancing date, the specification the regime selects. Our two specifications are a calm portfolio, the long-only tangency of Equation 9.5, and a stress portfolio, long-only minimum variance.

win <- 60L
base_spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only")
calm_spec   <- base_spec |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "StdDev")
stress_spec <- base_spec |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "StdDev")

both <- combine.portfolios(list(calm_spec, stress_spec))
opt_both <- optimize.portfolio(Re[(first_oos - win):(first_oos - 1L), ], both,
                               optimize_method = "ROI", maxSR = TRUE)
w_both <- extractWeights(opt_both)
class(opt_both)
#> [1] "opt.list"

Handed to optimize.portfolio() directly, a portfolio.list is solved specification by specification and extractWeights() returns a matrix with a row for each. The backtest needs both portfolios at every month. The stress portfolio comes from optimize.portfolio.rebalancing() on a sixty-month window, set up as in Section 25.2. The calm one is computed as the single quadratic program of Section 8.2, because maxSR = TRUE under ROI is a one-dimensional search that solves a quadratic program at every step, and the package run below confirms that the two routes agree.

# Long-only tangency: min y'Sy subject to y'mu = 1, y >= 0, then w = y / sum(y).
tan_qp <- function(mu, S) {
  n <- length(mu)
  if (max(mu) <= 0) return(setNames(rep(NA_real_, n), names(mu)))
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S * 1e4, dvec = rep(0, n),
                            Amat = cbind(mu * 1e2, diag(n)), bvec = c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)
  y <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  setNames(y / sum(y), names(mu))
}

NoteWhat regime.portfolios() does with the regime series

Each line is one of the measurements above.

  • The regime is a list index. optimize.portfolio() selects portfolio.list[[regime]], so the series must be coded 1, 2, … in the order of the list. A series coded 0 and 1 passes the constructor. A stress month, coded 1, then receives the calm specification without a message, and a calm month, coded 0, fails and leaves a row of NA in the weights.
  • The lookup is contemporaneous. The regime is read at the last date of the estimation window, the rebalancing date. The series must hold at each date what was known on that date; a hand-dated series is used unlagged, and a regime dated with hindsight enters the backtest with nothing to mark it.
  • Dates must match exactly. When the rebalancing date is missing from the regime index, here because the index is a day off, the first specification is used and the only trace is a warning.
  • Every value needs a specification and every specification a value. The constructor counts distinct values and refuses a series with a third value, with an NA, or one that never visits a regime.
  • Only the assets must agree. Constraints and objectives may differ: a stress specification confined to half the budget, with the rest in the bill, returns weights that sum to 0.5.
  • Arguments in ... reach every specification. maxSR = TRUE is passed to the stress specification too, which has no return objective; its weights are unaffected and a warning is raised at each stress date.
  • extractWeights() returns one xts, a row per rebalancing date, whichever specification produced it, and the regime used is in $opt_rebalancing[[i]]$regime. extractObjectiveMeasures() and extractStats() return lists by regime and label them differently: the first sorts the regimes, the second numbers them in order of first appearance, so that here its regime.1 holds the 11 stress dates.

19.3 Detected Against Dated Regimes, Out of Sample

19.3.1 The strategies and their accounting

From observation 121, the first month of model year 11 and the start of the register of Section 25.3, we run the static policy, which holds the calm portfolio throughout, and four alternatives; the stress portfolio held throughout is reported beside them as a second static row. Three switch to the stress portfolio by Equation 19.7: on the dated episodes, using the label of the month the return is earned in, which no investor had and which is the infeasible upper bound; on the turbulence flag of the rebalancing month; and on the predicted probability of Equation 19.2 from the real-time filter. The fourth holds the tangency portfolio of the mixture moments of Equation 19.5. For it the regime moments of the sixteen classes are sample moments weighted by the smoothed probabilities of each December’s fit, on the expanding sample because a sixty-month window need not contain a stress month at all.

regime_moments <- function(k) {                      # weighted moments of the sixteen at refit k
  e  <- rt$refit[k]; th <- rt$fits[[k]]
  sm <- kim(hamilton(y[seq_len(e)], th), th$P)
  list(m = lapply(1:2, function(s) stats::cov.wt(X[seq_len(e), ], wt = sm[, s] / sum(sm[, s]),
                                                 method = "ML")),
       share = colMeans(sm), n_eff = colSums(sm),
       all = stats::cov.wt(X[seq_len(e), ], method = "ML"))
}
mixture <- function(m, p) {                          # the mixture moments
  mu <- p[1] * m[[1]]$center + p[2] * m[[2]]$center
  S  <- p[1] * (m[[1]]$cov + tcrossprod(m[[1]]$center - mu)) +
        p[2] * (m[[2]]$cov + tcrossprod(m[[2]]$center - mu))
  list(mu = mu, S = S)
}

The accounting is that of the register: weights set at the end of month \(t\) earn the returns of \(t + 1\), drift, and are traded back at the turnover of Equation 23.1 and the cost of Equation 23.2, at the per-class rates of pm_costs_default() from Section 23.3. A switch is a trade like any other, so its cost is in the same column.

cost_rate <- pm_costs_default()[sub(".*_", "", colnames(R))]
stopifnot(!anyNA(cost_rate)); names(cost_rate) <- colnames(R)

run_path <- function(W) {
  stopifnot(identical(colnames(W), colnames(R)), nrow(W) == length(oos))
  Rm    <- zoo::coredata(R)[oos, ]
  gross <- rowSums(W * Rm)                                       # month t+1, weights set at t
  held  <- rbind(0, (W * (1 + Rm) / (1 + gross))[-nrow(W), ])    # drifted weights; flat before the first trade
  trade <- abs(W - held)
  tibble::tibble(date = dates[oos], gross = gross, turnover = rowSums(trade) / 2,
                 cost = as.numeric(trade %*% cost_rate)) |>
    dplyr::mutate(net = gross - cost)
}
sharpe_of <- function(r) {                                       # as the register: compounded, over the bill
  n <- length(r)
  (prod(1 + r)^(12 / n) - prod(1 + rf[oos])^(12 / n)) / (stats::sd(r) * sqrt(12))
}
performance <- function(path) {
  x <- xts::xts(path$gross, order.by = path$date)
  tibble::tibble(vol = stats::sd(path$gross) * sqrt(12), sharpe = sharpe_of(path$gross),
                 es = as.numeric(PerformanceAnalytics::ES(x, p = 0.95, method = "historical",
                                                          invert = FALSE)),
                 drawdown = as.numeric(PerformanceAnalytics::maxDrawdown(x)),
                 turnover = 12 * mean(path$turnover), cost = 12 * mean(path$cost),
                 sharpe_net = sharpe_of(path$net))
}
# Difference of two Sharpe ratios in standard errors (Jobson-Korkie, as corrected by Memmel).
z_against <- function(p, q) {
  a <- p$gross - rf[oos]; b <- q$gross - rf[oos]
  s <- c(mean(a) / stats::sd(a), mean(b) / stats::sd(b)); rho <- stats::cor(a, b)
  (s[1] - s[2]) / sqrt((2 - 2 * rho + (s[1]^2 + s[2]^2 - 2 * s[1] * s[2] * rho^2) / 2) / length(a))
}

19.3.2 Reading the result

The two static rows are checks before they are results. The static policy’s Sharpe ratio of 0.546 is the register’s 0.546 for the maximum-Sharpe strategy at this window, and the stress portfolio’s 0.828 is its 0.829 for minimum variance, both read from output/tables/oos-sweep.csv; the accounting here is the register’s.

With the regimes dated, switching works. The infeasible strategy raises the Sharpe ratio from 0.546 to 0.811 and cuts the maximum drawdown from 20.1% to 12.6% while spending 12% of the months in the stress portfolio. The constant mix with the same share earns 0.582, so 0.23 of the gain is timing and the rest is holding some of a portfolio that the register already ranks above the calm one.

That last column is what the detected strategies have to be read against. The turbulence switch reaches 0.582, which keeps 14% of the dated strategy’s advantage over the static policy, and it is -0.02 against its own constant mix: the flag chooses its months no better than a fixed blend would, at 142% turnover and 24 basis points a year in costs against 15 for the static policy. The filtered model reaches 0.525, below the static policy, and it is in the stress portfolio in 37% of all months against 12% for the dated regimes: in 29% of the months before the credit crisis, where the smoothed probability of Figure 19.1 marks almost nothing, and in 41% of the months from the crisis on, most of them the model years of elevated volatility that follow it. The constant mix with its share earns 0.659, so its timing is worth -0.13, against +0.23 for the dated regimes. The mixture rule, at 0.524, is below the static policy: its regime covariance matrices are estimated from as few as 4 effective months, and Equation 19.5 passes that error to the optimizer.

None of this is distinguishable from sampling error. By Equation 25.2 the standard error of the static policy’s Sharpe ratio over 252 months is 0.22; the paired statistic in the z column, which uses the correlation between the two return series (Memmel 2003), is +0.30 for the turbulence switch and -0.30 for the filtered model, and +2.78 for the strategy that knew the dates. And one row needs no detector at all: the stress portfolio held throughout has a Sharpe ratio of 0.828, above both detected strategies. On this market the question of when to hold the minimum-variance portfolio matters less than the question of whether to.

Table 19.4 shows where each number comes from. In the credit crisis the static policy loses 10.8% and the stress portfolio 1.6%. The turbulence switch, flagged in the first month, captures most of the difference at -2.8%. The filtered model, at -10.7%, captures almost none of it: it flags the crisis one month after it begins, the switch takes effect the month after that, and by then the static policy had already lost 13.5%, against 10.8% for the episode as a whole. That is the episode a two-state model is built for, and a lag of two months was enough to miss it. In the rates shock there is nothing to capture: the stress portfolio is mostly government bonds, it loses 10.1%, and the investor who knew every date loses the same. The detected strategies lose more, 12.4% and 13.8%, because they enter and leave during it. A stress portfolio is a hedge against the stress that resembles the past, and Table 19.1 had already said that the fourth episode does not.

Three limits on that conclusion. One path of 252 months contains 4 episodes, and the ranking of the detectors rests on them. The threshold, the summary series, the number of states and the two portfolios were each chosen once and not searched over, which is why the exercises vary them rather than the chapter. And the stress portfolio is a model too: what to do when the regime model itself may be wrong, rather than only its state, is the question of Section 28.1.

Key Takeaways

  • A regime-switching model is Equation 19.1 plus a filter. The prediction and update steps of Equation 19.2 and Equation 19.3 give the probability of each state from data through \(t\); the smoother of Equation 19.4 gives it from the whole sample, and only the first is available to an investor (Hamilton 1989; Kim 1994).
  • One month ahead the investor faces the mixture moments of Equation 19.5. The covariance contains a between-regime term, and a mixture of Gaussian regimes has fat tails by Equation 19.6; the two-state model of the equal-weighted return accounts for about half of its excess kurtosis and none of its skewness.
  • Dating and detecting are different operations. Smoothed probabilities and full-sample parameters both carry the future into a backtest, and before the first stress regime has occurred a two-state model has nothing to detect it with.
  • regime.portfolios() switches between specifications by a regime series and is equivalent to selecting rows from separate rebalancing runs. It reads the regime of the rebalancing date itself, uses the regime value as a list index, and checks neither the coding nor the timing of the series.
  • On this market the infeasible dated switch is well ahead of the static policy; the turbulence switch keeps a small part of that by point estimate, the filtered model none, neither is distinguishable from the static policy by a paired test, and holding the stress portfolio throughout has a higher Sharpe ratio than either detector. In the rates shock no version of the switch helps, because the stress portfolio was built for a different stress.
