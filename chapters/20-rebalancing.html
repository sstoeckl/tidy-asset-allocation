22  Rebalancing and What It Costs

The question. The paper portfolio is free. What does the real one cost?

Every portfolio in this book so far has been rebalanced back to its target weights each month at no charge. That assumption is not a small one. It is worth more to some strategies than others, and this chapter measures how much — using the out-of-sample runs of Section 24.1, which were computed with and without costs from the start for exactly this purpose.

sweep <- readr::read_csv("output/tables/oos-sweep.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
R <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)
R <- R[, !grepl("money_market", colnames(R))]

22.1 What you need

22.1.1 Turnover is a measure of how much you traded

# Half the L1 distance between the weights you drifted into and the weights you
# want. Halved because selling one asset to buy another is one trade, counted
# once, not twice.
turnover <- function(w_before, w_after) sum(abs(w_after - w_before)) / 2

PortfolioAnalytics exports this as turnover(), taking the same definition. Annualise it by summing over a year, and it becomes the number a trading desk recognises: a hundred per cent turnover means the portfolio was replaced once.

22.1.2 Costs are not a single number

The rate depends on what you are trading. A government bond is cheap; a high-yield credit position is not; an emerging equity sits between. Using one average rate across a multi-asset portfolio charges the cheap sleeves too much and the dear ones too little, and it is exactly the sleeves you trade most that are misestimated.

pm_costs_default()
#> money_market   government    commodity       equity       credit     .default 
#>       0.0001       0.0002       0.0008       0.0010       0.0025       0.0010

These are one-way rates in decimal — 2 basis points for a government bond against 25 for credit. They are arguments with defaults, not constants, and Appendix B records where the numbers come from. Vary them; the conclusions below survive a factor of two either way, and a chapter that told you otherwise would be hiding its sensitivity.

Note which class is dearest. US_credit had the highest Sharpe ratio in Table 3.1 and the optimiser in Section 9.1 bought it heavily. It is also the most expensive thing in the universe to trade.

22.2 How to actually do it

22.2.1 A turnover constraint is not a turnover constraint everywhere

PortfolioAnalytics will accept turnover_constraint() under both solver families, and they mean different things by it.

Under ROI it is a hard constraint: the solver will not return a portfolio that trades more than the limit. Under CVXR it is implemented as a penalty added to the objective, so a sufficiently attractive portfolio buys its way past the limit and the limit bends. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions, and only one of them is what “constraint” normally means.

The same split applies to costs. transaction_cost_constraint() exists only under ROI. Ask for proportional costs under CVXR and they are not applied — and you are not told.

22.2.2 The accounting trap that quietly rewrites your backtest

PerformanceAnalytics::Return.portfolio() is the obvious way to turn a weight history into a return series. It has a behaviour worth knowing before you rely on it.

Rs <- R[1:24, ]

# Weights that begin deliberately late -- as they must, since the first ones
# cannot be computed until the estimation window has filled.
w <- xts::xts(matrix(0, 2, ncol(Rs), dimnames = list(NULL, colnames(Rs))),
              order.by = zoo::index(Rs)[c(12, 18)])
w[, "US_government"] <- 1

pr <- Return.portfolio(Rs, weights = w)

The call warns, and the warning is the whole story: “First weights period is after the start of the data series. Setting weights for the preceding periods to equal weight.”

tibble::tibble(
  month     = format(zoo::index(pr), "%Y-%m"),
  portfolio = as.numeric(pr),
  if_equal  = rowMeans(zoo::coredata(Rs)),
  if_stated = as.numeric(Rs[, "US_government"])
) |>
  dplyr::slice(c(1:3, 11:14))
#> # A tibble: 7 × 4
#>   month   portfolio if_equal if_stated
#>   <chr>       <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>
#> 1 1992-01  -0.00258 -0.00258  -0.0172 
#> 2 1992-02  -0.0249  -0.0256   -0.00762
#> 3 1992-03  -0.0274  -0.0283   -0.00381
#> 4 1992-11  -0.0209  -0.0216   -0.00575
#> 5 1992-12   0.0153   0.0172    0.0150 
#> 6 1993-01   0.0164   0.0206    0.0164 
#> 7 1993-02   0.0278   0.0261    0.0278

For the first eleven months the series tracks the equal-weighted portfolio, not the one that was specified. In a rolling backtest the estimation window always consumes the start of the sample, so this is not an edge case — it is the normal case, and it silently prepends a strategy nobody chose. tidypm::pm_backtest() does the drift-and-rebalance accounting explicitly instead, in about twenty-five auditable lines.

22.3 What else is in there

Return.portfolio(verbose = TRUE) returns far more than a return series.

detail <- Return.portfolio(Rs, weights = w, verbose = TRUE)
names(detail)
#> [1] "returns"      "contribution" "BOP.Weight"   "EOP.Weight"   "BOP.Value"   
#> [6] "EOP.Value"

BOP.Weight and EOP.Weight are the weights at the beginning and end of each period — before and after drift. Their difference is the turnover, without reconstructing it:

drift <- rowSums(abs(zoo::coredata(detail$BOP.Weight) -
                     zoo::coredata(detail$EOP.Weight))) / 2
round(utils::head(drift[drift > 0], 5), 4)
#> [1] 0.0155 0.0088 0.0117 0.0154 0.0066

contribution breaks the portfolio return down by asset for every period, which is the raw material for Section 23.1. Most readers take $returns and never discover that the rest is there.

22.4 Why it works

22.4.1 Trading less helps the rules and hurts the optimisers

freq <- sweep |>
  dplyr::filter(window == 60, basis == "gross") |>
  dplyr::select(strategy, frequency, sharpe, ann_turnover) |>
  tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = frequency,
                     values_from = c(sharpe, ann_turnover))

The first three numeric columns are Sharpe ratios; the last three are annual turnover.

Equal weighting and inverse volatility improve when you leave them alone — 0.387 to 0.401 — while trading a quarter as much. Maximum Sharpe collapses from 0.232 to 0.079.

The reason is what each strategy is tracking. A rule-based weight is a target: letting it drift is a small, mean-reverting error, and rebalancing to it more often mostly buys noise. An optimised weight is an estimate: it decays as the sample it was fitted to recedes, and the strategy needs frequent re-estimation precisely because the thing it estimated will not hold still. Section 3.2 measured that decay directly — the rolling mean wanders by more than its own level.

So the optimisers are caught between two costs. Rebalance often and pay to chase an unstable estimate; rebalance rarely and hold a stale one.

22.4.2 And the cost falls hardest on the strategies that can least afford it

bite <- sweep |>
  dplyr::select(universe, window, frequency, strategy, basis, sharpe) |>
  tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = basis, values_from = sharpe) |>
  dplyr::mutate(bite = gross - net) |>
  dplyr::group_by(strategy) |>
  dplyr::summarise(gross = median(gross), net = median(net),
                   median_bite = median(bite), worst_bite = max(bite),
                   .groups = "drop") |>
  dplyr::arrange(dplyr::desc(median_bite))

The ordering is monotone in how much a strategy optimises. Maximum Sharpe loses a median of 0.021 and up to 0.046; equal weighting loses 0.003.

Put beside its gross figure, the maximum-Sharpe portfolio surrenders about a sixth of a Sharpe ratio that was already the lowest in the set. It is the most expensive strategy to run and the worst one to hold — and neither fact is visible in Section 9.4, where it is the best portfolio in the chapter.

That is the case for treating costs as part of the objective rather than as an audit afterwards, which is what turnover_objective() and transaction_cost_constraint() are for, and what Section 18.1 does by construction.

Exercises

  1. Double every rate in pm_costs_default() and recompute Table 22.2. Does the ordering change, or only the magnitudes? At what multiple does the maximum-Sharpe strategy’s net Sharpe reach zero?
  2. The cost model charges by asset class. Replace it with a single flat rate equal to the average and compare. Which strategies are flattered by the simplification, and why?
  3. Add a turnover_constraint() capping annual turnover at 25 per cent to the maximum-Sharpe specification and rerun it under both ROI and CVXR. Measure the realised turnover under each. Which one bound?
  4. Using Return.portfolio(verbose = TRUE), compute the drift turnover of an equal-weighted portfolio that is never rebalanced. How far does it get from equal weighting after thirty years, and which series does it become?
