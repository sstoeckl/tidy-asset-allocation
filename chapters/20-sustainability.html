20  Sustainable Investing Constraints

An investor who excludes a set of names, or who requires the portfolio’s average sustainability rating to clear a floor, has added a line to the mandate: what does the line cost, and who pays? Pedersen et al. (2021) answered the first question by drawing the ESG-efficient frontier, the highest Sharpe ratio attainable at each level of a portfolio’s rating, so that the cost of a floor is a distance along a curve rather than an opinion; Pástor et al. (2021) answered the second in equilibrium, where a taste for green assets lowers their expected return, so that the investor who holds them pays in expectation and the firm that issues them is paid in a lower cost of capital. The two results together fix what this chapter can and cannot claim: a constraint that does not bind costs nothing, a constraint that binds costs tracking error and expected return at a rate the Lagrange multiplier of Section 12.1 prices, and neither is an alpha.

We first set out the investor’s problem with a taste for a rating and with a floor on it, the exclusion as a restriction of the universe, the tilt as a factor-exposure constraint on a score, and the two facts that complicate all three, that a rating is an estimate and that tastes are priced; then construct rating levels for the 123 equities from the rating changes the modeled market ships, and implement an exclusion, a floor and a best-in-class floor on the equity sleeve of Section 21.2, auditing each with the functions of Section 12.2; then measure what the three cost out of sample inside the policy of Section 15.3, and how much of an exclusion list survives a second rater. On the horizon axis of Section 3.1 a sustainability constraint is strategic: it is a permanent line of the policy, set once, and it conditions on nothing but the ratings in force.

20.1 Tastes, Floors, and Exclusions

20.1.1 The investor’s problem with a taste for a rating

Let \(\boldsymbol{s}\) be a vector of ratings over the \(N\) names, one number per name on a fixed scale, and \(\bar{s}_{\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{s}'\boldsymbol{w}\) the portfolio’s rating. Pedersen et al. (2021) give the investor of Section 3.1 a third term,

\[ \max_{\boldsymbol{w}} \; \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} + \theta\,\boldsymbol{s}'\boldsymbol{w}, \tag{20.1}\]

a taste \(\theta \geq 0\) for the rating, and show that every solution lies on the ESG-efficient frontier: for each attainable \(\bar{s}\), the portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio among those with \(\boldsymbol{s}'\boldsymbol{w} = \bar{s}\). An investor with \(\theta = 0\) sits at the frontier’s peak, the tangency portfolio of Equation 9.5; an investor with a taste moves along it, giving up Sharpe ratio for rating at the rate the curve’s slope sets. The same frontier is traced by a mandate written as a floor,

\[ \min_{\boldsymbol{w}} \; \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{w} = 1, \;\; \boldsymbol{w} \geq \boldsymbol{0}, \;\; \boldsymbol{s}'\boldsymbol{w} \geq \bar{s}, \tag{20.2}\]

because the floor’s multiplier \(\lambda_s\) in the Lagrangian of Equation 12.2 is the taste that would have produced the same portfolio: \(\theta = \lambda_s\) at the optimum. Complementary slackness then says what the floor costs. When the unconstrained portfolio already clears \(\bar{s}\), \(\lambda_s = 0\) and the line could have been left out; when it binds, \(\lambda_s > 0\) is the shadow price of Equation 12.3, the rate at which variance, or tracking error when the objective is active risk, falls per notch of floor given up. A floor is a constraint of the linear-exposure family of Table 12.1, so the feasible set stays convex and Equation 20.2 is one quadratic program (Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004). Jagannathan and Ma (2003) and Frost and Savarino (1988) read a binding constraint as information about the estimates; a sustainability floor is not that. It is a preference, and its cost is a cost, which is why Pedersen et al. (2021) draw it as a frontier rather than argue it away.

20.1.2 Exclusion as a restriction of the universe

The oldest form of the constraint is a list of names that may not be held, \(w_i = 0\) for \(i \in E\): a box constraint with both bounds at zero, or equivalently a smaller universe. For a capitalization-weighted sleeve with benchmark \(\boldsymbol{b}\), reweighting the survivors pro rata gives active weights

\[ x_i = \begin{cases} -\,b_i & i \in E, \\[2pt] \;\; b_i \dfrac{b_E}{1 - b_E} & i \notin E, \end{cases} \qquad b_E = \sum_{i \in E} b_i, \qquad \text{active share} = b_E , \tag{20.3}\]

so the active share of Cremers and Petajisto (2009) is the benchmark weight of the excluded set, and the tracking error of Equation 24.2 is \(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{x}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{x}}\) with that \(\boldsymbol{x}\): an exclusion costs active risk in proportion to how much of the index it removes and how differently the removed names move. For an optimized sleeve the cost has a sharper reading. In the first-order condition \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} = \lambda_0\boldsymbol{\iota} + \boldsymbol{\delta}\) of Equation 12.4, the multiplier of an excluded name is unrestricted in sign, and the Jagannathan–Ma identity still holds: the constrained portfolio is the unconstrained minimum-variance portfolio on \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma} - (\boldsymbol{\delta}\boldsymbol{\iota}' + \boldsymbol{\iota}\boldsymbol{\delta}')\). A name the optimizer would not have held anyway has \(\delta_i \geq 0\) and its exclusion is slack. A name it wanted has \(\delta_i < 0\), and excluding it is the same as raising every covariance involving it by \(-\delta_i\) until the optimizer drops it on its own. An exclusion that binds is therefore an implicit risk view about the excluded name, and \(-\delta_i\) is its price. Hong and Kacperczyk (2009) found the equilibrium counterpart: names shunned by norm-constrained institutions are held by fewer investors, trade at lower valuations and earn higher expected returns, which is the premium the excluding investor forgoes; Heinkel et al. (2001) had derived the mechanism, a higher cost of capital for the excluded firm when enough capital excludes it.

20.1.3 Tilts as a factor-exposure constraint on a score

A floor on the portfolio’s rating is Equation 20.2, and in the vocabulary of Section 12.2 it is factor_exposure_constraint() with the score vector in place of a column of betas. A best-in-class mandate imposes the floor within each group \(g\) rather than across the universe, and because

\[ \frac{\sum_{i \in g} w_i s_i}{\sum_{i \in g} w_i} \;\geq\; \bar{s} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \sum_{i \in g} w_i \,(s_i - \bar{s}) \;\geq\; 0 , \tag{20.4}\]

it is linear too: one row per group of a factor-exposure matrix whose entries are the centered scores, bounded below at zero. The difference between the two forms is where the rating is bought. An across-the-universe floor lets the solver meet it in whichever region has the cheapest rating in tracking-error terms, which, when the regions’ budgets are fixed by the policy above the sleeve, means concentrating the tilt where it costs least; a best-in-class floor spreads the tilt and costs more, which Equation 20.4 makes measurable.

20.1.4 A rating is an estimate

The score in Equation 20.2 is treated as data, and it is not. Berg et al. (2022) document that the ratings of six major providers for the same firm correlate at 0.54 on average, with pairwise correlations from 0.38 to 0.71, and decompose the divergence into what is measured, how it is measured and how the pieces are weighted. For a constraint the consequence is direct: the floor is imposed on \(\hat{\boldsymbol{s}} = \boldsymbol{s} + \boldsymbol{e}\), and with a different rater the same mandate excludes different names and tilts toward different ones. Avramov et al. (2022) put the error into Equation 20.1 and find that rating uncertainty lowers the demand for green assets and weakens their expected-return effect, as estimation error in \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) did in Section 8.1; Gibson Brandon et al. (2021) find that the disagreement itself is priced in the cross-section. The one dimension on which the measurement problem is smaller is carbon, where emissions are a quantity rather than an opinion, and Bolton and Kacperczyk (2021) find a premium on it. The modeled market carries no emissions data, so a carbon budget is the one construction this chapter cannot run; its mechanics are Equation 20.2 with emissions in place of a rating and an upper bound in place of a floor.

20.1.5 Tastes are priced

Finally, the equilibrium of Pástor et al. (2021): when investors have a taste for green assets, those assets’ expected returns are lower in equilibrium by the amount of the taste, so that the frontier of Pedersen et al. (2021) slopes down for the reason the demand curve slopes down. The realized returns of a green tilt can nevertheless be high for a long time, and Pástor et al. (2022) show that the green outperformance of their sample is what an unexpected strengthening of tastes produces, not what the equilibrium promises. A constraint and an alpha are different objects: the first is a cost paid for a property of the portfolio, the second a claim about expected returns that Section 24.1 would ask for an error bar on. The evidence on the alpha is mixed on purpose: Friede et al. (2015) aggregate some two thousand studies and find a non-negative relation in most of them, Serafeim and Yoon (2022) find that only financially material rating news moves prices, and Zerbib (2019) measures the green premium in bonds at a few basis points. None of that is needed to price the constraint, which is what the rest of the chapter does.

20.2 Implementation

20.2.1 A level from a path of changes

ImportantThe market ships rating changes, not ratings

sim_esg_changes_quarterly holds 1113 integer rating changes on 122 of the 123 equities, dated at quarter ends from model year 11 on, and no starting level, as Section 2.2 records. Cumulating the changes gives each name’s rating up to its own unknown constant, and a screen or a floor needs the constant. The chapter therefore constructs the levels under a stated assumption, and the assumption is an argument of a function so that a reader can change it.

The assumption is this. Each name has a latent score on a seven-point scale, the number of grades the common letter scales use; its starting value is drawn once, with the book’s seed, from a normal distribution centered on the scale’s midpoint with a standard deviation of 1.2 grades, so that the middle three grades hold 79% of the names in expectation and the extremes are rare, the bell shape a vendor’s grade distribution has; the shipped changes then move the score by whole grades from the quarter in which they are dated, and the score is clipped to the scale. A different distribution is a different rater, which Berg et al. (2022) say is the realistic case, and the application redraws it.

esg_start <- function(assets, dist = function(n) stats::rnorm(n, mean = 4, sd = 1.2),
                      seed = 1602, scale = c(1, 7)) {
  set.seed(seed)
  setNames(pmin(pmax(dist(length(assets)), scale[1]), scale[2]), assets)
}
esg_levels <- function(start, changes = sim_esg_changes_quarterly, dates = d, scale = c(1, 7)) {
  stopifnot(all(changes$asset %in% names(start)))
  L <- matrix(start, length(dates), length(start), byrow = TRUE,
              dimnames = list(NULL, names(start)))
  for (k in seq_len(nrow(changes))) {
    from <- match(TRUE, dates > changes$date[k])   # in force from the first month that starts after the change
    if (is.na(from)) next
    L[from:nrow(L), changes$asset[k]] <- L[from:nrow(L), changes$asset[k]] + changes$esg_change[k]
  }
  pmin(pmax(L, scale[1]), scale[2])
}
grade <- function(s) pmin(pmax(round(s), 1), 7)

net <- tapply(esg$esg_change, esg$asset, sum)
c(share_upgrades = mean(esg$esg_change > 0), mean_net_change = mean(net),
  mean_score_t1 = mean(L[t1, ]), mean_score_end = mean(L[nrow(L), ]),

The changes are not symmetric: 65% of them are upgrades, and a rated name ends the sample 4.2 grades above where it started. On a bounded scale that drift is a compression toward the ceiling, and Table 20.1 shows it: a cross-section that began bell-shaped around grade 4 ends with 76% of the names at the top grade. Figure 20.1 draws the path.

One sentence settles what the ratings can and cannot do to the returns. Appendix A records that the changes have no effect on the returns, so any performance difference below is a universe and constraint effect, measured on a market in which no rating premium exists, and not a priced rating factor; what it can still be, an exposure the rating carries to characteristics that are priced, is what the application finds.

20.2.2 The sleeve, its benchmark, and its covariance

The sleeve is the one Section 21.2 built: all 123 names, capitalization weighted within region on the last capitalization before the month began, with the regional budgets fixed above the sleeve. Here the five regions receive one fifth each, the simplest multi-region policy; the application uses the policy’s own split. Moments are estimated on the 120 months before the register’s first out-of-sample month with the shrinkage of Section 7.2, because the sample matrix of 123 names on that window is singular by Equation 7.2.

W <- 120L
shrunk <- function(X) {
  S <- corpcor::cov.shrink(X, verbose = FALSE)
  matrix(as.numeric(S), ncol(X), ncol(X), dimnames = list(colnames(X), colnames(X)))
}
bench_of <- function(t, budget) {                   # regional budgets, cap weighted within region, by name
  b <- setNames(numeric(N), colnames(X))
  for (g in names(budget)) { j <- names(region)[region == g]; b[j] <- budget[[g]] * cap_w_of(t, j) }
  b
}
budget <- setNames(rep(0.2, length(regions)), regions)

S <- shrunk(X[(t1 - W):(t1 - 1L), ])

p_vol    <- function(w, S)    sqrt(12 * as.numeric(t(w) %*% S %*% w))
score_of <- function(w, s)    sum(w * s) / sum(w)

20.2.3 Exclusion

The screen removes the bottom fifth of each region by score, ties broken by position so that the count is fixed, and reweights the survivors within their region so that the policy’s regional budgets are untouched.

bottom_of <- function(s, q = 0.2)                    # the names in the bottom fifth of their region
  unlist(lapply(split(names(s), region[names(s)]), function(j)
    j[rank(s[j], ties.method = "first") <= ceiling(q * length(j))]), use.names = FALSE)
screen_w <- function(b, excl) {                      # drop the names, reweight within region
  w <- b; w[excl] <- 0
  for (g in unique(region[names(b)])) {
    j <- names(b)[region[names(b)] == g]
    if (sum(w[j]) > 0) w[j] <- w[j] * sum(b[j]) / sum(w[j])
  }
  w
}
excl <- bottom_of(s)
w_ex <- screen_w(b, excl)
b_E  <- sum(b[excl])
stopifnot(abs(sum(w_ex) - 1) < 1e-12, abs(sum(abs(w_ex - b)) / 2 - b_E) < 1e-12)   # eq-20-exclusion
c(excluded = length(excl), benchmark_weight_removed = b_E,
  active_share = sum(abs(w_ex - b)) / 2, tracking_error = te_of(w_ex, b, S),
  score_benchmark = score_of(b, s), score_screened = score_of(w_ex, s))

The screen removes 27 names carrying 18.7% of the benchmark, which by Equation 20.3 is the active share exactly, at a tracking error of 1.85% a year; it lifts the sleeve’s score from 4.13 to 4.44. For the optimized sleeve the price of each excluded name follows from the multipliers, and the identity of Equation 12.4 is checked with the excluded names’ multipliers in it.

gmv_lo <- function(S) {                              # long-only minimum variance, eq-09-gmv under w >= 0
  n <- ncol(S)
  w <- quadprog::solve.QP(2 * S, rep(0, n), cbind(rep(1, n), diag(n)), c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)$solution
  w <- pmax(w, 0); setNames(w / sum(w), colnames(S))
}
w_mv  <- gmv_lo(S)
keep  <- setdiff(colnames(X), excl)
w_mvx <- setNames(numeric(N), colnames(X)); w_mvx[keep] <- gmv_lo(S[keep, keep])
lambda0 <- as.numeric(t(w_mvx) %*% S %*% w_mvx)
delta   <- setNames(as.numeric(S %*% w_mvx) - lambda0, colnames(X))
free0   <- setdiff(names(which(w_mvx <= 1e-6)), excl)           # at zero by choice, not by exclusion
stopifnot(all(abs(delta[w_mvx > 1e-6]) < 1e-7), all(delta[free0] > -1e-7))   # complementary slackness
S_tilde <- S - (outer(delta, rep(1, N)) + outer(rep(1, N), delta))
w_jm    <- as.numeric(solve(S_tilde, rep(1, N))); w_jm <- w_jm / sum(w_jm)
stopifnot(max(abs(w_jm - w_mvx)) < 1e-6)                        # eq-12-jm with the exclusions in it
wanted  <- excl[delta[excl] < -1e-7]                            # excluded names the optimizer would have held
c(reproduced_to = max(abs(w_jm - w_mvx)), excluded = length(excl), binding = length(wanted),
  held_unconstrained = sum(w_mv[excl] > 1e-6),
  vol_unconstrained = p_vol(w_mv, S), vol_screened = p_vol(w_mvx, S))
#>          reproduced_to               excluded                binding 
#>  0.0000000000000002042 27.0000000000000000000 12.0000000000000000000 
#>     held_unconstrained      vol_unconstrained           vol_screened 
#> 10.0000000000000000000  0.0786390938852850191  0.0818493596425207276
round(sort(-delta[wanted] * 12 * 1e4, decreasing = TRUE)[1:3], 2)   # the dearest exclusions, annualized variance x 1e4
#> EU_EQU_26 JP_EQU_11 CH_EQU_11 
#>     33.50     31.66     20.65

Of the 27 exclusions, 12 bind on the minimum-variance sleeve and the rest are slack, names the optimizer would not have held given the others; for comparison, the unconstrained portfolio holds 10 of them. The identity reproduces the screened solution to 2e-16, and the multiplier of each binding exclusion is the amount by which its covariances would have to rise for the optimizer to drop it unasked. The screen costs the minimum-variance sleeve 0.32 percentage points of volatility, from 7.86% to 8.18%.

20.2.4 A floor on the sleeve’s rating

The floor enters as factor_exposure_constraint() with the score vector as \(\boldsymbol{B}\), bound by name and asserted, on a minimum-variance sleeve under a ten percent cap per name and the regional budgets as a group constraint with equal bounds, the construction of Section 12.2. The best-in-class form is Equation 20.4, one column per region of centered scores.

shrinkage_moments <- function(R, portfolio) {
  S <- shrunk(zoo::coredata(R))
  list(mu = colMeans(R), sigma = S, m3 = NULL, m4 = NULL)
}
grp  <- split(seq_len(N), region[colnames(X)])
gbud <- budget[names(grp)]
stopifnot(identical(names(grp), names(gbud)))
base_sleeve <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(X)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "box", min = 0, max = 0.10) |>
  add.constraint(type = "group", groups = grp, group_min = unname(gbud), group_max = unname(gbud))

floor_spec <- function(spec, s, floor) {             # s'w >= floor, across the universe
  stopifnot(identical(names(s), names(spec$assets)))
  spec |> add.constraint(type = "factor_exposure", B = s, lower = floor, upper = max(s))
}
bic_B <- function(s, floor, groups = unique(region[names(s)])) {   # eq-20-bic: one column per region
  B <- sapply(groups, function(g) (s - floor) * (region[names(s)] == g))
  rownames(B) <- names(s); B
}
bic_spec <- function(spec, s, floor) {
  B <- bic_B(s, floor); stopifnot(identical(rownames(B), names(spec$assets)))
  spec |> add.constraint(type = "factor_exposure", B = B, lower = rep(0, ncol(B)), upper = rep(max(s), ncol(B)))
}

NoteThe package has no tracking-error objective, so the second frontier is solved by hand

Section 24.2 established that PortfolioAnalytics cannot write a tracking-error budget, and the same is true of tracking error as an objective: the covariance of active returns is singular along the benchmark, which its quadratic path refuses. The minimum-tracking-error sleeve under the same floor is therefore \(\min_{\boldsymbol{w}} (\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{b})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{b})\) written directly in quadprog, which also returns the multipliers; the two routes are checked against each other on the problem both can solve.

min_te <- function(S, b, cap = pmax(0.10, b), B = NULL, lower = NULL, budget, mu = NULL) {
  # min (w-b)'S(w-b)  s.t. regional budgets, 0 <= w <= cap, B'w >= lower; with mu: min w'Sw instead
  n <- length(b); G <- sapply(names(budget), function(g) as.numeric(region[names(b)] == g))
  Amat <- cbind(G, diag(n), -diag(n)); bvec <- c(unname(budget), rep(0, n), -cap)
  k <- if (is.null(B)) 0L else NCOL(B)
  if (k > 0) { Amat <- cbind(Amat, B); bvec <- c(bvec, lower) }
  dvec <- if (is.null(mu)) 2 * as.numeric(S %*% b) else rep(0, n)
  sol  <- quadprog::solve.QP(2 * S, dvec, Amat, bvec, meq = length(budget))
  w <- pmax(sol$solution, 0); w <- setNames(w / sum(w), names(b))
  attr(w, "lambda") <- if (k > 0) sol$Lagrangian[ncol(Amat) - k + seq_len(k)] else numeric(0)
  w
}
# the quadratic program and the package agree on the minimum-variance problem with a floor
w_pa <- pa_minvar(floor_spec(base_sleeve, s, 5))

stopifnot(max(abs(w_pa - w_qp)) < 1e-5)

w_te0 <- min_te(S, b, budget = gbud)                                   # no floor: the benchmark itself

Read Figure 20.2 from the left. A floor across the universe at or below the benchmark’s own rating of 4.13 costs the minimum-tracking-error sleeve nothing, which the hidden check asserts: the solution is the benchmark and the multiplier is zero. Above it the cost rises at an increasing rate, as a convex frontier must, reaching 3.76% of tracking error at a floor of 5.5. The best-in-class form costs more at every floor, 4.99% at the top, and it already binds at a floor of 4, because the US benchmark’s own rating is 3.81 and a floor every region must clear cannot buy the rating where it is cheapest. The minimum-variance panel tells the same story in volatility from a different starting point: the unconstrained sleeve’s rating is 4.09, so floors below that are slack for it too.

20.2.6 The audit

Every solution above is checked against the specification it came from with the audit_spec() of Section 12.2, restricted to the constraint types this chapter writes, and the slack of each floor is read off directly.

audit_spec <- function(w, spec, tol = 1e-6) {
  w <- w[names(spec$assets)]
  dplyr::bind_rows(lapply(spec$constraints, function(cn) {
    if (!isTRUE(cn$enabled)) return(NULL)
    ok <- switch(cn$type,
      full_investment = , weight_sum = sum(w) >= cn$min_sum - tol && sum(w) <= cn$max_sum + tol,
      box = , long_only = all(w >= cn$min - tol) && all(w <= cn$max + tol),
      group = { g <- vapply(cn$groups, function(i) sum(w[i]), numeric(1))
                all(g >= cn$cLO - tol & g <= cn$cUP + tol) },
      factor_exposure = { e <- as.numeric(t(cn$B) %*% w)
                          all(e >= cn$lower - tol & e <= cn$upper + tol) },
      NA)
    tibble::tibble(constraint = cn$type, satisfied = ok)
  }))
}

20.3 What the Constraints Cost Out of Sample

The sleeve now sits inside the policy of Section 15.3, recomputed as Section 21.3 recomputed it, so that the equity budget \(\omega\) and its regional split are computed rather than quoted.

Re <- R - rf; S16 <- stats::cov(Re); mu16 <- colMeans(Re)
qp <- function(S, a) {       # min w'Sw s.t. a'w = 1, w >= 0, rescaled: a = 1 gives GMV, a = mu the tangency
  n <- ncol(S)
  w <- quadprog::solve.QP(2 * S, rep(0, n), cbind(a, diag(n)), c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)$solution
  w <- pmax(w, 0); setNames(w / sum(w), colnames(S))
}

The sleeve holds the 72 names of the EU and US regions at the policy’s split. Three constructions fill it, re-estimated monthly on the trailing 120 months from the register’s first out-of-sample month, with the ratings in force at each month’s start: the screen, which removes the bottom fifth of each region and reweights the survivors; the floor, a minimum-tracking-error sleeve whose rating must reach 5, one grade above the drawn cross-section’s center; and the best-in-class floor, 5 in each region. The floor is fixed for the whole period because the constraint is strategic, and the drift of Figure 20.1 will test what a fixed floor means on a rising scale. The benchmark, the capitalization-weighted sleeve at the same split and the same monthly machinery, is the unconstrained sleeve every cost is measured against.

Three things are in Table 20.2. First, the costs in active risk. The screen tracks the unconstrained sleeve at 3.03% a year, the floor at 1.44% and the best-in-class floor at 1.39%, each arriving at the policy multiplied by \(\omega\) as Equation 21.1 says. Second, the turnover. The unconstrained sleeve trades 36% a year to follow its own capitalization weights; the screen trades 43%, the excess being the 33 months in which a rating change moved a name across the quintile boundary and forced a sale or a repurchase, which is the trading Section 23.3 prices and which the net column charges at 10 basis points. Third, the returns, and here the table does not say what the construction led us to expect. The two floors earn information ratios of -0.30 and -0.06 against the unconstrained sleeve, within two standard errors of zero by Equation 24.10, and cost the policy 0.028 and 0.006 of Sharpe ratio. The screen earns -0.57, which is 2.6 standard errors from zero, and costs the policy 0.122 of Sharpe ratio, from 0.879 to 0.756. On a market whose returns do not respond to the ratings, a screen that costs two percentage points of return a year needs an explanation, and the first question is whether it is the draw.

Table 20.3 is the explanation. The shipped upgrades cluster by sector: Healthcare names gained 5.1 grades on average over the sample and Basic Materials names 3.2, and the sectors upgraded most are the sectors whose names earned least in this sample, with a rank correlation of -0.71 across the 8 sectors. A rating is therefore partly a sector label, and a screen that removes the lowest-rated fifth of each region removes names from the sectors that happened to pay, under any rater who sees the same changes. The returns do not respond to the ratings; the ratings respond to something the returns also respond to. That is the general case rather than a quirk of this market: real ratings load on industry, size and region (Berg et al. 2022), and Pedersen et al. (2021) price a rating constraint through exactly those exposures. The cost of a constraint on an unpriced characteristic is the cost of the priced characteristics it is correlated with, which is what Section 5.2 would recover by regressing the screen’s active return on the sector series.

Figure 20.3 is complementary slackness on a calendar. The floor binds from the register’s first month until July of model year 14, when the unconstrained sleeve’s own rating reaches 5 on the strength of the shipped upgrades; from then on the floor sleeve is the benchmark, its ex-ante tracking error is zero, and the line in the mandate costs nothing. Everything the floor cost in Table 20.2 was paid in the first 3.5 model years. A strategic constraint written as an absolute level on a scale that drifts is a constraint that expires; one written relative to the benchmark, a grade above it, would bind throughout and cost throughout, and the exercises ask for it.

20.3.1 A second rater

The last question is how much of the exclusion list is the rater’s. A second rater draws a different starting cross-section, correlated with the first at \(\rho\), sees the same shipped changes, and runs the same screen; the share of the first rater’s exclusions that the second does not exclude is the disagreement Berg et al. (2022) measured, in the units a mandate feels it. Their average correlation across providers is 0.54; a correlation of zero is a rater who shares nothing but the changes, and 0.8 a close one.

flip_share <- function(rho, seed, t) {
  L2 <- esg_levels(second_rater(rho, seed))
  e1 <- bottom_of(L[t, js]); e2 <- bottom_of(L2[t, js])
  1 - length(intersect(e1, e2)) / length(e1)
}
t_end <- nrow(X)

  dplyr::mutate(flipped = purrr::map2_dbl(rho, when, function(r, w)
    mean(vapply(seeds, function(sd) flip_share(r, sd, if (w == "register start") t1 else t_end), numeric(1)))))

At the correlation Berg et al. (2022) report, 54% of the first rater’s exclusion list is not on the second rater’s at the register’s start, and 28% at the end of the sample, when twenty model years of shared changes have pulled the two cross-sections together. With uncorrelated raters the lists share almost nothing at the start, 81% flipped, which is what two independent draws of a bottom fifth must give; with closely agreeing raters the list still turns over by 34%. The content of the mandate, which names are out, is therefore largely the rater’s choice, while its cost in Table 20.2 and the sector exposure behind that cost are not, because every rater sees the same changes. That is the state of the data, and it is the reason the chapter has priced the constraint rather than the names: the price survives a change of rater, the list does not. What a screen does to a backtest’s error bar is a question for Section 25.3, and whether the tilt’s realized cost is stable across re-estimation windows is the kind of question Section 28.3 asks of every construction in the book.

Key Takeaways

  • A taste for a rating and a floor on it trace the same ESG-efficient frontier (Pedersen et al. 2021): the floor’s multiplier is the taste, a floor the unconstrained portfolio already clears costs nothing, and one that binds costs variance or tracking error at the rate of Equation 12.3. In equilibrium the taste is priced (Pástor et al. 2021), so the constraint is a cost and not an alpha.
  • An exclusion is a box constraint at zero. For a capitalization-weighted sleeve its active share is the benchmark weight removed, Equation 20.3; for an optimized sleeve the Jagannathan–Ma identity holds with the excluded names’ multipliers in it, and a binding exclusion is the same as raising the excluded name’s covariances until the optimizer drops it (Jagannathan and Ma 2003; Hong and Kacperczyk 2009).
  • A rating floor is factor_exposure_constraint() with the score as \(\boldsymbol{B}\); a best-in-class floor is Equation 20.4, linear in centered scores, and costs more because it cannot buy the rating where it is cheapest. The package cannot write tracking error as an objective, so the minimum-tracking-error sleeve is solved by hand and checked against the package on the problem both can solve.
  • The modeled market ships rating changes and no levels, so the levels here are a construction, stated and seeded, and the returns do not respond to them. The shipped changes are mostly upgrades, so a fixed floor expires as the scale drifts and costs nothing once it is slack. The screen nevertheless costs return out of sample under every rater, because the changes cluster by sector and the sectors upgraded most earned least: a constraint on an unpriced characteristic costs what the priced characteristics it is correlated with cost.
  • A rating is an estimate (Berg et al. 2022; Avramov et al. 2022): at the correlation between real raters, roughly half of an exclusion list is the rater’s choice. Price the constraint, not the names.
