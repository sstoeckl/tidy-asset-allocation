21  The Equity Sleeve

An equity sleeve arrives with two things fixed from above: a budget, and a benchmark it will be measured against. Which stocks should fill it, and is whatever the selection earns still visible once the sleeve is one line of the allocation that set the budget? Fama and French (1992) and Fama and French (1993) showed that the cross-section of stock returns lines up with a few observable characteristics, which is what makes selection by sorting a method rather than a habit, and Treynor and Black (1973) showed how a portfolio selected for its alpha is to be combined with the passive portfolio it departs from, so the two questions have been one question since. This is the first chapter of the book to work inside a class rather than across classes: the granularity axis of Section 3.1 made concrete, on the 123 equities behind the five equity series that Parts III to V allocated as single numbers.

We first set out the sleeve’s benchmark and mandate, the arithmetic that carries its active return into the total portfolio, the sorts this market can run, and the estimation problem that so many names create; then we rebuild the benchmark from its instruments, measure equal against capitalization weighting, sort on size and momentum, race a factor covariance against shrinkage, and build a minimum-variance sleeve under a tracking-error budget, directly and as a sub-portfolio; then we fill the equity budget of the policy of Section 15.3 three ways and ask what the total portfolio noticed. On the horizon axis a permanent tilt toward a characteristic is strategic and a signal re-read every month, such as momentum, is tactical. The sleeve built here is strategic: it conditions on nothing but a rolling covariance estimate and the benchmark’s weights.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)   # 16 risky classes

A  <- as_xts_pm(sim_asset_returns_monthly[sim_asset_returns_monthly$asset %in% eq$asset_id, ],
                date, asset, return)[, eq$asset_id]

21.1 Selection Inside a Budget

21.1.1 The benchmark, the mandate, and the arithmetic of a sleeve

The sleeve’s benchmark is the class series it sits inside: the capitalization-weighted regional equity index of Section 2.2, which Section 15.1 handed down as the policy’s investable proxy. Write \(\boldsymbol{b}\) for the benchmark’s weights over the \(N\) names of the region, \(\boldsymbol{v}\) for the sleeve’s, and \(\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{v} - \boldsymbol{b}\) for the active weights of Equation 24.1. The mandate is the one Section 24.1 wrote down: as much active return as a tracking-error budget \(\tau\) buys, \(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{x}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{x}} \leq \tau\), scored by the information ratio of Equation 24.3.

The sleeve holds a share \(\omega\) of the total portfolio and the policy holds the same share in the benchmark. With every other line of the policy held as it is,

\[ r^{P}_t - r^{B}_t = \omega\,(r^{S}_t - r^{b}_t), \qquad \alpha_P = \omega\,\alpha_S, \qquad \text{TE}_P = \omega\,\text{TE}_S, \qquad \text{IR}_P = \text{IR}_S , \tag{21.1}\]

where \(r^{S}\) and \(r^{b}\) are the sleeve’s return and its benchmark’s. The budget scales active return and tracking error alike and leaves the information ratio alone: selection skill is not diluted by the allocation above it, only its weight in the outcome is. How much the total portfolio’s Sharpe ratio can gain is the result of Treynor and Black (1973). With the sleeve’s excess return regressed on the benchmark’s, \(\alpha\) the intercept and \(\sigma_\varepsilon\) the residual volatility, the optimal combination of active sleeve and passive portfolio reaches

\[ \text{SR}^2_{P} = \text{SR}^2_{B} + \left(\frac{\alpha}{\sigma_\varepsilon}\right)^2 , \tag{21.2}\]

the second term being the appraisal ratio of Section 24.1. An appraisal ratio of one quarter added to a policy Sharpe ratio of three quarters gives \(\sqrt{0.75^2 + 0.25^2} = 0.791\), a ceiling reached only if \(\omega\) is chosen for it rather than set by the policy. The application checks both: whether the sleeve earned an information ratio at all, and how much of it a fixed budget let through.

21.1.2 Selection by characteristic

A sort ranks the names on a characteristic known at the start of the month and holds the favored group. Size was the first (Banz 1981; Fama and French 1992), momentum the most durable (Jegadeesh and Titman 1993), value the one that completes the standard set (Fama and French 1993; Asness et al. 2013). Building the factors as tradable portfolios is the subject of Scheuch et al. (2023) and is not reproduced here. Size and momentum need capitalizations and past returns, which ship; value needs a fundamental, and the modeled market carries none, so no value sort can be built on it, and Asness et al. (2013) is the account of what it would add, a premium negatively correlated with momentum in every market they study. Fama and French (2012) find the premia local rather than global, and Section 5.2 found why on this market: a global size sort in a five-region universe sorts on region. Every sort below is therefore run within a region and averaged across the five, as Griffin (2002) recommends; Section 18.2 estimates the same two characteristics as a parametric policy with standard errors.

What a sleeve buys with many names is breadth. The fundamental law of Grinold (1989), Equation 24.4, puts the attainable information ratio at \(\text{IC}\sqrt{\text{BR}}\cdot\text{TC}\): a monthly sort over one region’s names takes several hundred independent decisions a year where the sixteen-class allocator of Section 24.1 takes sixteen, so the same information ratio needs a far smaller information coefficient (Grinold and Kahn 2000), less what the long-only constraint takes back (Clarke et al. 2002).

21.1.3 Equal weighting is an active bet

The simplest alternative to the benchmark holds every name at \(1/N\). Its active weights are \(1/N - b_i\), long every name smaller than average and short every larger one, rebalanced against drift every month: a size tilt and a contrarian rule at once, and Plyakha et al. (2021) find both parts matter. DeMiguel et al. (2009) is the reason the rule is taken seriously; Section 24.3 showed that as a benchmark it embeds a permanent bet against the largest market. Inside a sleeve it is a bet against the largest names, and the question is whether it pays.

21.1.4 The covariance matrix of a sleeve

Everything the sleeve optimizes needs \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) over its names, and Equation 7.2 settles what the sample estimate can deliver: with \(N = 123\), any window shorter than 124 months gives a singular matrix, and Section 7.1 showed that a minimizer handed one finds the portfolios it believes are riskless and holds them. A sleeve over the full universe therefore needs one of the repairs of Section 7.2 before it can be optimized at all. Shrinkage (Ledoit and Wolf 2003, 2004) pulls the sample matrix toward a well-conditioned target; the alternative is to assert a structure. With \(k\) statistical factors extracted from the returns themselves,

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_k = \boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}' + \boldsymbol{D}, \tag{21.3}\]

where \(\boldsymbol{B}\) is \(N \times k\), the first \(k\) principal components scaled by their volatilities, and \(\boldsymbol{D}\) the diagonal matrix of residual variances: \(N(k+1)\) parameters in place of \(N(N+1)/2\), and full rank whatever \(T\) is because \(\boldsymbol{D}\) is positive. Connor and Korajczyk (1993) justify principal components as the factors and give a test for \(k\); Bai and Ng (2002) give the criteria for choosing \(k\) that are now standard. Which repair is better is settled out of sample, as Section 7.3 settled it.

Within one region, with \(N\) between 17 and 37, the matrix is estimable but a ten-year window gives about three observations per parameter, and shrinkage still keeps the weights from reading noise. The practical form of a concentrated sleeve is a cardinality limit, at most \(K\) names, which Section 12.2 showed to be a mixed-integer program (Bienstock 1996) that the package honors only on its linear paths; the convex substitute is a cap per name, itself a shrinkage of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) by Jagannathan and Ma (2003). The tracking-error budget has a closed-form reading for a minimum-variance sleeve: the Lagrangian of variance subject to \(\boldsymbol{x}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{x} \leq \tau^2\) is, up to a constant and a positive scale, the one-parameter family

\[ \boldsymbol{v}(\theta) = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}\; \boldsymbol{v}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{v} - 2\theta\,\boldsymbol{b}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{v}, \qquad \theta = \frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda} \in [0, 1), \tag{21.4}\]

so that without further constraints \(\boldsymbol{v}(\theta) = \theta\boldsymbol{b} + (1-\theta)\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{mv}}\): the budget picks a blend of the benchmark and the minimum-variance portfolio. Under a box the blend is no longer linear, but Equation 21.4 is still one quadratic program per \(\theta\), and a root search finds the \(\theta\) that spends the budget exactly, which spares the rolling loop a cone solver several hundred times. Roll (1992) is the standing warning: the family does not ask whether the benchmark is efficient.

21.2 Implementation

21.2.1 The benchmark, rebuilt from its instruments

The class series are capitalization weighted on the previous week’s capitalization, weekly, and compounded to months, the one-period lag Section 5.2 imposed on its market proxy. Rebuilding them from the instruments is the check that the benchmark is one the sleeve could have held.

cap_w <- sim_marketcap_weekly[sim_marketcap_weekly$asset %in% eq$asset_id, ] |>
  tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = asset, values_from = market_cap) |>
  dplyr::arrange(date)
Aw <- as_xts_pm(sim_asset_returns_weekly[sim_asset_returns_weekly$asset %in% eq$asset_id, ],

stopifnot(all(month_of %in% seq_along(d)))

rebuilt <- sapply(regions, function(g) {

Capitalization weighting is itself concentrated: the United States index holds 35 names and behaves like 18.7 of them, the Swiss index holds 17 and behaves like 5.9.

Equal weighting earns more than its benchmark in every region, by 1.3 to 4.1 percentage points a year, at a tracking error of 3.9% to 7.3%, larger than most mandates allow. The information ratios run from 0.24 to 0.49; by Equation 24.10 their standard error over 372 months is about 0.18, so the smaller ones are not distinguishable from zero and the larger ones barely are. Whether the gap is a size premium or the rebalancing of Plyakha et al. (2021) is what the sorts separate.

21.2.3 Sorting on size and momentum

Size is the benchmark weight itself; momentum is the return over months \(t-12\) to \(t-2\), skipping the most recent month as Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) do. Both are sorted into terciles within each region and the five spreads are averaged. The long-short spread is the favored tercile minus the opposite one; the long-only spread, which a sleeve can hold, is the favored tercile minus the equally weighted region. Standard errors are those of Newey and West (1987) at six lags, the call Section 5.2 uses.

mom <- matrix(NA_real_, nrow(X), ncol(X), dimnames = dimnames(X))
for (t in 13:nrow(X)) mom[t, ] <- apply(1 + X[(t - 12):(t - 2), ], 2, prod) - 1

sort_spread <- function(signal, favored) {        # favored tercile: 1 lowest, 3 highest
  t(vapply(seq_len(nrow(X)), function(t) {
    if (anyNA(signal[t, ])) return(c(long_short = NA_real_, long_only = NA_real_))
    legs <- vapply(regions, function(g) {
      j <- names(region)[region == g]; q <- dplyr::ntile(signal[t, j], 3)
      c(fav = mean(X[t, j][q == favored]), opp = mean(X[t, j][q == 4 - favored]),
        all = mean(X[t, j]))
    }, numeric(3))
    m <- rowMeans(legs)                             # each region counts once
    c(long_short = m[["fav"]] - m[["opp"]], long_only = m[["fav"]] - m[["all"]])
  }, numeric(2)))
}
nw <- function(x) {
  x <- x[!is.na(x)]; m <- stats::lm(x ~ 1)
  c(mean = 12 * mean(x), t = unname(stats::coef(m)) /
      sqrt(sandwich::NeweyWest(m, lag = 6, prewhite = FALSE)[1, 1]), months = length(x))
}
size_s <- sort_spread(Cap, favored = 1)             # small minus big
mom_s  <- sort_spread(mom, favored = 3)             # winners minus losers
sorts  <- tibble::tibble(
  sort = rep(c("size, small minus big", "momentum, winners minus losers"), each = 2),
  leg  = rep(c("long-short", "long-only"), 2)) |>
  dplyr::bind_cols(dplyr::bind_rows(lapply(list(size_s[, 1], size_s[, 2], mom_s[, 1], mom_s[, 2]),
                                           function(x) as.list(nw(x)))))

Size is there and momentum is not. Small names earn 4.9 percentage points a year more than large ones within their region, at \(t = 3.7\), and the long-only version a sleeve could hold keeps 2.5 points of it at \(t = 3.1\); this is the gap of Table 21.2 seen from the characteristic’s side, and Section 18.2 recovers the same sign with a standard error on the coefficient. The momentum spread is +1.6 points at \(t = 0.96\). The absence is a property of the modeled data rather than of the strategy: each instrument’s idiosyncratic component is drawn independently period by period, as Section 2.2 describes, so a name’s own past return carries no information about its own future return, and cross-sectional momentum, which lives in exactly that autocorrelation, cannot exist here by construction. A real market has it (Jegadeesh and Titman 1993; Fama and French 2012); this one tests the machinery, and a reader who wants the premium should run the same code on the real-data sleeves of Section 2.3.

21.2.4 A covariance matrix for one hundred and twenty-three names

statistical.factor.model() fits Equation 21.3 by principal components and extractCovariance() assembles the matrix. On the full sample it runs; on the ten-year window it is needed for, it does not.

fm  <- lapply(1:3, function(k) statistical.factor.model(A, k = k))
S_k <- lapply(fm, extractCovariance)
share <- stats::prcomp(X)$sdev^2; share <- share / sum(share)      # variance by component

factor_covs <- function(X, ks = 1:3) {             # eq-21-factor-cov for several k at once
  pc <- stats::prcomp(X)
  lapply(ks, function(k) {
    B <- pc$rotation[, seq_len(k), drop = FALSE]; f <- X %*% B
    e <- X - f %*% t(B); e <- sweep(e, 2, colMeans(e))
    B %*% stats::cov(f) %*% t(B) + diag(colSums(e^2) / (nrow(X) - k - 1L))
  })
}
stopifnot(all(mapply(function(a, b) max(abs(a - b)) < 1e-12, factor_covs(X), S_k)))
refused <- tryCatch(statistical.factor.model(A[1:120, ], k = 3), error = conditionMessage)
c(first_component = share[1], next_two = sum(share[2:3]), refused = refused)
#>                  first_component                         next_two 
#>              "0.272666293574177"             "0.0797665088248939" 
#>                          refused 
#> "fewer observations than assets"
Importantstatistical.factor.model() refuses the case it exists for

The function stops whenever \(T < N\), which Section 7.2 recorded and which is exactly the window on which Equation 21.3 is needed: principal components need enough components, not enough columns. factor_covs() is the body of the function without the check, and the assertion above shows that it returns the package’s matrix wherever the package returns one.

The first component carries 27% of the variance and the next two 8% together. The race is the one R/ch05-estimator-race.R ran for Section 7.3: minimum variance, long only, on a rolling window of one hundred and twenty months, held one month, from the register’s first out-of-sample month. The sample matrix is singular on every window by Equation 7.2, and quadprog refuses it rather than inverting noise.

min_var <- function(S) {                           # long only, fully invested
  n <- ncol(S)
  sol <- try(quadprog::solve.QP(2 * S, rep(0, n), cbind(rep(1, n), diag(n)),
                                c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1), silent = TRUE)
  if (inherits(sol, "try-error")) return(NULL)
  w <- pmax(sol$solution, 0); w / sum(w)
}
shrunk <- function(X) {                            # corpcor's matrix, stripped to a plain one
  S <- corpcor::cov.shrink(X, verbose = FALSE)
  matrix(as.numeric(S), ncol(X), ncol(X), dimnames = list(colnames(X), colnames(X)))
}
N   <- ncol(X)
W   <- 120L
oos <- seq.int(W + 1L, nrow(X))                     # the register's out-of-sample months
stopifnot(oos[1] == 121L)
sample_refused <- is.null(min_var(stats::cov(X[seq_len(W), ])))

paths <- lapply(oos, function(t) {
  Xw <- X[(t - W):(t - 1L), ]
  c(list(shrunk = min_var(shrunk(Xw))),
    setNames(lapply(factor_covs(Xw), min_var), paste("factor, k =", 1:3)))
})
score <- function(r, rf_oos) {                      # the register's definitions
  n <- length(r)
  c(ann_return = prod(1 + r)^(12 / n) - 1, ann_vol = stats::sd(r) * sqrt(12),
    sharpe = (prod(1 + r)^(12 / n) - prod(1 + rf_oos)^(12 / n)) / (stats::sd(r) * sqrt(12)))
}
race <- dplyr::bind_rows(lapply(names(paths[[1]]), function(nm) {
  Wm <- t(vapply(paths, function(p) p[[nm]], numeric(N)))
  r  <- rowSums(Wm * X[oos, ])
  tibble::tibble(estimator = nm, !!!as.list(score(r, rf[oos])),
                 eff_names = mean(1 / rowSums(Wm^2)), held = mean(rowSums(Wm > 1e-6)))
}))
race <- dplyr::bind_rows(race, tibble::tibble(estimator = "equal weight",
                                              !!!as.list(score(rowMeans(X[oos, ]), rf[oos])),
                                              eff_names = N, held = N))

Scored on the months the register’s race covers, the shrinkage row reproduces Section 7.3’s to six decimals, which the hidden chunk asserts. The factor matrices give the lowest realized volatility, 11.1% for factor, k = 3 against 11.8% for shrinkage and 17.0% for equal weighting; the shrinkage matrix gives the highest Sharpe ratio, 0.82 against 0.67. Which repair is better depends on which number the mandate scores.

21.2.5 The selected sleeve

The sleeve is built for the United States as of the first out-of-sample month, with the shrinkage moments of Section 7.2 passed through momentFUN.

us   <- names(region)[region == "US"]
t1   <- oos[1]
A_us <- A[(t1 - W):(t1 - 1L), us]                   # the window before t1
S_us <- shrunk(zoo::coredata(A_us)); b_us <- bench_w(t1, us)
shrinkage_moments <- function(R, portfolio) {
  S <- shrunk(zoo::coredata(R))
  list(mu = colMeans(R), sigma = S, m3 = NULL, m4 = NULL, mean = colMeans(R), var = S)
}
base_us <- portfolio.spec(us) |>
  add.constraint("full_investment") |> add.constraint("long_only")

                                          optimize_method = "ROI", momentFUN = shrinkage_moments))
p_vol <- function(w, S = S_us) sqrt(12 * as.numeric(t(w) %*% S %*% w))
c(held = sum(w_mv > 1e-6), largest = max(w_mv), vol = p_vol(w_mv), benchmark_vol = p_vol(b_us))

#>       19.0000        0.1504        0.1145        0.1470

Unconstrained, minimum variance holds 19 of the 35 names and puts 15% in one of them. A mandate limits that, and the two ways of writing the limit behave differently in the package.

ImportantA position limit is honored on the ES path and dropped on the variance path 
K <- 10L
spec_K <- base_us |> add.constraint(type = "position_limit", max_pos = K)
w_K_var <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(A_us, spec_K |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var"),
                                             optimize_method = "ROI", momentFUN = shrinkage_moments))

#>            10            19            10

Under a variance objective ROI has no integer formulation, so the limit of 10 names is read and discarded: 19 names come back, identical to the unconstrained solution, as Section 12.2 measured on the sixteen classes. Under a minimum-ES objective the same constraint goes to Rglpk as a mixed-integer linear program and is honored. A concentrated minimum-variance sleeve has no exact solver in the package; Chang et al. (2000)’s screening heuristic is the practical route, and the convex substitute used below is a cap per name.

The cap is ten percent, or the benchmark’s own weight where that is larger, since the largest American name reaches 24% of its index and a flat ten percent would force an underweight of that size whatever the budget said. The budget is four percent a year, the figure Section 24.2 spent, and it enters through Equation 21.4, because the package cannot express it, for the reasons that chapter’s callout gives; the cone solver built there solves the same problem once, and the family solves it several hundred times.

te_of <- function(w, b, S) sqrt(12 * as.numeric(t(w - b) %*% S %*% (w - b)))
sleeve_family <- function(S, b, cap) {               # eq-21-family under a box
  n <- length(b); Sb <- as.numeric(S %*% b)
  Amat <- cbind(rep(1, n), diag(n), -diag(n)); bvec <- c(1, rep(0, n), -cap)
  function(theta) {
    w <- quadprog::solve.QP(2 * S, 2 * theta * Sb, Amat, bvec, meq = 1)$solution
    w <- pmax(w, 0); setNames(w / sum(w), names(b))
  }
}
select_sleeve <- function(S, b, tau, cap = pmax(0.10, b)) {
  f  <- sleeve_family(S, b, cap)
  w0 <- f(0)
  if (te_of(w0, b, S) <= tau) return(w0)             # the budget is slack
  theta <- stats::uniroot(function(th) te_of(f(th), b, S) - tau, c(0, 1), tol = 1e-9)$root
  f(theta)
}
tau   <- 0.04
w_sel <- select_sleeve(S_us, b_us, tau)
stopifnot(abs(te_of(w_sel, b_us, S_us) - tau) < 1e-6, all(w_sel <= pmax(0.10, b_us) + 1e-8))
c(held = sum(w_sel > 1e-6), largest = max(w_sel), tracking_error = te_of(w_sel, b_us, S_us),
  vol = p_vol(w_sel), active_share = sum(abs(w_sel - b_us)) / 2)

The sleeve holds 29 names, spends the budget exactly, and has an estimated volatility of 12.4% against the benchmark’s 14.7% and the unconstrained minimum’s 11.5%: the budget bought 71% of the distance between the two, which is Equation 21.4 read as a blend.

21.2.6 The sleeve as a sub-portfolio

mult.portfolio.spec() is the package’s structure for a sleeve the allocation layer can consume, and Section 15.2 established what it does and which arguments have to be set for it to run. Each regional sleeve becomes a sub-portfolio, minimum variance under the cap, re-estimated yearly on the trailing window; the top level allocates across the five proxies.

sleeve_spec <- function(a) portfolio.spec(a) |>
  add.constraint("full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "box", min = 0, max = 0.10) |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")
top <- portfolio.spec(regions) |>
  add.constraint("full_investment") |> add.constraint("long_only") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")

Three things carry over from Section 15.2: trailing_periods is the window less one, because the rolling solver counts the end point; the proxies in o_mp$R have 372 rows, the first 120 back-filled with a drifting equal-weight sleeve nobody chose; the weights come back as proxy.1 to proxy.5 in the order added. One is new: momentFUN passes through the dots of add.sub.portfolio(), so a sleeve can carry its own covariance estimator into the structure. It cannot carry the tracking-error budget, so the two stages of the application are run by hand.

21.3 Selection Under the Allocation Above It

The allocation above the sleeve is the policy of Section 15.3: a tangency portfolio inside the equity sleeve, minimum variance inside the bond sleeves, a tangency portfolio across the sleeves, all on the full sample. It is recomputed here so that the number it hands down is computed rather than quoted.

Re <- R - rf; S16 <- stats::cov(Re); mu16 <- colMeans(Re)
qp <- function(S, a) {       # min w'Sw s.t. a'w = 1, w >= 0, rescaled: a = 1 gives GMV, a = mu the tangency
  n <- ncol(S)
  w <- quadprog::solve.QP(2 * S, rep(0, n), cbind(a, diag(n)), c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)$solution
  w <- pmax(w, 0); setNames(w / sum(w), colnames(S))
}
kind <- sub(".*_", "", colnames(Re)); sl <- split(colnames(Re), kind)
v <- lapply(names(sl), function(k) {
  a <- sl[[k]]; if (length(a) == 1L) return(setNames(1, a))
  qp(S16[a, a], if (k == "equity") mu16[a] else rep(1, length(a)))
}); names(v) <- names(sl)

The equity budget is \(\omega = 29.1%\), split 70% United States and 30% Europe; the other three equity series receive nothing. We fill it three ways, holding every other line of the policy at its weight: with the benchmark itself, with the equally weighted sleeve, and with the selected sleeve, re-estimated monthly on the trailing 120 months in each region. Costs are the 10 basis points per unit traded of pm_costs_default(), charged on each sleeve’s monthly trades against its drifted weights as Equation 23.2 charges them; the benchmark pays for tracking its own capitalization weights, which on this market move by more than returns explain. Rebalancing the policy’s class weights costs the same under all three and is not charged.

rate <- pm_costs_default()[["equity"]]
path <- function(Wm, Xj, gross = rowSums(Wm * Xj)) {   # weights set at each month start
  drifted <- Wm * (1 + Xj) / (1 + rowSums(Wm * Xj))
  trade   <- rbind(0, abs(Wm[-1, , drop = FALSE] - drifted[-nrow(Wm), , drop = FALSE]))
  tibble::tibble(gross = gross, cost = rate * rowSums(trade), turnover = rowSums(trade) / 2)
}

Read the sleeve rows first. Equal weighting earns an information ratio of +0.26 against the benchmark over 252 months, with a standard error by Equation 24.10 of 0.22; the selected sleeve earns -0.05 with a standard error of 0.22. Neither is two standard errors from zero, which Section 24.3 said in advance about any ratio of this size at this length. What the selected sleeve did deliver is what it was built for: a volatility of 14.2% against the benchmark’s 16.5%, a realized tracking error of 3.6% against the four percent budgeted, and a Sharpe ratio of 0.72 against 0.61.

Now the total rows. With the benchmark in the sleeve the total portfolio is the policy, at a Sharpe ratio of 0.876; with the equally weighted sleeve it reaches 0.934, with the selected sleeve 0.951, and the net rows sit within a hundredth of the gross ones because the sleeves turn over 43% and 34% a year at ten basis points, against the 35% the benchmark itself needs to track its weights. The two sleeves get there by different routes. Equal weighting adds return, 6.36% against 6.01%, at the policy’s volatility; the selected sleeve adds nothing to return and takes the total’s volatility from 5.47% to 5.00%, because \(\omega\) times a sleeve 2.3 points less volatile than its benchmark is a policy about half a point less volatile. Its departures reach the policy at a tracking error of 1.1%, against 1.3% for the equally weighted sleeve.

Against sampling error, none of this is established. The two totals are correlated with the policy at 0.973 or more, and the test for a difference between two Sharpe ratios of Jobson and Korkie (1981) with the correction of Memmel (2003) uses that correlation; even so it gives \(z = 1.07\) for the equally weighted total and \(z = 1.75\) for the selected one, nearer the conventional threshold than the information ratios but on the same side of it. Selection survived the allocation in the sense Equation 21.1 allows: the information ratio is intact, active return and tracking error arrive scaled by \(\omega\), and what the total portfolio registers most clearly is the sleeve’s variance rather than its alpha, which 252 months cannot distinguish from zero. The policy’s largest drawdown bottoms in September of model year 31, inside the rates shock, and the maximum drawdowns of the three totals lie within 1.7 percentage points of one another.

Figure 21.1 is the chapter in one picture: two panels of the same shape and different scales, because the total’s active return is the sleeve’s times \(\omega\) month by month. The sleeve manager’s record is the upper panel; the investor who owns the policy lives in the lower one, where the breadth Equation 24.4 promised has been divided by the budget Section 15.3 set. That is the arithmetic a sleeve is hired under, and Section 22.1 meets it with a duration in place of a characteristic. Whether a sleeve’s information ratio is real is a question for Equation 24.10 and the register of Section 25.3; what reaches the total is a question for \(\omega\), and Section 23.3 prices the trading it costs.

Key Takeaways

  • A sleeve’s benchmark is the class series it sits inside, which reproduces from the instruments to machine precision with a one-week lag on the capitalization weights, and the sleeve is judged against it under a tracking-error budget (Grinold and Kahn 2000; Roll 1992).
  • Inside an allocation a sleeve’s active return and tracking error scale by its budget \(\omega\) and its information ratio does not, by Equation 21.1; Equation 21.2 bounds what the total Sharpe ratio can gain (Treynor and Black 1973).
  • On this market the size sort is present and momentum is absent by construction, because each name’s idiosyncratic component is drawn independently; value cannot be built for want of fundamentals. Equal weighting is a size tilt and a contrarian rule at once (Plyakha et al. 2021).
  • One hundred and twenty-three names make the sample covariance singular on any window shorter than one hundred and twenty-four months; the sleeve needs shrinkage or the factor structure of Equation 21.3. Out of sample the factor matrices give the lowest volatility and the shrinkage matrix the highest Sharpe ratio (Ledoit and Wolf 2004; Bai and Ng 2002).
  • A cardinality limit is dropped silently on the variance path and honored on the ES path; a cap per name is the convex substitute. The tracking-error budget is the one-parameter family Equation 21.4, and a sub-portfolio in mult.portfolio.spec() can carry its own covariance estimator but not the budget.
