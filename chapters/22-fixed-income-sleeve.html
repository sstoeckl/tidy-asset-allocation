22  The Fixed Income Sleeve

Draft|Code: script · exercises · solutions · all chapters|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

What changes in portfolio construction when the asset has a duration? Litterman and Scheinkman (1991) answered that three common factors, the level, the slope and the curvature of the yield curve, explain nearly all of the variation in government bond returns, so that a bond portfolio is described better by its exposures to those factors than by the list of bonds it holds. The curve itself has the three-parameter form of Nelson and Siegel (1987), which Diebold and Li (2006) turned into a dynamic model whose factors carry the same three names. This chapter is about the fixed income sleeve, the bond part of the allocation one level below the class series on which Section 15.1 allocates, and its claim is that duration targeting and portfolio optimization are the same problem in different clothes.

We first derive why a bond is a set of exposures rather than a return series, and what credit and currency add; then measure the exposures of the government and credit instruments of the modeled market on the curve factors that ship with it, build a sleeve whose duration is a constraint, and fit a duration overlay as a parametric policy; then run the sleeves out of sample against the class series the allocation sees. On the horizon axis of Section 3.1 the duration decision is strategic, a level of exposure held for the long run, and the positioning along the curve that the overlay varies with the term spread is tactical.

The data are the weekly curve factors of sim_curve_weekly and the bond instruments of sim_asset_returns_weekly, both described in Section 2.2 and Appendix A; monthly instrument and class series enter in the application. Everything is in dollars, unhedged, and the hedged return of a foreign bond is its unhedged return less the matching fx_return, Equation 2.4.

bonds <- dplyr::filter(sim_assets, asset_class %in% c("government", "credit"))

The curve table has one week more than the return table: it starts a week earlier, so that a change in the curve exists for the first return.

22.1 Bonds as Exposures to the Curve

22.1.1 Price, duration and the return decomposition

A zero-coupon bond paying one unit at maturity \(\tau\) has, at a continuously compounded yield \(y_t(\tau)\), the price

\[ P_t(\tau) = e^{-\tau\, y_t(\tau)} . \tag{22.1}\]

Hold it for a period \(\Delta\). It ages to maturity \(\tau-\Delta\) and is priced at the new yield for that maturity, so the log return is exactly

\[ \log\frac{P_{t+\Delta}(\tau-\Delta)}{P_t(\tau)} = \underbrace{\Delta\, y_t(\tau)}_{\text{carry}} + \underbrace{(\tau-\Delta)\big[y_t(\tau) - y_t(\tau-\Delta)\big]}_{\text{rolldown}} - \underbrace{(\tau-\Delta)\big[y_{t+\Delta}(\tau-\Delta) - y_t(\tau-\Delta)\big]}_{\text{duration}\times\text{yield change}} , \tag{22.2}\]

an identity obtained by adding and subtracting \((\tau-\Delta)\,y_t(\tau-\Delta)\). The first term is the yield earned by standing still, the second what the bond gains by sliding down an upward-sloping curve that does not move, and the third the loss from a rise in the yield at the bond’s new maturity, scaled by that maturity. Ilmanen (2011) treats the first two as the expected return of a bond on an unchanged curve, and Koijen et al. (2018) show that the same carry logic prices assets well beyond bonds. The scale factor on the yield change is the duration of Macaulay (1938), the maturity for a zero-coupon bond; the simple return \(e^{r}-1\) adds the second-order convexity term \(\tfrac{1}{2}(\tau-\Delta)^2(\Delta y)^2\), positive whichever way yields move. In the modeled market every government bond is a zero-coupon bond held at a constant maturity of two, five or ten years (Section A.2), so its duration is known rather than estimated, a gift for teaching and a limitation for realism.

The third term of Equation 22.2 is why a bond portfolio is a set of exposures. Suppose the yield changes at all maturities are driven by \(K\) factors, \(\Delta y(\tau) = \sum_k b_k(\tau)\,\Delta f_k\). A portfolio with weights \(w_i\) in bonds of maturities \(\tau_i\) then earns, up to carry and rolldown,

\[ r_p \approx -\sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_k\, \Delta f_k, \qquad \beta_k = \sum_{i} w_i\, \tau_i\, b_k(\tau_i), \tag{22.3}\]

and the weights matter only through the \(K\) numbers \(\beta_k\). Two portfolios with the same exposures have the same risk, whatever bonds they hold. With \(K=3\) this is the finding of Litterman and Scheinkman (1991), and it is the reason the sleeve below is specified as a set of exposures rather than as a set of weights.

22.1.2 Three factors of the curve

Nelson and Siegel (1987) write the whole curve at a date as

\[ y_t(\tau) = L_t + S_t\,\frac{1-e^{-\lambda\tau}}{\lambda\tau} + C_t\left(\frac{1-e^{-\lambda\tau}}{\lambda\tau} - e^{-\lambda\tau}\right), \tag{22.4}\]

with loadings that Diebold and Li (2006) read as level, slope and curvature: the loading on \(L_t\) is one at every maturity, the loading on \(S_t\) falls from one at the short end to zero at the long end, so that \(S_t\) is the short rate minus the long rate, and the loading on \(C_t\) is a hump that peaks in the middle. Diebold and Li (2006) fix the decay \(\lambda\) at \(0.0609\) per month, which puts the hump at thirty months, estimate the factors by least squares at each date, and show that they track three observable quantities almost perfectly: the long yield, the short minus the long yield, and twice a middle yield minus the two ends. Litterman and Scheinkman (1991) reached the same three factors without a functional form, as the first three principal components of yield changes, the first explaining about ninety percent of the variance. With three maturities and three factors, as here, any three-factor description is an exact rotation of the three yields; which rotation the data ship, Section 22.2 measures. The rank deficiency of the bond block in Section 7.1 is the same fact seen from the covariance matrix: five curves of three factors span the fifteen government bonds, and one credit factor per region spans the credit bonds with them.

22.1.3 Credit as a separate exposure

A corporate bond of maturity \(\tau\) yields the government rate plus a spread \(s_t(\tau)\), so its price is \(e^{-\tau(y_t + s_t)}\) and Equation 22.2 acquires a fourth term, \(-(\tau-\Delta)\,\Delta s\): the spread duration, which for a zero-coupon bond equals its duration. Merton (1974) explains the spread as the value of the default option the bondholder has written; Elton et al. (2001) show that the expected loss from default explains only a small part of it, with taxes and a premium for systematic risk making up the rest; and Collin-Dufresne et al. (2001) find that spread changes across issuers are driven mostly by one common factor, which is also how this market is built: the two grades of a region share one credit factor. Two features matter for the sleeve. The spread is persistent: the weekly first-order autocorrelation of the US investment-grade spread is 0.990, a half-life of 67 weeks, so a spread that widens in a crisis stays wide for as long as the credit cycle does. And the volatility of a spread is roughly proportional to its level, which is why Ben Dor et al. (2007) measure credit exposure as duration times spread rather than as duration alone: in this market the weekly changes of the US high-yield spread have 3.4 times the standard deviation of the investment-grade changes.

22.1.4 Duration targeting as a constrained optimization

The classical use of duration is immunization: Fisher and Weil (1971) showed that a bond portfolio whose duration equals the investor’s horizon is protected, to first order, against a parallel shift of the curve. A duration target is therefore a linear constraint on the weights, \(\sum_i w_i D_i = \bar D\), and minimizing variance subject to it is still a quadratic program of the kind Section 12.1 and Section 13.1 solve. In PortfolioAnalytics such a constraint is a factor_exposure constraint with the durations as the exposure vector \(\boldsymbol{B}\): the package has no notion of a bond, and does not need one, because by Equation 22.3 a duration target is a bound on the portfolio’s loading on the level factor, and a second column bounds the loading on the slope, which decides whether the duration is delivered by a bullet at the middle of the curve or a barbell of its ends. The level \(\bar D\) is strategic: for a long-horizon investor Campbell and Viceira (2002) argue that the long bond, not the bill, is the riskless asset, and how much duration the policy portfolio carries is a decision of that kind. The deviation from \(\bar D\) conditioned on the curve is tactical, and Section 22.2 writes it as the parametric policy of Section 18.1.

22.1.5 Currency, and why the two decisions are not separable abroad

Every series is in dollars, so a foreign bond carries its local return and its currency’s move together, and Section 4.3 showed that how much of a foreign holding is currency depends on the asset. For a bond it is most of it:

fx_share <- function(series, region) {
  f <- sim_fx_monthly[sim_fx_monthly$region == region, ]
  stopifnot(all(f$date == dates))
  u <- as.numeric(R[, series])
  1 - stats::var(u - f$fx_return) / stats::var(u)          # @eq-04-fx: share removed by hedging
}
round(c(JP_government = fx_share("JP_government", "JP"),
        EU_government = fx_share("EU_government", "EU")), 3)
#> JP_government EU_government 
#>         0.916         0.842

Hedging removes 92% of the variance of JP_government: a dollar investor who buys a Japanese bond for its duration takes a currency position several times the size of the interest-rate position. That is the case for fully hedging bonds that Campbell et al. (2010) make and the “free lunch” of Perold and Schulman (1988); Black (1989) argues for a universal partial hedge, and Karnosky and Singer (1994) attribute the result to the two decisions separately. For the sleeve the consequence is that the duration and currency decisions cannot be taken one at a time: an unhedged foreign bond is a currency position with a small duration attached, and Section 22.2 measures what that does to the fit of the curve factors.

22.1.6 Predictability from the curve

The tactical case rests on the finding that the shape of the curve forecasts bond returns. Fama and Bliss (1987) showed that forward spreads forecast the excess return of long bonds over short ones; Campbell and Shiller (1991) showed that the yield spread forecasts long-bond returns in the direction opposite to the expectations hypothesis; Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005) found that a single combination of forward rates forecasts the excess returns on bonds of every maturity with \(R^2\) up to about forty percent; and Fama and French (1989) placed the term spread among the business-cycle predictors of both stock and bond returns. Section 16.3 tested the term spread on this market and found it the one signal whose out-of-sample \(R^2\) survives, which is the signal the duration overlay below conditions on.

22.2 Measuring and Targeting the Exposures

22.2.2 Factor exposures, and which bond is which

The exposures of Equation 22.3 are regression coefficients: each instrument’s weekly return on the weekly changes of its region’s level, slope and curvature, and for credit also of its grade’s spread. Each regression is run twice, on the return as shipped and on the hedged return, which subtracts the region’s fx_return.

dcv <- as.data.frame(lapply(cv[-1], diff))                 # factor changes, aligned with the returns
exposure <- function(id) {
  rg <- bonds$region[bonds$asset_id == id]; gr <- bonds$credit_grade[bonds$asset_id == id]
  X  <- data.frame(level = dcv[[paste0(rg, "_Level")]], slope = dcv[[paste0(rg, "_Slope")]],
                   curvature = dcv[[paste0(rg, "_Curvature")]],
                   spread = if (is.na(gr)) 0 else dcv[[paste0(rg, "_", gr, "_Spread")]])
  f   <- if (rg == "US") 0 else fx[[rg]]
  fml <- if (is.na(gr)) y ~ level + slope + curvature else y ~ level + slope + curvature + spread
  mh  <- stats::lm(fml, data = cbind(y = as.numeric(A[, id]) - f, X))   # hedged
  mu  <- stats::lm(fml, data = cbind(y = as.numeric(A[, id]),     X))   # unhedged, as shipped
  b   <- stats::coef(mh)
  tibble::tibble(asset = id, region = rg, class = bonds$asset_class[bonds$asset_id == id],
                 grade = ifelse(is.na(gr), "", gr),
                 level = b[["level"]], slope = b[["slope"]], curvature = b[["curvature"]],
                 spread = if (is.na(gr)) NA_real_ else b[["spread"]],
                 r2_hedged = summary(mh)$r.squared, r2_unhedged = summary(mu)$r.squared)
}
expo <- dplyr::bind_rows(lapply(colnames(A), exposure))

sim_assets carries no maturity, so which instrument is the two-, five- or ten-year has to be inferred, and Equation 22.2 says how: the loading on the level is minus the duration, and the ten-year is the most rate-sensitive bond of its region and grade. The inference is asserted rather than assumed, by requiring every implied duration to lie within ten percent of the maturity it has been assigned.

expo <- expo |>
  dplyr::group_by(region, class, grade) |>
  dplyr::mutate(maturity = c(10, 5, 2)[rank(level)]) |>      # most negative loading: the ten-year
  dplyr::ungroup()
stopifnot(all(abs(-expo$level / expo$maturity - 1) < 0.10),
          all(table(expo$region, expo$maturity) == 3L))      # three bonds per maturity and region
mat <- setNames(expo$maturity, expo$asset)
id_of <- function(rg, tau, cls = "government", gr = "")
  expo$asset[expo$region == rg & expo$class == cls & expo$grade == gr & expo$maturity == tau]

The level loadings of the government bonds in Table 22.1 are -1.92, -4.80, -9.58, minus the maturity divided by one plus the yield: the modified duration under annual compounding, so the bonds are repriced in full rather than through the first-order term of Equation 22.2. The credit bonds have the same durations and a spread duration of the same size, as the theory says, and every \(R^2\) is 0.970 or better: a US bond is spanned by the factors up to the time variation of its carry, the rank deficiency of Section 7.1 seen one instrument at a time.

The slope and curvature columns reveal which rotation of the three yields the factors are: the ten-year loads on the level alone, the two-year equally on level and slope, the five-year on the level and half of the slope and half of the curvature.

So the shipped factors are the observable level, slope and curvature that Diebold and Li (2006) use to interpret their latent ones: the ten-year yield, the two-year minus the ten-year, and twice the five-year minus the two- and ten-year. The latent loadings of Equation 22.4 at the Diebold–Li decay, three lines of code, are a different rotation of the same three yields, and the exposures of Equation 22.3 transform with the rotation while the risk they describe does not.

lam <- 0.0609 * 12                                            # Diebold-Li decay, per year

}

NoteWorked: the week the ten-year moved most

Equation 22.2 is checked on the week in which the US ten-year yield moved most, with \(y^{c} = \log(1+y)\) the continuously compounded yield.

yc  <- log(1 + Y_us[, "y10"])                                  # continuously compounded
i   <- which.max(abs(diff(yc)))                                # the week of the largest move
dy  <- diff(yc)[i]; y0 <- yc[i]; tau <- 10; d <- 1 / 52
r_actual <- as.numeric(A[i, id_of("US", 10)])
carry    <- d * y0
duration <- -(tau - d) * dy
convexity <- 0.5 * (tau - d)^2 * dy^2
round(c(yield_change_bp = 1e4 * dy, carry = carry, duration = duration,
        convexity = convexity, first_order = carry + duration,
        second_order = carry + duration + convexity, actual = r_actual), 5)
#> yield_change_bp           carry        duration       convexity     first_order 
#>        53.15923         0.00060        -0.05306         0.00141        -0.05245 
#>    second_order          actual 
#>        -0.05105        -0.05084

In May of model year 18 the ten-year yield rose by 53 basis points. Substituting into Equation 22.2,

\[ r \approx \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{52}\times 0.0314}_{\text{carry}} \;-\; \underbrace{9.981 \times 0.00532}_{\text{duration}} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\times 99.62 \times 0.00532^{\,2}}_{\text{convexity}} = -0.0510, \]

against an actual return of -0.0508. The duration term is the whole of the move, the convexity gives back 14 basis points of the loss, and the 2 basis points that remain are that week’s rolldown and the error of the expansion.

22.2.3 What the currency does to the fit

Outside the US the same regressions tell two stories.

Hedged, the foreign government bonds are explained by their own curves as well as the US bonds are, a mean \(R^2\) of 0.959 across the four regions, the lowest JP_GOV_01 at 0.696. Unhedged, the mean is 0.144: the same local return sits inside a currency move that explains most of the variance, the share computed in Section 22.1, so a duration target set on an unhedged foreign sleeve controls a minority of its risk.

22.2.4 The sleeve as a set of exposures

The sleeve is the fifteen government bonds, weekly and unhedged, and the duration is a factor_exposure constraint whose \(\boldsymbol{B}\) holds the duration of each bond from the level loadings above. The matrix is bound to the asset names and the binding asserted, for the reason the callout below gives; every bound is widened by a millionth of a year, for the reason the callout in Section 22.3 gives.

gov <- expo$asset[expo$class == "government"]
G   <- A[, gov]
B   <- cbind(duration = -expo$level, slope = -expo$slope)[match(gov, expo$asset), ]
rownames(B) <- gov
stopifnot(identical(rownames(B), colnames(G)))
sleeve <- function(assets, B, lower, upper = lower, band = 1e-6) {
  stopifnot(identical(if (is.matrix(B)) rownames(B) else names(B), assets))
  portfolio.spec(assets) |>
    add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
    add.constraint(type = "long_only") |>
    add.constraint(type = "factor_exposure", B = B,            # band: the application says why
                   lower = lower - band, upper = upper + band) |>
    add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")
}
exposures <- function(w) setNames(as.numeric(t(w[rownames(B)]) %*% B), colnames(B))
ann_vol   <- function(w) sqrt(as.numeric(t(w) %*% stats::cov(G) %*% w) * 52)

w_free <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(G, sleeve(gov, B[, "duration"], 0, 20),
                                            optimize_method = "ROI"))
w_five <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(G, sleeve(gov, B[, "duration"], 5),
                                            optimize_method = "ROI"))
round(w_free[w_free > 0.005], 3)
#> UK_GOV_01 US_GOV_01 
#>     0.005     0.995
round(w_five[w_five > 0.005], 3)
#> CH_GOV_03 EU_GOV_03 JP_GOV_03 UK_GOV_03 US_GOV_01 US_GOV_03 
#>     0.008     0.018     0.106     0.057     0.600     0.212
rbind(unconstrained = c(exposures(w_free), vol = ann_vol(w_free)),
      duration_5    = c(exposures(w_five), vol = ann_vol(w_five))) |> round(3)
#>               duration slope   vol
#> unconstrained    1.922 1.919 0.016
#> duration_5       5.000 1.133 0.038

Left to itself, minimum variance holds the US two-year and almost nothing else, at a duration of 1.92: the least volatile bond is the shortest one in the home currency, and the argument of Section 9.3 applies unchanged. Told to carry a duration of five, the optimizer delivers it as a barbell: the US two-year and ten-year carry 81% of the portfolio between them, with 19% in foreign ten-year bonds whose currency moves diversify the dollar curve, and the five-year is not held at all. The duration is met exactly, and the slope exposure that comes with it is 1.13.

The constructor checks that B has as many rows as there are assets and nothing else: row names are stored, never compared with the asset names. A B whose rows are in a different order than colnames(R) is accepted and the constraint is applied to the wrong bonds.

22.2.5 The duration overlay as a parametric policy

The tactical layer is written as the policy of Equation 18.1 with one characteristic. Let \(z_t\) be the US term spread, the ten-year less the two-year yield, standardized, and let the long end of the sleeve be the US ten-year and the short end the US two-year. The weight on the long end is

\[ w_t = \bar w + \theta\, z_t, \qquad \hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t} u\!\big(w_t\, r^{10}_{t+1} + (1-w_t)\, r^{2}_{t+1}\big), \tag{22.5}\]

with \(u\) the power utility of Equation 18.2 at \(\gamma = 5\). The anchor \(\bar w\) is the strategic decision, set so that the policy at \(z_t = 0\) has the duration of the equal-weighted two-, five- and ten-year ladder, which is the class series the allocation sees; \(\theta\) is the tactical one. The data are monthly, with the curve read at each month end from the last weekly observation of that month, as Section 16.2 does.

i_last <- findInterval(dates, cv$date)                      # last weekly curve point in each month
stopifnot(all(format(cv$date[i_last], "%Y-%m") == format(dates, "%Y-%m")))
term    <- -cv$US_Slope[i_last]                             # ten-year less two-year, decimal
r_long  <- as.numeric(Am[, id_of("US", 10)])
r_short <- as.numeric(Am[, id_of("US", 2)])
gamma   <- 5
D_bar   <- mean(c(2, 5, 10))                                # the ladder's duration, in years
w_bar   <- (D_bar - 2) / (10 - 2)                           # long-end weight that delivers it
crra <- function(r) (1 + r)^(1 - gamma) / (1 - gamma)
policy_utility <- function(theta, z, rl, rs) {
  w <- w_bar + theta * z
  mean(crra(w * rl + (1 - w) * rs))                         # @eq-22-overlay
}
z <- (term - mean(term)) / stats::sd(term)
# the signal at t sits beside the return of t + 1: z[-Tn] against r[-1]
fit_theta <- function(i) stats::optimize(policy_utility, c(-3, 3), z = z[-Tn][i],
                                         rl = r_long[-1][i], rs = r_short[-1][i],
                                         maximum = TRUE)$maximum
theta_hat <- fit_theta(seq_len(Tn - 1L))

set.seed(1602)
block_index <- function(n, block = 12L) {                   # the block bootstrap of chapter 9
  starts <- sample.int(n - block + 1L, ceiling(n / block), replace = TRUE)
  as.vector(outer(0:(block - 1L), starts, "+"))[seq_len(n)]
}
boot  <- vapply(seq_len(500L), function(b) fit_theta(block_index(Tn - 1L)), numeric(1))
ce    <- function(theta)                                    # monthly certainty equivalent
  ((1 - gamma) * policy_utility(theta, z[-Tn], r_long[-1], r_short[-1]))^(1 / (1 - gamma)) - 1
round(c(theta = theta_hat, se_block_bootstrap = stats::sd(boot), t = theta_hat / stats::sd(boot),
        ce_gain_annual = 12 * (ce(theta_hat) - ce(0))), 3)
#>              theta se_block_bootstrap                  t     ce_gain_annual 
#>              0.715              0.461              1.553              0.007

The fitted \(\hat\theta\) is 0.72: one standard deviation of term spread moves the long-end weight by 72 percentage points, or the duration by 5.7 years, in the direction Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005) predicts. Its block-bootstrap standard error (Künsch 1989) is 0.46, a \(t\)-statistic of 1.55, and the in-sample certainty-equivalent gain over the constant policy is 0.74 percentage points a year: the sign the literature predicts without the precision to be sure of it, the usual state of a tactical signal (Welch and Goyal 2008).

22.3 The Sleeve Out of Sample

The application runs from observation 121, the first out-of-sample month of the register of Section 25.3, with a sixty-month window and monthly rebalancing. One plain limitation first: the five government class series are each the equal-weighted ladder of the same three maturities, so they share one duration and a duration target cannot be set on them. The sleeves therefore run on the fifteen government instruments behind those classes and are judged against the class series. The equal-weighted sleeve holds the five government class series equally, the benchmark the allocation sees, with a duration of 5.67. The constant-duration sleeve is minimum variance on the fifteen instruments with the duration bound to that same 5.67, the durations being the maturities, which need no estimation. The duration overlay is the same optimization with the target moved by the policy of Equation 22.5, re-estimated every month on the data available then. And credit enters as a separate exposure: the five credit class series join the universe with a second column of \(\boldsymbol{B}\), the spread duration, bound so that the sleeve carries the duration and the spread duration of an equal-weighted portfolio of all ten bond classes, its benchmark.

tilt <- vapply(120:Tn, function(t) {                           # everything indexed <= t

The band argument of sleeve() is not cosmetic. With band = 0, the exposure equality reaches quadprog as two opposing inequalities, the same degeneracy that Section 9.2 documents for full_investment, and on these fifteen bonds 89 of the 253 rolling windows come back with the message seen above and weights of NA; the same windows solve once the bounds are a millionth of a year apart. pm_backtest() counts such periods in n_failed and carries the previous weights forward, and the assertion above requires the count to be zero, so that the table below describes the strategy that was specified.

Three things in Table 22.3 are worth stating. The constant-duration sleeve has the duration of the equal-weighted government sleeve and 4.3% volatility against 7.0%, because it holds 75% of its weight, on average, in US bonds and so carries little currency: the duration is the same and the risk is not, the point of Section 22.1 about foreign bonds made in a backtest. The overlay’s Sharpe ratio is 0.39 against 0.37 for the constant target, at 2.4 times the turnover; whether that difference is more than one sample is the question the in-sample \(t\)-statistic has answered. Credit as a second exposure raises the Sharpe ratio to 0.52, against 0.38 for the equal-weighted ten classes with the same two exposures, at the price of a skewness the table does not show and Section 10.1 would.

The rates shock is the episode in which duration is the whole story. Every bond portfolio lost: the equal-weighted government sleeve -15.3% over its 12 months, the constant-duration sleeve -11.6% with the same duration and less currency, and the overlay -5.3%. The overlay lost least because the curve had flattened before yields rose, so the policy had taken the target down to an average of 2.2 years through the episode, as Figure 22.2 shows. That is what a term-spread signal is meant to do and did; it is one episode, and the same figure shows the target at its upper bound through model years in which nothing followed.

The credit crisis is the opposite case, and the spreads say why.

In the credit crisis the ten-year yield fell by 97 basis points and the high-yield spread widened by 625: the same duration that earned the government ten-year +12.6% cost the high-yield ten-year -32.4% through its spread duration, and the sleeve with credit lost where the government sleeve gained. In the rates shock the level did the damage and the spreads added to it, so every bond lost together: the two exposures of the credit sleeve, in opposite combinations.

The class series the allocation of Section 15.1 sees, US_government and its four siblings, is the equal-weighted ladder, and that is the sleeve’s benchmark: the allocation decides how much of the portfolio is government bonds, and the sleeve’s job is a set of exposures relative to the ladder, a duration, a position on the curve, a spread duration, each written here as a bound on a factor loading. The package can nest the sleeve under the allocation itself, through mult.portfolio.spec() and add.sub.portfolio(), which optimize each sleeve on its own rebalancing calendar and allocate across the proxy return series; exercise 4 runs it and finds the proxies back-filled with equal weights before the first rebalance, the trap of Section 23.2. The same loadings are what a scenario needs: Appendix H shocks the level, the slope and the spreads directly and reads off, through Equation 22.3, what every sleeve and every class does. What the exposures cost to maintain is the subject of Section 23.1, and whether the overlay’s single sample is evidence is the subject of Section 25.1.

Key Takeaways

  • A bond’s return is carry plus rolldown minus duration times the yield change, Equation 22.2, so a bond portfolio is a set of exposures to the factors that move yields, and two portfolios with the same exposures have the same interest-rate risk whatever bonds they hold (Litterman and Scheinkman 1991).
  • Three factors describe the curve. The modeled market ships the observable level, slope and curvature of Diebold and Li (2006), a rotation of the two-, five- and ten-year yields; the principal components of yield changes recover the same three shapes.
  • A duration target is a factor_exposure constraint on the level loading, a second column decides between barbell and bullet, and minimum variance subject to both is still a quadratic program. B is bound by position, an exact target can be reported infeasible, and an infeasible one returns NA weights without a warning.
  • For a dollar investor a foreign government bond is mostly currency: hedged, the curve factors explain it as well as a US bond; unhedged, a fraction of it. The duration and currency decisions are taken together or not at all (Campbell et al. 2010).
  • The duration overlay is the parametric policy of chapter 18 with one characteristic: the sign the literature predicts, a \(t\)-statistic below two, and out of sample the smallest loss in the rates shock because the curve had flattened first, which is one episode.
