23  Rebalancing and Transaction Costs

Draft|Code: script · exercises · all chapters|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

Every portfolio in this book so far has been rebalanced back to its target weights each month at no charge. That assumption is not a small one. Perold (1988) named the gap between the paper portfolio and the real one the implementation shortfall, and the gap is worth more to some strategies than to others.

We first define turnover and the cost model, then set out what the theory says about how often to trade, then measure the effect on the out-of-sample runs of Section 25.1, which were computed with and without costs from the start for exactly this purpose. The finding is that rebalancing less often helps the rule-based portfolios at every estimation window, that what it does to the optimized ones changes sign with the window, and that the cost bite is monotone in how much a strategy optimizes.

sweep <- readr::read_csv("output/tables/oos-sweep.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
R <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)

23.1 Turnover, Costs, and When to Trade

23.1.1 Turnover

# Half the L1 distance between the weights you drifted into and the weights you
# want. Halved because selling one asset to buy another is one trade, counted
# once, not twice.
turnover <- function(w_before, w_after) sum(abs(w_after - w_before)) / 2

Formally, between the weights the portfolio has drifted into and the weights it is being moved to,

\[ \tau = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left| w_i^{\text{after}} - w_i^{\text{before}} \right| , \tag{23.1}\]

halved because selling one asset to buy another is a single trade. If each asset \(i\) costs \(c_i\) per unit traded one way, the portfolio return net of costs is

\[ r^{\text{net}}_t = r^{\text{gross}}_t - \sum_{i=1}^{N} c_i \left| w_{i,t}^{\text{after}} - w_{i,t}^{\text{before}} \right| . \tag{23.2}\]

PortfolioAnalytics exports a turnover() of its own, but it divides the same sum by the number of assets rather than by two, so on these sixteen series it reports an eighth of Equation 23.1; the function defined above is the one we use. Annualized by summing over a year, Equation 23.1 becomes the figure a trading desk recognizes: a hundred percent turnover means the portfolio was replaced once.

w0 <- rep(1 / ncol(R), ncol(R)); w1 <- replace(w0, 1, w0[1] + 0.1)
stopifnot(all.equal(PortfolioAnalytics::turnover(w1, w0) * ncol(R) / 2, turnover(w0, w1)))

Equation 23.2 is the simplest useful cost model and it is linear, which means it has no notion of trading a large position more expensively than a small one. That assumption is defensible at the asset-class scale of this chapter and fails at the scale of a single security, where market impact grows with size in the manner of Kyle (1985) and execution becomes its own optimization problem (Almgren and Chriss 2001). Amihud (2002) gives the cross-sectional evidence that illiquidity is priced, and Novy-Marx and Velikov (2016) show how many published anomalies do not survive their own trading costs.

23.1.2 Costs are not a single number

The rate in Equation 23.2 depends on what is being traded. A government bond is cheap, a high-yield credit position is not, and an emerging equity sits between. Using one average rate across a multi-asset portfolio charges the cheap sleeves too much and the dear ones too little, and the sleeves traded most are the ones misestimated most.

pm_costs_default()
#> money_market   government    commodity       equity       credit     .default 
#>       0.0001       0.0002       0.0008       0.0010       0.0025       0.0010

These are one-way rates in decimal: 2 basis points for a government bond against 25 for credit. They are arguments with defaults rather than constants, and Appendix D records where the numbers come from. The conclusions below survive varying them by a factor of two either way, and a chapter claiming otherwise would be concealing its own sensitivity.

Note which class is dearest. US_credit has the third-highest Sharpe ratio of the sixteen in Table 4.1, and credit is the most expensive thing in this universe to trade: a sleeve the optimizers of Section 9.1 have reason to want costs most to hold at a moving weight.

23.1.3 What theory says about how often to trade

With costs in the objective, the optimal policy is no longer to return to the target every period. Constantinides (1986) and Davis and Norman (1990) show that the solution takes the form of a no-trade region around the target: while the drifted weights stay inside it, the right action is to do nothing, and when they leave it the right action is to trade back only to its boundary rather than to the target itself. Trading is worth its cost only once the portfolio is far enough away.

Gârleanu and Pedersen (2013) give the modern version for a portfolio whose target is itself moving. Their optimal trade is a partial adjustment,

\[ \boldsymbol{w}_t = \boldsymbol{w}_{t-1} + \Delta\left(\boldsymbol{w}^{\text{aim}}_t - \boldsymbol{w}_{t-1}\right), \qquad \Delta \in (0, 1), \tag{23.3}\]

where the aim portfolio is a cost-weighted average of the current optimum and the optima expected in future, and the adjustment rate falls as costs rise. The practical content of Equation 23.3 is that a strategy whose target moves quickly should trade partially and often, while one whose target is stable should let it drift. That distinction is exactly what the measurements below recover.

23.2 Implementation

23.2.1 A turnover constraint is not a turnover constraint everywhere

PortfolioAnalytics will accept turnover_constraint() under both solver families, and they mean different things by it.

ImportantThe same constraint means two different things to two solvers

Under ROI a turnover constraint is hard: the solver will not return a portfolio trading more than the limit. Under CVXR it is implemented as a penalty added to the objective, so a sufficiently attractive portfolio buys its way past the limit and the limit bends. Neither behavior is wrong, and they answer different questions, but only one of them is what the word constraint normally means.

The same split applies to costs. transaction_cost_constraint() exists only under ROI. Requesting proportional costs under CVXR applies nothing, and reports nothing.

23.2.2 The accounting that rewrites the start of a backtest

PerformanceAnalytics::Return.portfolio() is the obvious way to turn a weight history into a return series. It has a behavior worth knowing before relying on it.

Rs <- R[1:24, ]

# Weights that begin deliberately late -- as they must, since the first ones
# cannot be computed until the estimation window has filled.
w <- xts::xts(matrix(0, 2, ncol(Rs), dimnames = list(NULL, colnames(Rs))),
              order.by = zoo::index(Rs)[c(12, 18)])
w[, "US_government"] <- 1

pr <- Return.portfolio(Rs, weights = w)

The call warns, and the warning is the whole story: “First weights period is after the start of the data series. Setting weights for the preceding periods to equal weight.”

tibble::tibble(
  month     = fmt_model(zoo::index(pr)),
  portfolio = as.numeric(pr),
  if_equal  = rowMeans(zoo::coredata(Rs)),
  if_stated = as.numeric(Rs[, "US_government"])
) |>
  dplyr::slice(c(1:3, 11:14))
#> # A tibble: 7 × 4
#>   month                    portfolio if_equal if_stated
#>   <chr>                        <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>
#> 1 January of model year 1   -0.00637 -0.00637  -0.0174 
#> 2 February of model year 1  -0.0146  -0.0147   -0.00676
#> 3 March of model year 1     -0.0205  -0.0205   -0.00373
#> 4 November of model year 1  -0.0149  -0.0154   -0.00529
#> 5 December of model year 1   0.00602  0.00719   0.0154 
#> 6 January of model year 2    0.0176   0.00936   0.0176 
#> 7 February of model year 2   0.0291   0.0151    0.0291

For the first 12 months, up to and including the month the first weights are dated, the series tracks the equally weighted portfolio rather than the one specified. In a rolling backtest the estimation window always consumes the start of the sample, so this is not an edge case. It is the normal case, and it silently prepends a strategy nobody chose. tidypm::pm_backtest() does the drift-and-rebalance accounting explicitly instead, in about twenty-five auditable lines.

23.2.3 What verbose = TRUE returns

Return.portfolio(verbose = TRUE) returns considerably more than a return series.

detail <- Return.portfolio(Rs, weights = w, verbose = TRUE)
names(detail)
#> [1] "returns"      "contribution" "BOP.Weight"   "EOP.Weight"   "BOP.Value"   
#> [6] "EOP.Value"

BOP.Weight and EOP.Weight are the weights at the beginning and end of each period, before and after drift. Their difference is Equation 23.1, without reconstructing it.

drift <- rowSums(abs(zoo::coredata(detail$BOP.Weight) -
                     zoo::coredata(detail$EOP.Weight))) / 2
round(utils::head(drift[drift > 0], 5), 4)
#> [1] 0.0117 0.0078 0.0093 0.0129 0.0039

contribution breaks the portfolio return down by asset for every period, which is the raw material for Section 24.1. Most users take $returns and never discover that the rest is there.

23.3 What Rebalancing Costs, Measured

23.3.1 What rebalancing less often does to rules and to optimizers

freq <- sweep |>
  dplyr::filter(window == 60, basis == "gross") |>
  dplyr::select(strategy, frequency, sharpe, ann_turnover) |>
  tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = frequency,
                     values_from = c(sharpe, ann_turnover))

# Annual against monthly at every window: the rules always gain, the optimizers
# move together, and they lose only in the window of the table.
rules      <- c("equal_weight", "inverse_vol")
optimizers <- c("min_variance", "max_sharpe", "min_cvar")
chg <- sweep |>
  dplyr::filter(basis == "gross", frequency %in% c("months", "years")) |>
  dplyr::select(strategy, window, frequency, sharpe) |>
  tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = frequency, values_from = sharpe) |>
  dplyr::mutate(change = years - months)
opt <- chg[chg$strategy %in% optimizers, ]
win_lose <- unique(opt$window[opt$change < 0])
win_gain <- unique(opt$window[opt$change > 0])
stopifnot(all(chg$change[chg$strategy %in% rules] > 0),
          !length(intersect(win_lose, win_gain)), identical(win_lose, 60))

The first three numeric columns are Sharpe ratios; the last three are annual turnover.

Equal weighting and inverse volatility improve when left alone, at every estimation window of the sweep. The second of those is the risk-based portfolio of Section 14.3, and low turnover is most of its case. In the table equal weighting runs from 0.626 to 0.654 with 42 percent of the turnover. The three optimized portfolios lose at this window: maximum Sharpe falls from 0.546 to 0.348 and minimum CVaR from 0.893 to 0.697. That loss belongs to this window, not to optimizers in general: at the 120- and 36-month windows all three gain from annual rebalancing, by up to 0.096. What is stable across windows is the size of the move, not its sign: the rules shift by about 0.03 whichever window they are given, the optimizers by up to 0.20 in either direction.

Equation 23.3 explains why the two groups behave differently. A rule-based weight is a target that does not move: letting it drift is a small, mean-reverting error, the aim portfolio and the current portfolio stay close, and trading to it more often mostly buys noise. An optimized weight is an estimate whose target moves as the sample it was fitted to recedes, so the aim portfolio runs away from the holdings and the adjustment has to be made repeatedly; held for a year, it is a bet on one estimate, which is why the sign of the effect changes with the window while its size does not. Section 4.2 measured that decay directly: the rolling mean wanders by more than its own level.

The optimizers are therefore caught between two costs. Rebalance often and pay to chase an unstable estimate; rebalance rarely and hold a stale one. This is the practical reason Davis and Norman (1990)’s no-trade region is wide for a volatile target and narrow for a stable one.

23.3.2 And the cost falls hardest on the strategies that can least afford it

cfg <- sweep |>
  dplyr::select(window, frequency, strategy, basis, sharpe, ann_turnover) |>
  tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = basis, values_from = sharpe) |>
  dplyr::mutate(bite = gross - net)
bite <- cfg |>
  dplyr::group_by(strategy) |>
  dplyr::summarise(gross = median(gross), net = median(net),
                   median_bite = median(bite), worst_bite = max(bite),
                   .groups = "drop") |>
  dplyr::arrange(dplyr::desc(median_bite))

# Two accessors the takeaways read from, so that no number there is typed.
sr_at   <- function(s, f) freq[[paste0("sharpe_", f)]][freq$strategy == s]
bite_of <- function(s) bite$median_bite[bite$strategy == s]
stopifnot(all(c("equal_weight", "max_sharpe") %in% bite$strategy))

# The medians are ordered by how much each rule estimates; in every
# configuration the two rules lose less than any optimizer.
per_config <- cfg |>
  dplyr::group_by(window, frequency) |>
  dplyr::summarise(rules_below = max(bite[strategy %in% rules]) < min(bite[strategy %in% optimizers]),
                   top = strategy[which.max(bite)], .groups = "drop")
stopifnot(identical(bite$strategy, c("max_sharpe", "min_cvar", "min_variance", "inverse_vol", "equal_weight")),
          bite$gross[bite$strategy == "max_sharpe"] == min(bite$gross),
          all(per_config$rules_below))

The ordering of the medians is monotone in how much a strategy optimizes. Maximum Sharpe loses a median of 0.015 and up to 0.038, while equal weighting loses 0.003. In every one of the nine configurations the two rules lose less than any of the three optimizers; at the top, maximum Sharpe and minimum CVaR change places in 4 of the nine.

Set beside its gross figure, the maximum-Sharpe portfolio surrenders about 3 percent of a median Sharpe ratio that is already the lowest of the five, and 7 percent in its worst configuration. It is the most expensive strategy to run and, in the median configuration, the one with least to show for it; neither fact is visible in Section 9.3, where it is the best portfolio in the chapter. Novy-Marx and Velikov (2016) report the same pattern across published equity anomalies: the strategies with the strongest paper results are disproportionately the ones whose turnover consumes them.

That is the case for treating costs as part of the objective rather than as an audit afterwards, which is what turnover_objective() and transaction_cost_constraint() are for, what Equation 23.3 does analytically, and what Section 18.1 does by construction.

23.3.3 Calendar rules against band rules

Everything measured above rebalances on a calendar: monthly, quarterly, annually. That is not the only policy, and Equation 23.3 suggests it is not the natural one. A band policy ignores the calendar and trades when a weight leaves a tolerance around its target, which is the practical implementation of the no-trade region of Constantinides (1986) and Davis and Norman (1990).

The two policies fail in opposite ways. A calendar rule trades when nothing has happened and does not trade when something has: a portfolio that drifts sharply in week two of a quarter waits ten weeks for its correction. A band rule responds to the portfolio rather than to the date, but its turnover is unpredictable, which is awkward for anyone who has to budget trading costs in advance, and in a trending market it can trade repeatedly as a weight oscillates across a boundary.

Tokat and Wicas (2007) compare the families across a range of assumptions and find the choice matters far less than the width: a wide band and an infrequent calendar produce similar results, and both do better than frequent rebalancing once costs are charged. Donohue and Yip (2003) solve the problem properly as a dynamic program with costs and recover the no-trade region directly. The practical summary is that the width of the tolerance is the decision, and the trigger mechanism is close to a detail.

PortfolioAnalytics has no band policy. One is a few lines around the EOP.Weight history of Section 23.2: compare the drifted weights to the target each month and trade only when one has left its band.

23.3.4 Where the cost model stops being credible

Two limits are worth stating plainly, because the numbers above are only as good as Equation 23.2.

The model is linear and symmetric: the same rate to buy and to sell, the same rate for a one percent trade as for a twenty percent one. That is defensible for the index proxies of this chapter and not for a single security, where impact grows with size and with the fraction of daily volume consumed (Kyle 1985) and the execution schedule becomes a decision in its own right (Almgren and Chriss 2001).

The rates are constants, and real spreads widen exactly when portfolios most want to trade. In a real market, an episode like the credit crisis of Section 4.3 is a period in which the cost of trading credit runs at several times its calm-market level, so a constant rate understates the cost of a strategy whose turnover is concentrated in such episodes, which is precisely the profile of the optimized strategies here. The bite measured above is therefore a lower bound for them and close to exact for equal weighting.

Key Takeaways

  • Turnover is Equation 23.1 and the cost of it is Equation 23.2. Both are linear here, which is defensible at asset-class scale and fails at the scale of a single security, where impact grows with size (Kyle 1985; Almgren and Chriss 2001).
  • Costs are per asset class, not a flat rate: 2 basis points for a government bond against 25 for credit. The dearest class to trade carries the third-highest Sharpe ratio of the sixteen.
  • With costs, the optimal policy is not to return to the target. It is to trade back only to the edge of a no-trade region (Constantinides 1986; Davis and Norman 1990), or to adjust partially towards a moving aim portfolio as in Equation 23.3.
  • Rebalancing less often helps rule-based portfolios at every window; for the optimized ones the sign depends on the window. At sixty months equal weighting moves from 0.626 to 0.654 going from monthly to annual, while maximum Sharpe moves from 0.546 to 0.348; at 120 and 36 months all three optimizers gain.
  • The cost bite is monotone in how much a strategy optimizes: a median of 0.015 of Sharpe ratio for maximum Sharpe against 0.003 for equal weighting.
  • A turnover constraint is hard under ROI and a penalty under CVXR, and proportional costs exist only under ROI. The same specification means different things to the two backends.
  • Return.portfolio() back-fills equal weights for every period before the first supplied weight vector and warns rather than failing. A rolling estimation window always creates that gap, so this is the normal case rather than an edge case.

Exercises

  1. Double every rate in pm_costs_default() and recompute Table 23.2. Does the ordering change, or only the magnitudes? At what multiple does the maximum-Sharpe strategy’s net Sharpe reach zero?
  2. The cost model charges by asset class. Replace it with a single flat rate equal to the average and compare. Which strategies are flattered by the simplification, and why?
  3. Add a turnover_constraint() capping annual turnover at 25 per cent to the maximum-Sharpe specification and rerun it under both ROI and CVXR. Measure the realized turnover under each. Which one bound?
  4. Using Return.portfolio(verbose = TRUE), compute the drift turnover of an equal-weighted portfolio that is never rebalanced. How far does it get from equal weighting by the end of the 31 model years, and which series does it become?
