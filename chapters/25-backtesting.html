25  Backtesting and Diagnostics

Every construction chapter in this book ends with a line of out-of-sample evidence. This is where those lines come from, and where the machinery producing them is taken apart.

The order is deliberate. Most treatments of backtesting spend their length on how to run one and a paragraph on what can go wrong. Here it is the other way round, because running a backtest is four lines and the failure modes are the reason anybody needs a chapter. Harvey and Liu (2015) and López de Prado (2018) argue the same case at greater length: a backtest is not a simulation of history but a statistical estimate, and most of the work is establishing how much of it to believe.

We first set out what a rolling backtest is and what its two window arguments mean, then show a backtest that completes, reports the right length, and contains periods that never solved, then assemble the book’s strategy register and put an error bar on it.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)
Re <- R - sim_riskfree_monthly$return
win <- 60L

25.1 What a Backtest Estimates

A backtest is a loop with a rule about what it is allowed to see. At each rebalancing date \(t\), estimate on data ending strictly before \(t\), form a portfolio, hold it for one period, and record what it earned:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_t = g\!\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{t-W}, \dots, \boldsymbol{r}_{t-1}\right), \qquad r^{p}_t = \hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_t' \boldsymbol{r}_t , \tag{25.1}\]

with \(g\) the strategy and \(W\) the estimation window. The resulting series \(\{r^p_t\}\) is a sample, and every statistic computed from it is an estimate with a standard error. That is the framing this chapter keeps returning to.

spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")

bt <- optimize.portfolio.rebalancing(
  Re, spec, optimize_method = "ROI",
  rebalance_on    = "months",
  training_period = win,          # the first window
  rolling_window  = win - 1L      # and how far back each subsequent one reaches
)
length(bt$opt_rebalancing)
#> [1] 313

Two arguments deserve suspicion.

training_period is the number of observations before the first optimization. rolling_window is the length of \(W\) in Equation 25.1 thereafter, and it is inclusive at both ends, so rolling_window = 60 yields sixty-one observations. The call above passes win - 1L so that every window really holds sixty. This is the kind of detail that moves a Sharpe ratio in the third decimal, and almost no published result records which convention it used.

An expanding window is rolling_window = NULL: every optimization sees everything up to that date. It uses more data and assumes the world has not changed. Section 19.1 argues that it has.

25.2 Implementation

25.2.1 The backtest that looks complete and is not

Give the specification something a real mandate contains — a required return.

target <- 0.0040        # monthly, in excess of the bill: about 4.9% a year

mandate <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only") |>
  add.constraint(type = "return", return_target = target) |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var")

bt_mandate <- suppressWarnings(optimize.portfolio.rebalancing(
  Re, mandate, optimize_method = "ROI",
  rebalance_on = "months", training_period = win, rolling_window = win - 1L))

length(bt_mandate$opt_rebalancing)
#> [1] 313

The same 313 periods as before. The object has the right class, the right length and the right names. The contents are another matter.

table(vapply(bt_mandate$opt_rebalancing, function(x) class(x)[1], character(1)))

n_bad <- sum(no_solution)
stopifnot(length(bt_mandate$opt_rebalancing) == length(bt$opt_rebalancing), n_bad > 0)
bt_mandate$opt_rebalancing[[which(no_solution)[1]]][c("weights", "out", "solver_status")]
#> $weights
#>        CH_credit        CH_equity    CH_government        EU_credit 
#>               NA               NA               NA               NA 
#>        EU_equity    EU_government GLOBAL_commodity        JP_credit 
#>               NA               NA               NA               NA 
#>        JP_equity    JP_government        UK_credit        UK_equity 
#>               NA               NA               NA               NA

#> $out

#> $solver_status
#> [1] "Constraints are inconsistent, no solution."

Every entry has the class of a solved portfolio, and 6 of them hold none. Their weights are NA, their objective is NA, and the solver’s own verdict sits in $solver_status, where nothing downstream reads it.

ImportantA failed period is stored, not raised

This is not a bug in the loop. It is the loop working as written. When ROI reports that no feasible portfolio exists, optimize.portfolio() copies its NA solution through without reading the status code; when a solver throws, the loop’s foreach(.errorhandling = "pass") catches the failing iteration by design and stores the condition object in its place. Either way the backtest completes, the object is well formed, the length is right, and nothing warns.

What happens next depends on which function is reached for.

W_hat  <- extractWeights(bt_mandate)
pr_hat <- suppressWarnings(Return.portfolio(Re, weights = W_hat))
c(summary_works  = !inherits(try(summary(bt_mandate), silent = TRUE), "try-error"),
  na_weight_rows = sum(!stats::complete.cases(W_hat)),
  na_returns     = sum(is.na(pr_hat)))
#>  summary_works na_weight_rows     na_returns 
#>              1              6            243

Neither fails. extractWeights() returns a weight matrix with 6 rows of NA, and Return.portfolio() on it returns NA for every month from the first failed period to the end of the sample, 243 of them, behind a warning about weights not summing to one that reads like a rounding issue. A reader meeting that has almost no chance of tracing it back to a mandate infeasible in 6 months of the sample.

25.2.2 Diagnose every period, always

d <- diagnose_rebalancing(bt_mandate)
table(d$status)
#> 
#> converged    failed 
#>       307         6
fail <- d$date[d$status != "converged"]
pos  <- match(fail, zoo::index(Re))            # where in the sample they fall
stopifnot(length(fail) == n_bad, !anyNA(pos))

Now the part a count of failures would not have shown.

fmt_model(fail)
#> [1] "September of model year 11" "February of model year 12" 
#> [3] "March of model year 12"     "June of model year 12"     
#> [5] "July of model year 12"      "August of model year 12"
span <- diff(range(pos)) + 1L                  # months from the first failure to the last
stopifnot(span <= 12L,                         # all within one year
          in_episode(fail[1], "equity_bear"),  # the first at the end of the bear market
          !any(in_episode(fail[-1], "equity_bear")))

The failures are not scattered. All 6 fall within 12 months of each other, from September of model year 11 to August of model year 12: the first in the last month of the equity bear market of Section 4.3, the rest in the year after it, when no long-only combination of these sixteen series had earned 4.9 percent over the bill across the preceding sixty months.

A backtest dropping those periods does not lose a random sample of its history. It loses precisely the stretch in which the strategy was impossible and reports the average of the months in which it was easy. That is a survivorship problem rather than a rounding error, and it is the mechanism Harvey and Liu (2015) warn about when they say a backtest is an optimistic estimate by construction.

25.2.3 What to do about a period that failed

No answer preserves the strategy. Carrying the previous weights forward is the least dishonest option available, and it is still a different strategy from the one specified: one that stops trading exactly when its mandate becomes infeasible. pm_backtest() does that, warns when it happens, and reports the count in every result row.

run <- suppressWarnings(
  pm_backtest(Re, pm_strategy("mandate", spec = function(assets) mandate),
              estimation_window = win, riskfree = sim_riskfree_monthly,
              verbose = FALSE))
run$metrics |> dplyr::select(strategy, basis, sharpe, n_periods, n_failed)
#> # A tibble: 2 × 5
#>   strategy basis sharpe n_periods n_failed
#>   <chr>    <chr>  <dbl>     <int>    <int>
#> 1 mandate  gross  0.340       313        6
#> 2 mandate  net    0.309       313        6

The n_failed column is not decoration. A result with 6 failures in 313 periods is a different object from one with none, and a table omitting the number is not reporting the backtest that was run.

25.2.4 The parallel loop with no backend

optimize.portfolio.rebalancing() already contains a parallel loop.

out_list <- foreach::foreach(ep = iterators::iter(ep.i),
                             .errorhandling = "pass",
                             .packages = "PortfolioAnalytics") %dopar% { ... }

It never registers a backend. optimize.portfolio() calls registerDo* in its own body; the rebalancing function does not. Out of the box %dopar% falls through to sequential execution and says so only in a warning that reads like noise. Registering a cluster runs the same problem in a fraction of the time.

wall clock
default, no backend registered 273 s
eight workers 61 s

A 4.5-fold speedup on the same four strategies and 313 periods, with weights and metrics identical to the last decimal. pm_backtest(workers = 8) does the registration and restores whatever backend was there before.

One consequence is worth knowing. A message() or warning() emitted on a worker is lost, while an error still returns as a passed condition, so a parallel run is less talkative than a sequential one in exactly the situations where a message would help. Diagnose sequentially when something looks wrong, then re-run in parallel.

optimize.portfolio.parallel() is a different function for a different job. Despite the name it parallelizes repeated runs of a single optimization, its nodes argument spreading the random draws of a stochastic solver, and it has nothing to do with the rebalancing loop.

25.3 The Register, and How Much of It to Believe

25.3.1 The register, assembled

Every construction chapter contributes one strategy. Run together over the same sample, they are the book’s summary of itself.

sweep <- readr::read_csv("output/tables/oos-sweep.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)

Read the table from the bottom. The strategy estimating most, a full mean vector followed by a maximization over it, is last at this window, with 5 times the turnover of equal weighting and a drawdown 1.6 times as deep as minimum variance’s. Every rule that estimates no mean finishes ahead of it: the order is Minimum CVaR, Minimum variance, Equal weight, Inverse volatility, Maximum Sharpe. Minimum CVaR and minimum variance, which estimate a covariance matrix and no mean, lead; equal weighting, which estimates nothing, ranks 3 of 5. The window matters. At 36 months the tangency portfolio is also last, 0.41 of Sharpe ratio behind the best rule that estimates no mean; at 120 months it ranks 3 of 5 and the gap is 0.20.

The claim the book carries out of this table is the one that holds at every window: the rules that estimate no mean are ahead of the rule that estimates one, and the gap closes as the estimation window lengthens, which is what a sampling error in the mean that shrinks with \(W\) predicts. Neither part is a surprise to the literature: DeMiguel et al. (2009) find, across fourteen rules and seven datasets, that no mean-based rule consistently outperforms equal weighting, and Jagannathan and Ma (2003) report the strength of minimum variance. What this table adds is that it happens on a market whose covariance structure can be inspected and with a solver whose failures have been counted.

25.3.2 A backtest is a measurement, and it has an error bar

The Sharpe ratios above are point estimates from 252 monthly observations of Equation 25.1. Under independent and identically distributed returns, Lo (2002) gives the asymptotic standard error

\[ \operatorname{se}(\widehat{SR}) \approx \sqrt{\frac{1 + \tfrac{1}{2}SR^2}{T}} , \tag{25.2}\]

which, applied to the monthly Sharpe ratio over 252 months and annualized, works out as follows.

sr <- reg |>
  dplyr::select(strategy, sharpe) |>
  dplyr::mutate(se = sqrt((1 + (sharpe / sqrt(12))^2 / 2) / Tn) * sqrt(12),
                lower = sharpe - 1.96 * se,
                upper = sharpe + 1.96 * se)
sr
#> # A tibble: 5 × 5
#>   strategy     sharpe    se lower upper
#>   <chr>         <dbl> <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
#> 1 min_cvar      0.893 0.222 0.458 1.33 
#> 2 min_variance  0.829 0.221 0.395 1.26 
#> 3 equal_weight  0.626 0.220 0.195 1.06 
#> 4 inverse_vol   0.573 0.220 0.143 1.00 
#> 5 max_sharpe    0.546 0.220 0.115 0.976
stopifnot(all(outer(sr$lower, sr$upper, `<=`)))   # every interval overlaps every other

Every interval overlaps every other. On this sample none of the differences between these five strategies is statistically distinguishable from zero, including the gap between the best and the worst.

Two caveats point in opposite directions. Equation 25.2 assumes independent and identically distributed returns, which Section 4.3 showed this market violates, so the true intervals are if anything wider; Ledoit and Wolf (2008) give a bootstrap procedure that does not need the assumption, and Jobson and Korkie (1981) with the correction of Memmel (2003) give the test for a difference between two Sharpe ratios rather than for each separately. Against that, these five strategies were not selected from a large search, so the multiple-testing correction of White (2000) and Sullivan et al. (1999), and the deflated Sharpe ratio of Bailey and López de Prado (2014), would not widen them much further. A study that had tried a hundred specifications would need all of that machinery.

The honest reading does not undo the chapter, because two things survive it. The turnover and cost differences are not estimates but arithmetic on observed trades, and they are large. The failure count is not an estimate either: a strategy infeasible in 6 months of one year has a property no confidence interval averages away.

The conclusions worth keeping are the ones that do not depend on the third decimal of a Sharpe ratio. Most of the useful ones do not.

Two later chapters take this further rather than repeating it. Section 26.1 asks what forecast error costs in Sharpe ratio directly, instead of inferring it from a table of outcomes, and Section 28.1 asks what to do when the model itself is in doubt rather than only its parameters.

25.3.3 Attribution: where the return came from

A backtest says what a strategy earned. Attribution says which decisions earned it, and the standard decomposition is Brinson et al. (1986) and Brinson et al. (1991). Writing \(w_i\) and \(b_i\) for the portfolio and benchmark weights in segment \(i\), and \(r_i\) and \(\beta_i\) for the segment returns,

\[ \underbrace{\sum_i (w_i - b_i)\beta_i}_{\text{allocation}} + \underbrace{\sum_i b_i (r_i - \beta_i)}_{\text{selection}} + \underbrace{\sum_i (w_i - b_i)(r_i - \beta_i)}_{\text{interaction}} = \sum_i w_i r_i - \sum_i b_i \beta_i . \tag{25.3}\]

The three terms separate the decision to overweight a segment from the decision of what to hold inside it. For this book that split is the one the part structure is built on: allocation is Parts IV and V, selection is Part VI, and Equation 25.3 is what tells an investor afterwards which of the two was worth paying for.

The raw material is already available. Return.portfolio(verbose = TRUE) returns per-asset contributions for every period, as Section 23.2 showed, and those contributions are the \(w_i r_i\) terms of Equation 25.3. Section 24.1 does the work.

One caution about the famous result. Brinson et al. (1986) is routinely cited for the claim that asset allocation explains about ninety percent of performance, and Ibbotson and Kaplan (2000) show that the number answers a different question from the one it is usually quoted for: it is the share of the variance of returns over time for a single fund, not the share of the difference between funds, which is far smaller. Both are worth knowing, and conflating them is the most common misuse of an attribution result in the industry.

25.3.4 A taxonomy of look-ahead

Equation 25.1 is defined so that \(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_t\) sees only data before \(t\). Violations of that rule are the most common defect in a backtest and they are rarely as obvious as using next month’s return. Four kinds recur.

The estimation window can be misaligned, using data through \(t\) rather than \(t-1\). The one-period lag in the market proxy of Section 5.2 exists for exactly this reason.

The universe can be chosen with hindsight. Every asset in this book’s modeled market survives to the end of the sample, which is a survivorship bias by construction and one the real data sleeves of Section 2.3 do not share.

Parameters can be tuned on the same sample they are scored on. An estimation window length, a shrinkage intensity, a rebalancing frequency: each is a choice, and choosing it by looking at the out-of-sample result makes that result in-sample (Harvey and Liu 2015). The sweep behind Table 25.2 reports all nine configurations rather than the best one for this reason.

Vendor data can be revised after the fact. Macroeconomic series in particular are restated for years, so a backtest using the current vintage uses numbers nobody had at the time.

Only the first is a coding error. The other three are design decisions, and Lo (2004) makes the broader point that a rule fitted to one regime and tested on the same history is measuring the regime rather than the rule.

Key Takeaways

  • A backtest is Equation 25.1: an estimate produced by a rule that may see only the past. Every statistic computed from it carries a standard error (Harvey and Liu 2015).
  • rolling_window is inclusive at both ends, so asking for 60 gives 61 observations. Almost no published result says which convention it used.
  • optimize.portfolio.rebalancing() stores a failed period in the result list, as a portfolio of NA weights when ROI reports no solution and as a condition object when a solver throws, and completes normally. The object has the right class, the right length and the right names.
  • The failures are not random. All 6 here fall within one year, at the end of the equity bear market and just after it, which is precisely the stretch in which the mandate was impossible. Dropping them reports the average of the easy months.
  • Always diagnose every period and always carry the failure count into the result table. A backtest with 6 failures is a different object from one with none.
  • The rebalancing loop contains %dopar% but registers no backend, so it runs on one core and says so in a warning that reads like noise. Registering eight workers cut 273 seconds to 61 with identical results.
  • Applying Equation 25.2 to the register, every confidence interval overlaps every other. The differences in turnover, in cost, and in feasibility are not estimates and do not overlap.

Exercises

  1. Rerun the mandate backtest with return_target at 0.002 and 0.005 monthly. Report the number of failed periods in each and when they occur. At which target does the strategy stop being a strategy?
  2. Replace rolling_window = win - 1L with rolling_window = win and confirm that the windows now hold sixty-one observations. How much does the Sharpe ratio of the minimum-variance strategy move?
  3. Run the same backtest with an expanding window (rolling_window = NULL). Does it do better or worse, and is the difference larger than the error bar computed above?
  4. Take the failed periods and ask what a manager would actually have done. Implement one answer, rerun, and say plainly how your version differs from the strategy that was specified.
