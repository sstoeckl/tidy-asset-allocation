26  The Cost of Forecast Error

Draft|Code: script · exercises · solutions · all chapters|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

What does an error in a return forecast cost, and in which units should the cost be written? Salcher et al. (2026b) answer that the natural unit is the investor’s own: the forecast error measured in the risk metric of the optimizer that consumes it, of which the root mean squared error is a special case under assumptions that are rarely true. Chopra and Ziemba (1993) had put a number on the cost two decades earlier, finding errors in means roughly ten times as costly as errors in variances, and the number has been quoted ever since without the formula behind it. The formula exists: for a mean-variance investor the loss from acting on a wrong mean vector is an identity, a squared distance in which the covariance matrix is the metric (Salcher et al. 2026a). Every statistical score that ignores that metric, the RMSE of Welch and Goyal (2008) and Goyal et al. (2024), the out-of-sample \(R^2\) of Campbell and Thompson (2008), the predictive-accuracy test of Diebold and Mariano (1995), throws part of the cost away; Granger and Pesaran (2000) and Elliott and Timmermann (2016) are the forecasting literature’s own statement that an evaluation belongs in the units of the decision.

This chapter sits on neither side of the horizon axis of Section 3.1: it is about measurement, and the measure applies to a strategic estimate and a tactical forecast alike. We first derive the loss identity and read the risk-adjusted forecast error, the Sharpe-ratio shortfall, the RMSE and the covariance channel off it, then implement the measures and score three forecasts of the mean on rolling windows, check the covariance channel against the expectation of Kan and Zhou (2007) by simulation, and bound what the term-spread forecast of Section 16.2 could ever have earned; finally we ask which channel costs more on this market, where the cost is largest, and whether the register of Section 25.3 ranks its strategies by how much they estimate.

The data are the sixteen risky class series of Section 2.2, in excess of the Treasury bill as in Section 9.1. The full-sample moments serve as the truth wherever a truth is needed, as they did in Section 8.3.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)   # the sixteen risky classes
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return                                  # the bill
Re <- R - rf                                                       # excess returns
N  <- ncol(Re); Tn <- nrow(Re); dates <- zoo::index(R)
mu <- colMeans(Re); S <- stats::cov(Re)                            # the stand-in truth
theta2 <- as.numeric(t(mu) %*% solve(S, mu))                       # squared maximum Sharpe ratio, monthly
stopifnot(N == 16L, !anyNA(Re), model_year(dates[121]) == 11L)

26.1 The Loss in Risk Units

26.1.1 The certainty equivalent and the plug-in loss

A mean-variance investor with risk aversion \(\gamma\) values a position \(\boldsymbol{w}\) by its certainty equivalent, the objective of Section 3.1,

\[ CE(\boldsymbol{w}) = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}, \qquad \boldsymbol{w}^* = \frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\gamma}, \qquad CE^* = \frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}}{2\gamma} = \frac{\theta^2}{2\gamma}, \tag{26.1}\]

with \(\theta\) the maximum Sharpe ratio of Equation 9.5. Write positions and means in risk units, with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\) the symmetric square root,

\[ \boldsymbol{a} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\mu}, \qquad \boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{w}, \qquad\text{so that}\qquad \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} = \boldsymbol{v}'\boldsymbol{a}, \quad \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} = \|\boldsymbol{v}\|^2, \quad \|\boldsymbol{a}\| = \theta . \tag{26.2}\]

The vector \(\boldsymbol{a}\) lists the Sharpe ratios of \(N\) orthogonal directions, and the optimum is \(\boldsymbol{v}^* = \boldsymbol{a}/\gamma\). For any position the loss is then a squared distance:

\[ \begin{aligned} CE^* - CE(\boldsymbol{w}) &= \frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\boldsymbol{a}\|^2 - \boldsymbol{v}'\boldsymbol{a} + \frac{\gamma}{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^2 = \frac{1}{2\gamma}\left(\|\boldsymbol{a}\|^2 - 2\gamma\,\boldsymbol{v}'\boldsymbol{a} + \gamma^2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^2\right) = \frac{1}{2\gamma}\,\|\gamma\boldsymbol{v} - \boldsymbol{a}\|^2 . \end{aligned} \tag{26.3}\]

This is the loss function of Klein and Bawa (1976) and Kan and Zhou (2007) written in risk units, and it holds for every position, not only the plug-in. Now let the investor hold a forecast \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) of \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\), from any source, and know \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). The plug-in position \(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}/\gamma\) has \(\gamma\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{a}}\), and Equation 26.3 becomes

\[ CE^* - CE(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}) = \frac{1}{2\gamma}\,\|\hat{\boldsymbol{a}} - \boldsymbol{a}\|^2 = \frac{1}{2\gamma}\,(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \frac{\text{RAFE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^2}{2\gamma} , \tag{26.4}\]

where the risk-adjusted forecast error \(\text{RAFE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}) = \|\hat{\boldsymbol{a}} - \boldsymbol{a}\|\) is the Mahalanobis distance between forecast and truth under \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), the distance Stöckl and Hanke (2014) proposed as a multivariate forecast score and Salcher et al. (2026b) named. Equation 26.4 says three things at once. Welfare is lost only through forecast error; the error matters only in risk units; and the loss is exact, with no bound and no approximation. The metric is the one the optimizer uses: an error in a direction of low risk is multiplied by a large entry of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\), which is the same multiplication that turns the error into a position, the mechanism Michaud (1989) called error maximization and Best and Grauer (1991) wrote down. For the sample mean on \(T\) independent months, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{a}} \sim N(\boldsymbol{a}, \boldsymbol{I}_N/T)\) and the expected squared error is \(\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{I}_N/T) = N/T\): each of the \(N\) dimensions of the forecast costs \(1/T\), the variance of an estimated Sharpe ratio, whatever signal it carries (Salcher et al. 2026a). That is Equation 8.6 with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) known, and it is why the precision result of Merton (1980) in Section 4.1 is the price of everything in this chapter.

26.1.2 From the certainty equivalent to the Sharpe ratio

A Sharpe-ratio investor sees the direction of a position and not its length, so the identity turns into a bound. With \(\varphi\) the angle between \(\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}\) and \(\boldsymbol{a}\),

\[ SR(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}) = \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}} {\sqrt{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}} = \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}'\boldsymbol{a}}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}\|} = \theta\cos\varphi , \qquad \Delta = \theta - SR(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}) = \theta\,(1 - \cos\varphi) , \tag{26.5}\]

so the Sharpe-ratio shortfall \(\Delta\) depends on the forecast only through its angle with the truth. The distance from \(\boldsymbol{a}\) to the ray through \(\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}\) is \(\theta\sin\varphi\) when \(\varphi \le \pi/2\) and \(\theta\) otherwise, and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}\) lies on that ray, so \(\text{RAFE} \ge \theta\sin\varphi\); since \(1 - \cos\varphi \le \sin\varphi\) on \([0, \pi/2]\),

\[ \Delta \le c_0\,\text{RAFE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}), \qquad c_0 = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \text{RAFE} \le \theta, \\ 2 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases} \tag{26.6}\]

the first case because \(\text{RAFE} \le \theta\) forces \(\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}'\boldsymbol{a} \ge 0\) and hence \(\varphi \le \pi/2\) (Salcher et al. 2026b; Stöckl and Hanke 2026). The RAFE therefore bounds the Sharpe-ratio shortfall and equals, up to \(2\gamma\), the certainty-equivalent loss. The squared-Sharpe decomposition is the same statement read in squares: \(\theta^2 - SR(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}})^2 = \theta^2\sin^2\varphi\), the squared Sharpe ratio of the part of the truth the forecast points away from.

26.1.3 The RMSE as a special case

The usual score replaces \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) in Equation 26.4 by something simpler. Salcher et al. (2026b) order the simplifications as a nested sequence,

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{RAFE}^2 &= (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) &&\text{the investor's metric,}\\ \text{RAFE-CC}_\rho^2 &= (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\rho^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}), \quad \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\rho = \boldsymbol{D}_\sigma\big[(1-\rho)\boldsymbol{I} + \rho\,\boldsymbol{\iota}\boldsymbol{\iota}'\big]\boldsymbol{D}_\sigma &&\text{every correlation set to } \rho,\\ \text{RAFE-CC}_0^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(\hat{\mu}_i - \mu_i)^2}{\sigma_i^2} &&\text{correlations ignored,}\\ \text{RAFE-CV}^2 &= \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{\mu}_i - \mu_i)^2 &&\text{one volatility for all,}\\ N\cdot\text{RMSE}^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{\mu}_i - \mu_i)^2 &&\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \boldsymbol{I} . \end{aligned} \tag{26.7}\]

Each step discards something the optimizer uses. Setting the correlations to a constant removes the information about which combinations of assets are cheap to hold; setting them to zero treats a government bond and the credit index of the same region as unrelated, so that an error that cancels in the portfolio is charged twice; setting the variances equal makes a one-percent error on US_government cost the same as one on US_equity, although the first is a far larger move in Sharpe units; and \(\sigma = 1\) removes the scale altogether. In their simulation the correlation between the error measure and the tangency portfolio’s shortfall falls from 0.77 for the RAFE to 0.42 for the RMSE, and on their empirical data from 0.68 to 0.11 (Salcher et al. 2026b): heavy tails and time-varying moments widen the gap, because the RMSE loses almost all of its explanatory power under them and the RAFE keeps most of its. Kandel and Stambaugh (1996) had made the point from the other side, that an \(R^2\) too small to pass a statistical test can move an allocation a great deal, Cenesizoglu and Timmermann (2012) document the same disconnect on return-prediction models, and Section 27.1 draws the consequence for how a forecaster should be trained.

26.1.4 The covariance channel

When \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is estimated too, the plug-in position is \(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}/\gamma\) and a second error enters, the estimated precision matrix measured in true risk units, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{C}} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\), which equals \(\boldsymbol{I}_N\) when the estimate is right. The position in risk units is \(\gamma\boldsymbol{v} = \hat{\boldsymbol{C}}\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}\), and Equation 26.3 gives, path by path, \(2\gamma\,\text{loss} = \|\hat{\boldsymbol{C}}\hat{\boldsymbol{a}} - \boldsymbol{a}\|^2\). Normalizing to unit length and applying the triangle inequality to \(\hat{\boldsymbol{C}}\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}/\|\hat{\boldsymbol{C}}\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}\| - \boldsymbol{a}/\theta\) separates the two errors in the Sharpe-ratio shortfall,

\[ \Delta \le c_0\,\text{RAFE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}) + \theta\cdot\text{C-RAFE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}), \qquad \text{C-RAFE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}) = \big\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2} - \boldsymbol{I}_N\big\|_2 , \tag{26.8}\]

with \(\|\cdot\|_2\) the spectral norm, the largest relative eigenvalue error of the inverse (Salcher et al. 2026b). The covariance error is multiplied by \(\theta\): errors in risk cost most where the opportunity set is best. The expectation of the same identity is the result of Kan and Zhou (2007) that Section 8.1 quoted as Equation 8.6. Under normality \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) are independent, \((T-1)\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) is Wishart, and the two moments of the inverse are known,

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\big] = \alpha_N\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}, \quad \mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\big] = \beta_N\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}, \qquad \alpha_N = \frac{T-1}{T-N-2}, \quad \beta_N = \frac{(T-1)^2(T-2)}{(T-N-1)(T-N-2)(T-N-4)} , \tag{26.9}\]

both above one and both unbounded as \(N/T \to 1\). Taking the expectation of the mean term over \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) first gives \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}'\boldsymbol{\mu}] = \alpha_N\theta^2/\gamma\); the variance term gives \(\beta_N\,\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}]/\gamma^2 = \beta_N(\theta^2 + N/T)/\gamma^2\), because the sample mean has covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}/T\). Subtracting from \(2\gamma\,CE^* = \theta^2\) and adding and subtracting \(\theta^2\) and \(N/T\),

\[ 2\gamma\,\mathbb{E}[\text{loss}] = \underbrace{\frac{N}{T}}_{\text{mean noise}} \;+\;\underbrace{(1 - 2\alpha_N + \beta_N)\,\theta^2}_{\text{inversion error}} \;+\;\underbrace{(\beta_N - 1)\frac{N}{T}}_{\text{noise, amplified}} , \tag{26.10}\]

which is Equation 8.6 rearranged (Salcher et al. 2026a). The first term is the mean channel of Equation 26.4. The second is the cost of inverting a noisy matrix with the true mean in hand, and since \(1 - 2\alpha_N + \beta_N = (\alpha_N - 1)^2 + (\beta_N - \alpha_N^2)\) it collects a leverage bias and a direction error. The third is the interaction: the noise in the forecast, multiplied by the inverse that over-reacts to it. The substitution design of Table 8.3, which holds one input at its truth and estimates the other, measures the first two terms and attributes the third to neither, because it vanishes when either input is true. A long-only constraint caps every term, which is Jagannathan and Ma (2003)’s shrinkage again and the reason the Sharpe shortfalls of Section 8.3 are smaller than the unconstrained ones below. Heavier tails raise \(\alpha_N\) and \(\beta_N\), since the constants of Equation 26.9 grow with the kurtosis for elliptical returns, so the covariance channel is priced faster in \(N/T\) than the normal case says (Salcher et al. 2026a).

26.1.5 An upper bound from the forecast’s information

The measures above price a forecast that exists. A bound prices one that could. For a single asset let \(r_{t+1} = m_t + e_{t+1}\) with \(m_t\) the conditional mean the forecast would deliver, \(\operatorname{Var}(m_t) = R^2\sigma^2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(e_{t+1}) = (1 - R^2)\sigma^2\), where \(R^2\) is the forecast’s explanatory power in the sense of Equation 16.2 and \(S_0 = \mu/\sigma\) the asset’s unconditional Sharpe ratio. The conditional tangency position holds \(m_t/(\gamma\sigma_e^2)\), and its average conditional squared Sharpe ratio, the ceiling Campbell and Thompson (2008) compute, is

\[ \theta_c^2 = \frac{\mathbb{E}[m_t^2]}{\sigma_e^2} = \frac{\mu^2 + R^2\sigma^2}{(1 - R^2)\,\sigma^2} = \frac{S_0^2 + R^2}{1 - R^2} , \tag{26.11}\]

using \(\mathbb{E}[m_t^2] = \mu^2 + \operatorname{Var}(m_t)\). The multivariate version replaces squares by quadratic forms: with \(\boldsymbol{m}_t\) the conditional mean vector and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_e\) the conditional covariance matrix,

\[ \theta_c^2 = \mathbb{E}\big[\boldsymbol{m}_t'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_e^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}_t\big] = \boldsymbol{\mu}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_e^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu} + \operatorname{tr}\big(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_e^{-1}\operatorname{Var}(\boldsymbol{m}_t)\big) , \tag{26.12}\]

the squared Sharpe ratio of the unconditional tangency portfolio under the residual covariance plus the information the forecast adds in every direction. No strategy built on the forecast can exceed \(\theta_c\) on average; it is the statistical limit of what the information allows. The bound is tight for an investor who holds the conditional tangency position every month, with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_e\) known and no constraint, and slack for every restrained use of the forecast, a long-only tilt inside a band, a position on the sign of the forecast only, or a rule that averages the forecast over groups, each of which discards part of \(\operatorname{Var}(\boldsymbol{m}_t)\) on purpose and pays \(1/T\) for every dimension it keeps (Salcher et al. 2026a). Its sample counterpart on \(T\) months inherits the inflation of Equation 8.5, so the bound is estimated with the same error as everything else in this chapter.

26.2 Measuring the Error in the Investor’s Units

26.2.1 Two functions and a worked box

The measures are a few lines each. The long-only tangency and minimum-variance programs and the Sharpe ratio under given moments are those of Section 8.2, repeated by name; the Bayes-Stein estimator is Equation 6.4 as Section 6.2 wrote it.

gmv_qp <- function(S) {
  n <- ncol(S)
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S * 1e4, dvec = rep(0, n),
                            Amat = cbind(rep(1, n), diag(n)), bvec = c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)
  w <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  setNames(w / sum(w), colnames(S))
}
tan_qp <- function(mu, S) {
  n <- length(mu)
  if (max(mu) <= 0) return(structure(gmv_qp(S), fallback = TRUE))
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S * 1e4, dvec = rep(0, n),
                            Amat = cbind(mu * 1e2, diag(n)), bvec = c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)
  y <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  structure(setNames(y / sum(y), names(mu)), fallback = FALSE)
}
sharpe_of <- function(w, mu, S) as.numeric(sum(w * mu) / sqrt(t(w) %*% S %*% w)) * sqrt(12)

bayes_stein <- function(R) {                      # @eq-06-bayes-stein, chapter 6
  X <- zoo::coredata(R); Tw <- nrow(X); n <- ncol(X)
  m    <- colMeans(X)
  V    <- stats::cov(X) * (Tw - 1) / (Tw - n - 2)
  Vinv <- solve(V); iota <- rep(1, n)
  m0   <- as.numeric(t(iota) %*% Vinv %*% m) / as.numeric(t(iota) %*% Vinv %*% iota)
  d    <- m - m0
  v    <- (n + 2) / ((n + 2) + Tw * as.numeric(t(d) %*% Vinv %*% d))
  list(mu = setNames((1 - v) * m + v * m0, colnames(X)), mu0 = m0, v = v)
}
# @eq-26-rafe: the forecast error in risk units, monthly.
rafe <- function(mu_hat, mu, Sigma) {
  e <- mu_hat - mu
  sqrt(as.numeric(t(e) %*% solve(Sigma, e)))
}
rmse <- function(mu_hat, mu) sqrt(mean((mu_hat - mu)^2))

# @eq-26-loss-identity: 2 gamma times the certainty-equivalent loss of any position w,
# under the moments (mu, Sigma). gamma cancels when w is a plug-in position at gamma = 1.
ce_loss2 <- function(w, mu, Sigma) {
  d <- w - solve(Sigma, mu)
  as.numeric(t(d) %*% Sigma %*% d)
}
# @eq-26-cos: the Sharpe-ratio shortfall of a position against the maximum, annualized.
sharpe_shortfall <- function(w, mu, Sigma) {
  sqrt(12 * as.numeric(t(mu) %*% solve(Sigma, mu))) - sharpe_of(w, mu, Sigma)
}
NoteWorked: the first sixty months against the full-sample truth 
m60 <- colMeans(zoo::coredata(Re)[1:60, ])            # the sample mean of the first window
e60 <- m60 - mu
w60 <- solve(S, m60)                                   # plug-in position, gamma = 1, Sigma known

            cos  = as.numeric(t(m60) %*% solve(S, mu)) /
                   sqrt(as.numeric(t(m60) %*% solve(S, m60)) * theta2),
            loss = ce_loss2(w60, mu, S), short = sharpe_shortfall(w60, mu, S))
stopifnot(abs(wk$loss - wk$rafe^2) < 1e-12,                             # @eq-26-rafe
          abs(wk$short - sqrt(12 * theta2) * (1 - wk$cos)) < 1e-12)     # @eq-26-cos
round(unlist(wk), 4)

Substituting the first window’s sample mean and the full-sample moments into Equation 26.4 and Equation 26.5,

\[ \text{RAFE}^2 = (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = 0.0832 \quad\text{against}\quad N\cdot\text{RMSE}^2 = 0.000100, \]

\[ \cos\varphi = \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}'\boldsymbol{a}}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}\|\,\|\boldsymbol{a}\|} = 0.684, \qquad \Delta = \theta(1 - \cos\varphi) = 1.102 \times 0.316 = 0.348 \le c_0\,\text{RAFE} = 0.999 , \]

in annualized units. The certainty-equivalent loss at \(\gamma = 1\) is \(\text{RAFE}^2/2 = 0.0416\) a month against an optimum of \(\theta^2/2 = 0.0506\): with the covariance matrix known, the forecast error costs 82% of the attainable certainty equivalent. The expected \(\text{RAFE}^2\) on sixty months is \(N/T = 0.267\); this window came in below it.

26.2.2 Three forecasts of the mean on rolling windows

Section 6.3 re-estimated three vectors on rolling sixty-month windows, the sample mean, the Bayes-Stein estimate and the equilibrium return, and Section 8.2 measured the Sharpe gap of the portfolios built on the first. Here the same three forecasts are scored. The equilibrium needs the market-weight proxy of Section 6.2, repeated with its stated assumption.

The design is that of Salcher et al. (2026b): a training window supplies the forecast and the estimated covariance matrix, and the window that follows supplies the realization against which both are scored. They use sixty training and thirty-six test months on twelve industries; on sixteen series a thirty-six month test window has \(N/T = 0.44\) and a covariance matrix too poorly estimated to whiten with, so the test window here is sixty months too. For every window and forecast we record the RMSE, the RAFE whitened by the realized covariance matrix of the test window, the paper’s measure, and the RAFE whitened by the covariance matrix estimated on the training window, the measure available in real time and the sample Mahalanobis distance that Stöckl and Hanke (2026) show enters the corrected mean. Two rules convert each forecast into a position, the unconstrained plug-in direction \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) that Equation 26.4 and Equation 26.8 price and the long-only tangency portfolio the register holds, and each rule’s shortfall is its Sharpe ratio under the test-window moments against what its own perfect-foresight version attains there.

win <- 60L; test <- 60L
starts <- seq_len(Tn - win - test + 1L)
X <- zoo::coredata(Re)

roll <- lapply(starts, function(s) {
  tr <- s:(s + win - 1L); te <- (s + win):(s + win + test - 1L)
  m_tr <- colMeans(X[tr, ]); V_tr <- stats::cov(X[tr, ])
  m_te <- colMeans(X[te, ]); V_te <- stats::cov(X[te, ])
  wm   <- colMeans(W16[tr, , drop = FALSE])
  fc <- list(sample      = m_tr,
             bayes_stein = bayes_stein(X[tr, ])$mu,
             equilibrium = setNames(delta16 * as.numeric(V_tr %*% wm), colnames(Re)))   # @eq-06-equilibrium
  stopifnot(all(vapply(fc, function(f) identical(names(f), colnames(Re)), logical(1))))
  ceiling_u  <- sqrt(12 * as.numeric(t(m_te) %*% solve(V_te, m_te)))       # perfect foresight, unconstrained
  ceiling_lo <- sharpe_of(tan_qp(m_te, V_te), m_te, V_te)                  # perfect foresight, long only
  dplyr::bind_rows(lapply(names(fc), function(k) {
    w_u  <- solve(V_tr, fc[[k]])
    w_lo <- tan_qp(fc[[k]], V_tr)
    w_k  <- solve(V_te, fc[[k]])                                           # covariance known
    tibble::tibble(
      start = dates[te[1]], forecast = k,
      rmse       = rmse(fc[[k]], m_te),
      rafe_real  = rafe(fc[[k]], m_te, V_te) * sqrt(12),
      rafe_est   = rafe(fc[[k]], m_te, V_tr) * sqrt(12),
      short_u    = ceiling_u  - sharpe_of(w_u,  m_te, V_te),
      short_lo   = ceiling_lo - sharpe_of(w_lo, m_te, V_te),
      loss_known = 12 * ce_loss2(w_k, m_te, V_te))                        # @eq-26-rafe, to be checked
  }))
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows()

stopifnot(nrow(roll) == 3L * length(starts),
          max(abs(roll$loss_known - roll$rafe_real^2)) < 1e-9)            # the identity, window by window
c(windows = length(starts), first_test_month = fmt_model(min(roll$start)))

#>                     "253" "January of model year 6"

The last assertion is Equation 26.4 holding on data: with the covariance matrix taken as known, the certainty-equivalent loss equals the squared RAFE on every one of the 253 windows, to machine precision. The Sharpe-ratio shortfall is where the error measures can be compared.

pts <- roll |>

  tidyr::pivot_longer(c(rmse, rafe_real, rafe_est), names_to = "measure", values_to = "error") |>

Read Table 26.1 row by row. For the unconstrained plug-in the order is the one Equation 26.4 predicts, on every forecast and pooled: the RMSE is nearly uncorrelated with the shortfall, 0.09 pooled, while the RAFE under the realized covariance matrix correlates 0.55 and under the estimated one 0.56. The paper’s 0.68 on industry data is in the same place. For the long-only tangency the picture is different in one respect. The RAFE in the investor’s own estimated units still dominates the RMSE for every forecast, 0.64 against 0.49 for the sample mean, but the RAFE under the realized covariance matrix falls below the RMSE for the sample mean and for Bayes-Stein, 0.37 against 0.49, while for the equilibrium the two are 0.16 and 0.16. The reason is the constraint: a long-only position is \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) projected onto the simplex in the metric of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\), the matrix the investor held, and the measure that explains its shortfall is the one in that metric. Equation 26.4 does not cover constrained positions and Salcher et al. (2026b) report them only across strategies; here the effect is visible within one.

26.2.3 The covariance channel by simulation

Equation 26.10 is an expectation under normality, and chapter 8 measured the two channels by substitution with a long-only rule. Here the unconstrained plug-in is drawn from a normal distribution with this market’s moments, a few hundred times per window length, and the three terms are checked against the draws in certainty-equivalent and in Sharpe units.

set.seed(1602)
L <- chol(S)
draw_months <- function(Tw, mu, L) matrix(rnorm(Tw * length(mu)), Tw) %*% L + matrix(mu, Tw, length(mu), byrow = TRUE)
alpha_n <- function(Tw, n) (Tw - 1) / (Tw - n - 2)
beta_n  <- function(Tw, n) (Tw - 1)^2 * (Tw - 2) / ((Tw - n - 1) * (Tw - n - 2) * (Tw - n - 4))
Ts <- c(36L, 60L, 120L, 240L, 372L, 1000L); B <- 300L

mc <- lapply(Ts, function(Tw) {
  d <- vapply(seq_len(B), function(b) {
    Xb <- draw_months(Tw, mu, L); m <- colMeans(Xb); V <- stats::cov(Xb)
    w_both <- solve(V, m); w_mean <- solve(S, m); w_cov <- solve(V, mu)
    c(rafe2      = rafe(m, mu, S)^2,
      loss_both  = ce_loss2(w_both, mu, S), loss_mean = ce_loss2(w_mean, mu, S), loss_cov = ce_loss2(w_cov, mu, S),
      short_both = sharpe_shortfall(w_both, mu, S), short_mean = sharpe_shortfall(w_mean, mu, S),
      short_cov  = sharpe_shortfall(w_cov, mu, S))
  }, numeric(7))
  tibble::tibble(T = Tw, stat = rownames(d), value = rowMeans(d))
}) |> dplyr::bind_rows() |>
  tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = stat, values_from = value) |>
  dplyr::mutate(f_mean = N / T,
                f_inv  = (1 - 2 * alpha_n(T, N) + beta_n(T, N)) * theta2,
                f_amp  = (beta_n(T, N) - 1) * N / T,
                f_both = f_mean + f_inv + f_amp,                            # @eq-26-kz-split
                cov_share_ce = 1 - loss_mean / loss_both,
                cov_share_sr = 1 - short_mean / short_both)
stopifnot(identical(mc$T, Ts),
          all(abs(mc$rafe2 - mc$f_mean) / mc$f_mean < 0.05),               # E[RAFE^2] = N / T
          all(abs(mc$loss_both - mc$f_both) < pmax(0.1 * mc$f_both, 0.005)),   # @eq-26-kz-split, within sampling error
          all(abs(mc$loss_mean - mc$rafe2) < 1e-12),                       # the identity, draw by draw
          all(mc$short_mean > mc$short_cov))                               # in Sharpe units the mean channel leads

The simulation and the algebra agree to the second decimal: the mean channel averages 0.274 at sixty months against \(N/T = 0.267\), and the full loss 0.853 against the 0.845 of Equation 26.10. What the table adds to Table 8.3 is the third term. At sixty months the inversion error with the true mean in hand is only 0.100, the number a substitution design reports as the covariance channel, but the amplification of the mean’s noise by the inverse is 0.479, larger than the mean channel itself. In Sharpe units the ranking is the familiar one, the mean channel 0.53 against the covariance channel 0.15, because the Sharpe ratio does not see the length of a position and the amplification mostly lengthens it: \(\beta_N\) scales the whole of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\), and a scaled direction has the same Sharpe ratio. Section 26.3 returns to this.

26.2.4 The bound on the term-spread forecast

Section 16.2 found one signal that survived out of sample, the term spread of Fama and French (1989), and Section 16.3 turned it into a banded tilt around equal weighting. Equation 26.11 says what the forecast could have earned at most. The forecast is rebuilt here as chapter 16 built it: the spread read off the curve at each month end, the predictive regression of Equation 16.1 fit on an expanding window after a burn-in of 120 months, and the out-of-sample \(R^2\) of Equation 16.3 against the expanding historical mean.

cv <- sim_curve_weekly |>
  dplyr::filter(series == "US_Slope") |>
  dplyr::arrange(date)
i_last <- findInterval(dates, cv$date)                    # last weekly row on or before each month end
stopifnot(all(cv$date[i_last] <= dates), all(dates - cv$date[i_last] < 8))
term_lag <- as.numeric(xts::lag.xts(xts::xts(-cv$value[i_last], order.by = dates), 1))   # known at t - 1

burn <- 120L; oos <- seq.int(burn + 1L, Tn)
stopifnot(model_year(dates[oos[1]]) == 11L)
ols_fc <- function(y, x, m) {                             # fit strictly before m, forecast m
  tr <- which(!is.na(x) & seq_along(y) < m)
  b  <- stats::lm.fit(cbind(1, x[tr]), y[tr])$coefficients
  unname(b[1] + b[2] * x[m])
}
Fm <- sapply(colnames(Re), function(s) vapply(oos, function(m) ols_fc(X[, s], term_lag, m), numeric(1)))
Mm <- sapply(colnames(Re), function(s) vapply(oos, function(m) mean(X[seq_len(m - 1L), s]), numeric(1)))
Y  <- X[oos, ]
stopifnot(identical(colnames(Fm), colnames(Re)), !anyNA(Fm), nrow(Fm) == length(oos))

r2_os <- function(y, f, f0) 1 - sum((y - f)^2) / sum((y - f0)^2)      # @eq-16-r2os
r2 <- vapply(colnames(Re), function(s) r2_os(Y[, s], Fm[, s], Mm[, s]), numeric(1))

# The equal-weight policy as one asset: its forecast is the average forecast.
y_p <- rowMeans(Y); f_p <- rowMeans(Fm); m_p <- rowMeans(Mm)
r2_p  <- r2_os(y_p, f_p, m_p)
s0_p  <- mean(y_p) / stats::sd(y_p)                                   # unconditional, monthly
bound_p <- sqrt((s0_p^2 + max(r2_p, 0)) / (1 - max(r2_p, 0))) * sqrt(12)   # @eq-26-bound1
round(c(median_r2 = median(r2), positive = sum(r2 > 0), policy_r2 = r2_p,
        policy_sharpe = s0_p * sqrt(12), bound_one_asset = bound_p), 4)
#>       median_r2        positive       policy_r2   policy_sharpe bound_one_asset 
#>          0.0052         10.0000          0.0043          0.6491          0.6888
vol60 <- zoo::rollapply(Re, 60, stats::sd, align = "right", fill = NA)
band  <- 0.5 / N
kappa <- band / (0.5 / sqrt(12))                          # @eq-16-tilt
w_policy <- setNames(rep(1 / N, N), colnames(Re))
W_term <- t(vapply(seq_along(oos), function(i) {
  m  <- oos[i]
  s  <- Fm[i, ] / as.numeric(vol60[m - 1L, ]); s <- s - mean(s)
  tl <- pmin(pmax(kappa * s, -band), band)
  w  <- w_policy + tl - mean(tl); w <- pmax(w, 0); w / sum(w)
}, numeric(N)))
Rm <- zoo::coredata(R)[oos, colnames(Re)]
stopifnot(identical(colnames(W_term), colnames(Re)))
sr_arith <- function(r) mean(r - rf[oos]) / stats::sd(r - rf[oos]) * sqrt(12)
tilt <- c(policy = sr_arith(rowSums(Rm) / N), tilt = sr_arith(rowSums(W_term * Rm)))
stopifnot(abs(tilt[["policy"]] - s0_p * sqrt(12)) < 1e-12,
          bound_p > tilt[["policy"]],
          tilt[["tilt"]] - tilt[["policy"]] < bound_p - tilt[["policy"]])
round(tilt, 3)
#> policy   tilt 
#>  0.649  0.679

The forecast is weak and the bound is close. The equal-weight policy, treated as one asset whose forecast is the average of the sixteen, has an out-of-sample \(R^2\) of 0.43% and an unconditional Sharpe ratio of 0.649 over the 252 out-of-sample months; Equation 26.11 with these numbers,

\[ \theta_c = \sqrt{\frac{0.0351 + 0.0043}{1 - 0.0043}} \times \sqrt{12} = 0.689 , \]

allows a timer of the policy at most 0.040 of Sharpe ratio on top of it. The tilt of Equation 16.9 realized 0.679, 0.029 above the policy, inside the bound. Campbell and Thompson (2008) made the point that an \(R^2\) of this size is economically meaningful, and Equation 26.11 says how meaningful: a few hundredths of a Sharpe ratio. The one-asset bound is the right ceiling for a timer of the policy and a loose one for the tilt, which reads the forecast cross-sectionally; the multivariate ceiling of Equation 26.12 is higher, but reaching it needs the unconstrained conditional tangency position every month with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_e\) known, and the tilt keeps one dimension of that information inside a band and pays \(1/T\) for it (Salcher et al. 2026a). The distance to the ceiling is the price of restraint, paid because Section 8.3 showed what the unconstrained position costs when its inputs are estimated.

26.3 Which Channel Costs More

26.3.1 The two channels at 36, 60 and 120 months

The answer depends on the unit, and both answers are right. In the certainty equivalent the covariance channel costs more than the mean channel at thirty-six and sixty months, 68% of the loss at sixty, and less at 120, 44%, because the Wishart constants of Equation 26.9 approach one as \(N/T\) shrinks while the mean channel falls only as \(1/T\). In Sharpe units the mean channel costs more at every window, with the covariance channel at 13% at sixty months. Chopra and Ziemba (1993)’s ten-to-one is a Sharpe-unit statement; the certainty equivalent of an unconstrained investor charges the covariance error for the leverage it induces, which the Sharpe ratio does not see and a long-only constraint does not allow. For the register’s rules, long only and rebalanced monthly, the Sharpe reading is the relevant one, and it agrees with Table 8.3.

26.3.2 Where the shortfall is largest

The rolling measurement above cannot reach the last two episodes, because a sixty-month test window has to end inside the sample. Against the full-sample truth it can: for every sixty-month estimation window ending at \(t\), the RAFE of its sample mean and the shortfall of the two rules under the truth are one number each, and the windows can be sorted by the episode their last month falls in.

ends <- seq.int(win, Tn - 1L)
ceiling_lo_truth <- sharpe_of(tan_qp(mu, S), mu, S)

by_end <- lapply(ends, function(e) {
  Xw <- X[(e - win + 1L):e, ]; m <- colMeans(Xw); V <- stats::cov(Xw)

A window that ends inside the credit crisis produces the worst unconstrained portfolio, a shortfall of 0.92 against 0.49 for a window that ends outside every episode, and its RAFE of 1.70 is the measure saying so in advance of the portfolio. The long-only rule is hurt most by a window ending in the rates shock, 0.30 against 0.17. The series the true tangency portfolio holds most of is US_government, and its estimated mean is negative in 45% of the windows ending in the rates shock against none of the others; a long-only rule excludes a series whose mean it estimates below zero, and here it excludes the one the truth holds most of. The equity bear market produces a RAFE as large as the credit crisis, 1.72, and a smaller shortfall, which Equation 26.5 explains: the RAFE reads the length of the error and the shortfall its angle, and errors of the same length in different directions cost different amounts of Sharpe ratio.

26.3.3 The register, ranked by what it estimates

The register of Section 25.3 holds five strategies on the sixty-month window, and they can be ordered by how much they estimate: equal weighting estimates nothing, inverse volatility \(N\) volatilities, minimum variance the covariance matrix, minimum CVaR the lower tail of the whole return distribution, and the tangency portfolio the covariance matrix and the mean vector together. Figure 8.1 showed that the Sharpe gap, promised minus realized, follows that order for the four of them that chapter 8 ran. The shortfall of Salcher et al. (2026b) is a different quantity: each strategy’s own perfect-foresight Sharpe ratio on the out-of-sample months, with its weights solved on the moments of those months and held, minus what the rolling strategy realized there.

sweep <- utils::read.csv("output/tables/oos-sweep.csv") |>
  dplyr::filter(universe == "classes", frequency == "months", basis == "gross", window == 60)
oos_r <- seq.int(121L, Tn)
stopifnot(sweep$n_periods[1] - 1L == length(oos_r), model_year(dates[oos_r[1]]) == 11L)

sharpe_realized <- function(r, rf) {                       # as the register computes it
  n <- length(r)
  (prod(1 + r)^(12 / n) - prod(1 + rf)^(12 / n)) / (stats::sd(r) * sqrt(12))
}

  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |> add.constraint(type = "long_only") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk", name = "ES", arguments = list(p = 0.95))

           inverse_vol  = (1 / sqrt(diag(V_o))) / sum(1 / sqrt(diag(V_o))),

           max_sharpe   = tan_qp(m_o, V_o))

                      perfect_foresight = vapply(pf, function(w) sharpe_realized(as.numeric(Rt %*% w), rf[oos_r]), numeric(1))) |>
  dplyr::left_join(dplyr::select(sweep, strategy, label, realized = sharpe), by = "strategy") |>
  dplyr::mutate(shortfall = perfect_foresight - realized,

          reg$in_se[reg$strategy == "max_sharpe"] > 1, all(abs(reg$in_se[reg$strategy != "max_sharpe"]) < 1),

          reg$strategy[which.max(reg$shortfall)] == "max_sharpe",

          all(reg$shortfall[reg$strategy %in% c("inverse_vol", "min_variance")] < 0.05))

The order holds at both ends and breaks in the middle. Equal weighting has a shortfall of exactly zero, because a rule that estimates nothing has nothing to get wrong; inverse volatility loses 0.035 and minimum variance’s shortfall is -0.002, the covariance channel on sixteen well-estimated series, which Table 8.3 found nearly closed; and the tangency portfolio, the one rule that estimates the mean, loses 0.42, from a perfect-foresight 0.97 to a realized 0.55. The exception is minimum CVaR, whose shortfall is -0.19: the rolling strategy realized more than its own perfect-foresight version. The perfect-foresight portfolio minimizes expected shortfall, not variance, and the portfolio that minimizes the lower tail over 252 months at once is not the one with the highest Sharpe ratio over them, whereas the rolling version, re-solved on sixty months at a time, tracks the volatility clustering that Section 4.3 measured. A negative shortfall is not estimation paying off; it is the perfect-foresight benchmark of a non-Sharpe objective being the wrong ceiling, which Salcher et al. (2026b) note for their diversification rules too. Against the error bar of Equation 25.2 that Section 25.3 attaches to each realized Sharpe ratio, the tangency portfolio’s shortfall is 1.9 standard errors and every other strategy’s less than one. Section 27.1 asks whether a forecast trained on the RAFE rather than the RMSE narrows the one shortfall that is, and Section 28.1 asks what to do when the error is in the model rather than in its parameters.

Key Takeaways

  • For a mean-variance investor the cost of a wrong mean forecast is an identity: the certainty-equivalent loss is \(\text{RAFE}^2/(2\gamma)\), the squared Mahalanobis distance between forecast and truth under \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) (Salcher et al. 2026b, 2026a).
  • The Sharpe-ratio shortfall is \(\theta(1 - \cos\varphi)\), bounded by \(c_0\,\text{RAFE}\); the RMSE is the RAFE with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \boldsymbol{I}\). For the unconstrained plug-in the RAFE correlates 0.55 with the realized shortfall and the RMSE 0.09.
  • For a long-only position the measure that explains the shortfall is the RAFE in the covariance metric the position was built in; whitened by the realized covariance matrix it falls below the RMSE for two of the three forecasts.
  • The covariance channel has three terms, Equation 26.10: the mean’s noise \(N/T\), the inversion error on the signal, and the amplification of the noise by the inverse. In certainty-equivalent units it is the larger channel at thirty-six and sixty months; in Sharpe units the mean channel is the larger at every window, which is Chopra and Ziemba (1993)’s reading.
  • A forecast with out-of-sample \(R^2\) allows at most \(\theta_c^2 = (S_0^2 + R^2)/(1 - R^2)\). For the term spread on the equal-weight policy the bound allows 0.040 of Sharpe ratio and the tilt took 0.029.
  • The register’s shortfalls follow the order of estimation at both ends, zero for equal weighting and 0.42 for the tangency portfolio, with minimum CVaR the exception, whose perfect-foresight benchmark minimizes a tail rather than maximizes a Sharpe ratio.
