27  Decision-Aware Forecasting

Should a return forecaster be trained on mean squared error at all? The investor who uses the forecast does not pay for its error in squared percentage points but in the units of the decision it feeds, and Stöckl and Hanke (2026) show that for a mean-variance investor those units are known exactly: the certainty equivalent lost to a forecast error is the error measured in risk units, so the forecast can be corrected in those units after the fact, with an intensity that follows from the number of assets and the length of the sample alone. The general form of the question is the operations-research one, where Elmachtoub and Grigas (2022) propose training a predictor on the regret of the decision it induces rather than on its own accuracy; the finance case is the one in which that regret has a closed form.

This chapter sits on neither side of the horizon axis of Section 3.1: it is about estimation, and the same corrections apply to a long-run mean and to a conditional one. We first derive the mismatch between the statistical loss and the decision loss and three answers to it, then implement each on the sixteen class series in excess of the bill, scored out of sample from the first month of model year 11, and finally ask which of the three buys most on this market and where each fails.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return
Re <- R - rf
stopifnot(ncol(Re) == 16L, !anyNA(Re))
N <- ncol(Re); Tn <- nrow(Re); dates <- zoo::index(R)
rf_by_month <- setNames(rf, as.character(dates))
mu <- colMeans(Re); S <- stats::cov(Re)
gamma <- 5

27.1 Two Losses for One Forecast

27.1.1 The statistical loss and the decision loss

Let \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) be the vector of expected excess returns on \(N\) assets, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) their covariance matrix, and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) a forecast of \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) from any source, a sample mean or the predictive regression of Section 16.1. A forecaster is trained and judged on the statistical loss

\[ L_{\text{stat}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}) = (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})'(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}), \tag{27.1}\]

the squared error summed over assets. The investor of Section 3.1 with risk aversion \(\gamma\) maximizes the certainty equivalent \(CE(\boldsymbol{w}) = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\), attains \(CE^* = \boldsymbol{\mu}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}/(2\gamma)\) at \(\boldsymbol{w}^* = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}/\gamma\), and holds the plug-in position \(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}/\gamma\) when handed the forecast. Expanding \(CE(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}) = \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}/\gamma - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}/(2\gamma)\) and subtracting it from \(CE^*\) leaves a perfect square:

\[ CE^* - CE(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}) = \frac{1}{2\gamma}\, (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \frac{\text{RAFE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^2}{2\gamma}. \tag{27.2}\]

The quadratic form is the squared risk-adjusted forecast error of Salcher et al. (2026b), and Section 26.1 derives it and the bound it places on the Sharpe-ratio gap; we take it from there. In the language of Elmachtoub and Grigas (2022), Equation 27.2 is the regret of the mean-variance decision, and their proposal to train on regret instead of on accuracy is, for this decision, the proposal to train on Equation 27.2 instead of on Equation 27.1.

The two losses are quadratic forms in the same error under two metrics, the identity and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\), and they rank forecasts differently. In the eigenbasis of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_N\) and eigenvectors \(\boldsymbol{u}_j\), the error \(\boldsymbol{e} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}\) has \(L_{\text{stat}} = \sum_j (\boldsymbol{u}_j'\boldsymbol{e})^2\) while \(2\gamma\) times the decision loss is \(\sum_j (\boldsymbol{u}_j'\boldsymbol{e})^2/\lambda_j\): the same squared error costs \(\lambda_1/\lambda_N\) times more in the least volatile direction of the universe than in the most volatile one, and that ratio is the condition number Section 7.1 measured.

ev <- eigen(S, symmetric = TRUE)
unit_cost <- function(u) as.numeric(t(u) %*% solve(S, u))   # 2 gamma times the decision loss of a unit error
cost_top <- unit_cost(ev$vectors[, 1]); cost_bottom <- unit_cost(ev$vectors[, N])
c(top = cost_top, bottom = cost_bottom, log10_ratio = log10(cost_bottom / cost_top))
#>         top      bottom log10_ratio 
#>      91.079 1158322.436       4.104

On the sixteen classes a unit error along the first eigenvector costs 91 in the units of Equation 27.2 and the same error along the last costs 1.2e+06, a ratio of \(10^{4.1}\). A forecaster that minimizes Equation 27.1 spends its accuracy where the variance is large; the optimizer needs it where the variance is small, because \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\) places its largest weights there, the mechanism of Best and Grauer (1991) in Section 8.1. That a forecaster should be trained on the loss its user bears is an old point (Granger 1969; Elliott and Timmermann 2008); the mean-variance case adds that the loss is known in closed form. A forecaster trained on Equation 27.1 can be reconciled with a decision scored by Equation 27.2 in three places: after the forecast, by correcting its output in risk units and leaving the model alone, the post-optimal moment correction of Stöckl and Hanke (2026); between forecast and decision, by acting on fewer of its dimensions, which Salcher et al. (2026a) price; and inside the training loop, by replacing the loss with the certainty equivalent of the portfolio the forecast implies, the parametric policy of Section 18.1 taken one step further.

27.1.2 Correcting the mean in risk units

Take the setting of Kan and Zhou (2007): excess returns independent \(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) over \(T \geq N + 1\) months, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) the sample mean and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) the unbiased sample covariance matrix, so that \((T-1)\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) is Wishart and independent of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\). In risk units, with \(\boldsymbol{m} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{m}} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\), the forecast is \(\hat{\boldsymbol{m}} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{m}, \boldsymbol{I}/T)\) and its risk, the expected squared RAFE, is the trace of that covariance:

\[ \mathbb{E}\,\text{RAFE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^2 = \frac{N}{T}. \tag{27.3}\]

Each of the \(N\) dimensions costs \(1/T\), the variance of an estimated Sharpe ratio, whatever signal it carries; this \(N/T\) is what inflates the promised Sharpe ratio in Equation 8.5.

The correction is Stein estimation in these units. Fix a target in advance as \(\boldsymbol{P}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) with \(\boldsymbol{P}\) idempotent of rank \(r\); the two targets used here are zero, \(\boldsymbol{P} = \boldsymbol{0}\), and the grand mean, \(\boldsymbol{P} = \boldsymbol{\iota}\boldsymbol{\iota}'/N\), \(\boldsymbol{P}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} = \bar{\mu}\boldsymbol{\iota}\) with \(\bar{\mu}\) the average of the sixteen sample means. Whiten the forecast with any square root of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\), shrink its residual from the target by a James-Stein factor, and map back. Stöckl and Hanke (2026) show that the square root cancels and the estimated covariance matrix enters through one scalar, the sample Mahalanobis length of the residual:

\[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c = \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \frac{c}{T}\,\frac{(\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{P})\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\hat{q}}, \qquad \hat{q} = \big((\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{P})\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\big)'\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\big((\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{P})\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\big). \tag{27.4}\]

For the grand mean this reads \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c = \bar{\mu}\boldsymbol{\iota} + \big(1 - c/(T\hat{q})\big)(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \bar{\mu}\boldsymbol{\iota})\): the deviation of each sample mean from the average is scaled down by a factor that depends on how long that deviation is in estimated risk units. The risk follows from two facts. First, with \(q\) the same quadratic form under the true \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), the ratio \(\psi = q/\hat{q}\) satisfies \((T-1)\psi \sim \chi^2_{T-N}\) and is independent of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\), a property of the Wishart distribution, so the feasible corrector is the oracle corrector with its constant randomly rescaled to \(c\psi\). Second, conditional on \(\psi\), the identity of Stein (1981) turns the cross term between the error and the shrinkage direction into the divergence of that direction, which for an idempotent \(\boldsymbol{R} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\) is \((\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{R} - 2)/\|\boldsymbol{R}\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}\|^2\) with \(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{R} = N - r\). Taking the expectation over \(\psi\) gives the exact risk,

\[ \mathbb{E}\,\text{RAFE}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c)^2 = \frac{N}{T} - \frac{\tau_P}{T^2}\Big[2c\,(N - r - 2)\,\mathbb{E}\psi - c^2\,\mathbb{E}\psi^2\Big], \quad \mathbb{E}\psi = \frac{T-N}{T-1},\; \mathbb{E}\psi^2 = \frac{(T-N)(T-N+2)}{(T-1)^2}, \tag{27.5}\]

where \(\tau_P = \mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{R}\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}\|^{-2}\) is finite whenever \(N - r \geq 3\). Everything about \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) that matters is in the two moments of \(\psi\), and they depend on \(N\) and \(T\) only. The bracket is a concave quadratic in \(c\), positive on \(0 < c < 2(N-r-2)(T-1)/(T-N+2)\) and largest at

\[ c_f = (N - r - 2)\,\frac{T-1}{T-N+2}, \tag{27.6}\]

a function of \(N\), \(T\) and the target’s rank alone: no tuning, no validation window. It exceeds the oracle constant \(N - r - 2\) because \(\psi\) has mean below one, so the feasible corrector shrinks less at the same \(c\). For the grand mean the risk is below \(N/T\) for every \(N \geq 4\) and \(T \geq N + 1\): the corrected mean dominates the sample mean, the input of every plug-in rule in this book, whenever the sample is longer than the number of assets.

The lineage matters because the result is easy to mistake for an old one. Stein (1956) showed that the sample mean of three or more normal means is inadmissible; James and Stein (1961) gave the estimator and, for an unknown covariance matrix, the constant \((N-2)/(T-N+2)\), which is Equation 27.6 with \(r = 0\) divided by \(T - 1\); Efron and Morris (1975) moved the target to the grand mean; and Jorion (1986) brought the idea into portfolio choice as the Bayes-Stein estimator of Section 6.1, whose target is the expected return of the minimum-variance portfolio. What Equation 27.5 adds is that the target projection need not be orthogonal in risk units: the grand mean’s is symmetric only if \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\iota} \propto \boldsymbol{\iota}\), which no covariance matrix with a 4.4-fold spread of volatilities comes near, and the proof uses only \(\boldsymbol{R}^2 = \boldsymbol{R}\) and \(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{R} = N - r\). The Bayes-Stein target depends on \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\), and the theorem does not cover it. Two further things it says. The zero target rescales the forecast and leaves the direction of the plug-in position unchanged, so its gain is in the certainty equivalent and not in the Sharpe ratio; a target of rank one moves the direction. And the theorem ranks the corrected mean against the sample mean, not against rules that use no mean, such as \(1/N\) or minimum variance, whose gap to the optimum is a population quantity rather than an estimation error.

27.1.3 Using less of the forecast

The second answer keeps the forecast and acts on fewer of its dimensions. Salcher et al. (2026a) start from the same identity. With \(\boldsymbol{a} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\mu}\) the truth in risk units and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{b}} = \boldsymbol{a} + \boldsymbol{\eta}\) the forecast, an investor who acts only on the projection \(\boldsymbol{Q}\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}\) onto a \(k\)-dimensional subspace loses \(\|(\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{Q})\boldsymbol{a}\|^2 + \|\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\eta}\|^2\) on every path, because the cross term carries \((\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{Q})\boldsymbol{Q} = \boldsymbol{0}\); in expectation, with the isotropic error of the sample mean,

\[ 2\gamma\,\mathbb{E}[\text{loss}] = \underbrace{\|(\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{Q})\boldsymbol{a}\|^2}_{\text{signal discarded}} + \underbrace{\frac{k}{T}}_{\text{noise retained}} . \tag{27.7}\]

A dimension is worth acting on if and only if its squared Sharpe ratio exceeds \(1/T\), the squared standard error of an estimated Sharpe ratio (Jobson and Korkie 1981; Lo 2002). Shrinkage of the kind in Equation 27.4, or the two-fund scalar of Kan and Zhou (2007) and the combination with \(1/N\) of Tu and Zhou (2011), is a soft reduction: it pays the price on every one of the \(N\) dimensions whether or not the dimension carries signal, and loses to a hard reduction whenever the signal is concentrated on a few. The familiar rules are fixed cells of the grid spanned by the number of covariance dimensions estimated and the number of forecast dimensions acted on: the plug-in rule of Section 8.1 keeps \(N\) and \(N\), minimum variance the level alone inside a basis of \(N\), \(1/N\) of DeMiguel et al. (2009) one and one, the decile sort of Fama and French (1993) the sign of the ranking and no covariance matrix, the parametric tilt of Brandt et al. (2009) a slope inside a basis of two, and covariance shrinkage (Ledoit and Wolf 2004) every forecast dimension with the other side reduced. None sets its granularity from what the forecast can deliver.

The cell that moves does. Order the assets by the forecast, let \(\rho\) be the cross-sectional rank correlation between the forecast and the next month’s return in risk units, the information coefficient of Grinold (1989), and suppose the true profile is linear in the rank. Then the slope of that profile in risk units is \(\tau^2 = \rho^2/(1 - \rho^2)\), cutting the ranking into \(G\) equal groups and acting on the group averages discards about \(N\tau^2/G^2\) of signal and retains \(G/T\) of noise, and the loss is minimized at

\[ G^* = \left(\frac{2\,N\,T\,\rho^2}{1 - \rho^2}\right)^{1/3}. \tag{27.8}\]

The cube root is the rate Cattaneo et al. (2020) derive for the number of characteristic-sorted portfolios, but the price per group here is \(1/T\) regardless of how many assets the group averages, because averaging lowers the noise of a portfolio’s mean and its risk in the same proportion; \(G^*\) is therefore small, and it falls to one group, which is \(1/N\), when the forecast cannot rank.

27.1.4 The decision loss inside the training loop

The third answer changes the loss the forecaster is trained on. Let \(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_t)\) be a forecaster with parameters \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) and conditioning variables \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\), and let \(\boldsymbol{w}(\cdot)\) be the map from a forecast to a weight vector: the plug-in map, or the clipped tilt of Equation 16.9. The decision-aware estimator maximizes the sample certainty equivalent of the portfolio the forecast implies,

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\; \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\Big[r_{p,t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,r_{p,t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^2\Big], \qquad r_{p,t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \boldsymbol{w}\big(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_t)\big)'\boldsymbol{r}_{t+1}, \tag{27.9}\]

and the parametric policy of Brandt et al. (2009) in Equation 18.2 is the special case in which the forecast is the weight itself, \(\boldsymbol{w}(f) = f\); Bertsimas and Kallus (2020) call this prescriptive rather than predictive analytics, and Elmachtoub and Grigas (2022) give the loss its name. The connection to the first answer is exact in one case. With the plug-in map, a known \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) and \(\boldsymbol{r}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_t + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t+1}\), the expected certainty equivalent of month \(t\) is \([\boldsymbol{\mu}_t'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_t - (f_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}_t)'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(f_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}_t)]/(2\gamma)\) by the algebra of Equation 27.2, so that

\[ \max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\;\mathbb{E}\,CE \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\;\mathbb{E}\,\big(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_t) - \boldsymbol{\mu}_t\big)'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\big(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_t) - \boldsymbol{\mu}_t\big): \tag{27.10}\]

the decision-trained forecaster is the least-squares forecaster with its errors reweighted by \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\), generalized least squares in the metric the optimizer uses. That is also why the third answer generalizes beyond mean-variance: nothing in Equation 27.9 uses the quadratic form. Replace the certainty equivalent by any utility, the plug-in map by any map with clipping, constraints or costs, and the forecaster by any model of Gu et al. (2020), and only the closed form of Equation 27.10 is lost.

What is also lost is precision. A certainty equivalent realized over \(T\) months carries the noise of a Sharpe ratio, whose standard error is of order \(\sqrt{(1 + SR^2/2)/T}\) (Lo 2002), and a tuning parameter chosen by that criterion is a further estimated quantity whose cost none of the displays above contains; Salcher et al. (2026a) make the same point for a number of groups chosen from the window. Each answer therefore comes with the condition under which it does not help. The correction of Equation 27.4 drops every deviation from the target when \(c_f \geq T\hat{q}\), which happens when \(T\) is small relative to \(N\); the corrected mean is then the grand mean, the tangency rule becomes minimum variance, and the correction cannot do better than a rule that never looked at the mean. The rule of Equation 27.8 returns one group when the forecast cannot rank, and buys nothing when the forecast’s cross-sectional content is small to begin with. And the loop of Equation 27.9 can choose worse on both counts when the certainty equivalent it tunes on is noisier than the gain it chases. One of the three partially falsifies its own prediction on this market, and the implementation says which.

27.2 Three Corrections on Sixteen Classes

27.2.1 The corrected mean in a few lines

Equation 27.4 and Equation 27.6 are a function of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\), \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\), \(N\), \(T\) and the target, with the positive part of the factor taken as implementations do.

correct_mean <- function(R, target = c("grand", "zero"), c = NULL) {
  target <- match.arg(target)
  X <- zoo::coredata(R); T <- nrow(X); N <- ncol(X)
  mu <- colMeans(X); S <- stats::cov(X)
  r   <- if (target == "grand") 1L else 0L                 # rank of the target projection
  Pmu <- if (target == "grand") rep(mean(mu), N) else rep(0, N)
  d   <- mu - Pmu                                          # (I - P) mu_hat
  q   <- as.numeric(t(d) %*% solve(S, d))                  # its length in estimated risk units
  if (is.null(c)) c <- (N - r - 2) * (T - 1) / (T - N + 2) # c_f of @eq-27-constant
  h   <- max(1 - c / (T * q), 0)                           # the James-Stein factor, positive part
  list(mu = setNames(Pmu + h * d, colnames(X)), factor = h, c = c, q = q, N = N, T = T)
}

cm_full <- correct_mean(Re)
round(c(c_f = cm_full$c, Tq = cm_full$T * cm_full$q, factor = cm_full$factor), 4)
#>     c_f      Tq  factor 
#> 13.4721 22.8625  0.4107

On the full sample of 372 months, Equation 27.6 with \(N = 16\) and \(r = 1\) is

\[ c_f = (16 - 1 - 2)\,\frac{371}{358} = 13.47, \qquad 1 - \frac{c_f}{T\hat{q}} = 1 - \frac{13.47}{22.86} = 0.411, \]

so that even on thirty-one model years the deviation of each sample mean from the grand mean is kept at 41% of its size. The Bayes-Stein estimator of Section 6.2 is the natural comparison and is copied here unchanged.

bayes_stein <- function(R) {
  X <- zoo::coredata(R); T <- nrow(X); N <- ncol(X)
  mu   <- colMeans(X)
  S    <- stats::cov(X) * (T - 1) / (T - N - 2)   # Jorion's unbiased scaling
  Sinv <- solve(S); iota <- rep(1, N)
  mu0  <- as.numeric(t(iota) %*% Sinv %*% mu) / as.numeric(t(iota) %*% Sinv %*% iota)
  d    <- mu - mu0
  v    <- (N + 2) / ((N + 2) + T * as.numeric(t(d) %*% Sinv %*% d))
  list(mu = setNames((1 - v) * mu + v * mu0, colnames(X)), mu0 = mu0, v = v)
}
bs_full <- bayes_stein(Re)

Bayes-Stein shrinks 53% of the way toward the minimum-variance mean, the corrected mean 59% of the way toward the grand mean, and only the second has the exact risk of Equation 27.5 behind it; exercise 2 checks it by simulation.

The constant falls with \(T\) toward the oracle value \(N - 3 = 13\), and at \(T = 36\) it is 1.59 times that value: the shorter the window, the further \(\psi\) sits below one. The factor applied in practice is small at every window, between 0.23 and 0.41 on average, and on 22% of the sixty-month windows the positive part binds and every deviation from the grand mean is removed.

27.2.2 Four means in the tangency rule

The correction is scored where Section 8.2 scored the sample mean: the long-only tangency portfolio on rolling windows, as the quadratic program of that chapter, so that the register of Section 25.3 is reproduced, with the same estimated covariance matrix for every mean.

gmv_qp <- function(S) {
  n <- ncol(S)
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S * 1e4, dvec = rep(0, n),
                            Amat = cbind(rep(1, n), diag(n)), bvec = c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)
  w <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  setNames(w / sum(w), colnames(S))
}
tan_qp <- function(mu, S) {
  n <- length(mu)
  if (max(mu) <= 0) return(structure(gmv_qp(S), fallback = TRUE))
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S * 1e4, dvec = rep(0, n),
                            Amat = cbind(mu * 1e2, diag(n)), bvec = c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)
  y <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  structure(setNames(y / sum(y), names(mu)), fallback = FALSE)
}
# The register's Sharpe ratio: compounded, in excess of the bill. The certainty
# equivalent is annualized from monthly excess returns at the chapter's gamma.
sharpe_realized <- function(r, rf) {
  n <- length(r)
  (prod(1 + r)^(12 / n) - prod(1 + rf)^(12 / n)) / (stats::sd(r) * sqrt(12))
}
ce_annual <- function(e) 12 * mean(e) - gamma / 2 * 12 * stats::var(e)

oos <- seq.int(121L, Tn)                        # the months the register scores
stopifnot(model_year(dates[oos[1]]) == 11L, model_year(dates[120]) == 10L)

Each window supplies five fully invested rules: the tangency portfolio on the sample mean, on Bayes-Stein and on the corrected mean, equal weighting and minimum variance. One assertion inside the loop states the theory’s claim about the zero target: its unconstrained position is a scalar multiple of the sample mean’s in every window, so the long-only tangency rule, which is invariant to that scalar, cannot see it.

run_rules <- function(win) {
  lapply(oos, function(m) {
    X  <- Re[(m - win):(m - 1L), ]
    Xm <- zoo::coredata(X); V <- stats::cov(Xm); mh <- colMeans(Xm)
    cm_g <- correct_mean(X, "grand"); cm_0 <- correct_mean(X, "zero"); bs <- bayes_stein(X)
    stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(solve(V, cm_0$mu), cm_0$factor * solve(V, mh))))   # same direction
    means <- list(sample = mh, bayes_stein = bs$mu, corrected = cm_g$mu)
    rules <- c(lapply(means, tan_qp, S = V),
               list(equal_weight = setNames(rep(1 / N, N), colnames(Re)), min_variance = gmv_qp(V)))
    stopifnot(all(vapply(rules, function(w) identical(names(w), colnames(Re)), logical(1))))
    tibble::tibble(
      date = dates[m], rule = names(rules), factor = cm_g$factor,
      total  = vapply(rules, function(w) sum(w * zoo::coredata(R)[m, ]), numeric(1)),
      excess = vapply(rules, function(w) sum(w * zoo::coredata(Re)[m, ]), numeric(1)))
  }) |> dplyr::bind_rows() |> dplyr::mutate(window = win)
}
roll <- dplyr::bind_rows(run_rules(60L), run_rules(120L))

27.2.3 Granularity on the term-spread tilt

The second answer needs a forecast with a cross-section, and the one forecast in Section 16.3 that survived out of sample is the term spread, rebuilt here exactly as there: the US slope read off the weekly curve at each month end, lagged once, and an expanding regression of each series’ excess return on it from the first month of model year 11. The other six predictors of that chapter’s elastic net are built beside it for the third answer.

The forecast becomes a weight through the tilt of Equation 16.9, with the same band \(\delta = 1/(2N)\) and scale, and the reduction is inserted where Equation 27.7 says it belongs: after the forecast has been put into volatility units and demeaned, before it is clipped. Four cells come from one function: every magnitude is chapter 16’s tilt; three rank groups replaces each forecast by the average of its tercile; the sign keeps top minus bottom at the largest tilt the band allows, the long-short sort of Fama and French (1993) around the policy; equal weighting discards the forecast.

vol60 <- zoo::rollapply(Re, 60, stats::sd, align = "right", fill = NA)
band  <- 0.5 / N
kappa <- band / (0.5 / sqrt(12))
w_policy <- setNames(rep(1 / N, N), colnames(Re))

tilt_from <- function(F, idx, reduce = identity) {
  stopifnot(identical(colnames(F), colnames(Re)), nrow(F) == length(idx))
  W <- t(vapply(seq_along(idx), function(i) {
    m  <- idx[i]
    s  <- F[i, ] / as.numeric(vol60[m - 1L, ])   # volatility units, known at m - 1
    s  <- reduce(s - mean(s))                    # relative view, then the reduction
    tl <- pmin(pmax(kappa * s, -band), band)
    w  <- w_policy + tl - mean(tl); w <- pmax(w, 0); w / sum(w)
  }, numeric(N)))
  dimnames(W) <- list(as.character(dates[idx]), colnames(Re))
  stopifnot(all(abs(rowSums(W) - 1) < 1e-12), all(W >= 0))
  W
}
steps <- function(G) function(s) {                         # group averages
  if (G <= 1L) return(s * 0)
  stats::ave(s, ceiling(rank(s, ties.method = "first") * G / length(s)))
}
sign_only <- function(s) {                                 # top third +band, bottom third -band
  g <- ceiling(rank(s, ties.method = "first") * 3 / length(s))
  (band / kappa) * (as.numeric(g == 3L) - as.numeric(g == 1L))
}

cost_rate <- pm_costs_default()[sub(".*_", "", colnames(Re))]
stopifnot(!anyNA(cost_rate)); names(cost_rate) <- colnames(Re)
score_W <- function(W, idx) {
  Rm <- zoo::coredata(R)[idx, colnames(Re), drop = FALSE]
  Em <- zoo::coredata(Re)[idx, colnames(Re), drop = FALSE]
  stopifnot(identical(colnames(W), colnames(Re)), nrow(W) == nrow(Rm))
  r <- rowSums(W * Rm); e <- rowSums(W * Em)
  drifted <- W * (1 + Rm) / (1 + r)
  trade   <- abs(W[-1, , drop = FALSE] - drifted[-nrow(W), , drop = FALSE])
  cost    <- c(as.numeric(trade %*% cost_rate), 0)
  c(sharpe = sharpe_realized(r, rf_by_month[as.character(dates[idx])]),
    ce = ce_annual(e), ce_net = ce_annual(e - cost),
    turnover = 12 * mean(c(rowSums(trade) / 2, 0)))
}

The cell that moves needs the information coefficient. At each out-of-sample month the regression is fit on the months before it, its fitted values are ranked against the realized returns of those months, both in volatility units, and the Spearman correlations are averaged: the ranking ability an investor standing at that month could measure. Equation 27.8 then gives the number of groups, rounded and capped at 8.

ic_of <- function(m) {
  tr <- seq.int(61L, m - 1L)
  Fhat <- vapply(colnames(Re), function(s) {
    d  <- pairs_of(s); ok <- which(!is.na(d$term) & seq_len(Tn) < m)
    b  <- stats::lm.fit(cbind(1, d$term[ok]), d$y[ok])$coefficients
    b[1] + b[2] * d$term[tr]
  }, numeric(length(tr)))
  V60 <- zoo::coredata(vol60)[tr - 1L, ]; Y60 <- zoo::coredata(Re)[tr, ]
  mean(vapply(seq_along(tr), function(i)
    stats::cor(Fhat[i, ] / V60[i, ], Y60[i, ] / V60[i, ], method = "spearman"), numeric(1)))
}

The forecast cells all stand above equal weighting, by 0.017 to 0.031 in Sharpe ratio and by 13 to 25 basis points of certainty equivalent net of costs, the order of gain Section 16.3 found. Among themselves they are indistinguishable: the spread across the four forecast cells is 0.013 in Sharpe ratio and 9 basis points of certainty equivalent, against a standard error of the Sharpe ratio over these months of about 0.22 by the formula of Lo (2002). The information coefficient on the estimation windows averages 0.110, so Equation 27.8 asks for 4 to 5 groups, and the rule’s cell earns 0.644, inside the same spread. The coefficient realized on the forecasts actually made is 0.030, lower than on any estimation window, and at that value the formula gives 1.9 groups: the number read from fitted values overstates what the forecast delivers, at no visible cost here because nothing in the granularity has one. The sign cell trades 37% a year against 21% for the full vector and still matches it after costs. This is the second condition of the theory: a forecast whose cross-sectional content is a near-common shift, the term spread lifting every bond series together, leaves Equation 27.7 little to discard and little to retain, and the band of Equation 16.9, not the granularity, sets what the forecast is worth.

27.2.4 Tuning the elastic net on the certainty equivalent

The third answer is tried on the forecaster of Section 16.2 that lost: the elastic net of Zou and Hastie (2005) on seven predictors, which chose its penalty by five-fold cross-validated squared error on contiguous blocks, re-tuned once a year. Here the same folds and grid of penalties serve two criteria: the pooled out-of-fold squared error across the sixteen series, the loss of Equation 27.1, and the certainty equivalent of the tilt of Equation 16.9 built from the out-of-fold forecasts over the training months, the loss of Equation 27.9 with the tilt as the map. The penalty is the only parameter the loop chooses; the coefficients are refit inside glmnet at whichever penalty each criterion selects.

en_vars  <- c("mom", "rev", "term", "ig", "hy", "level", "turb")
D  <- lapply(colnames(Re), pairs_of); names(D) <- colnames(Re)
ok <- stats::complete.cases(D[[1]][, en_vars])
stopifnot(all(vapply(D, function(d) identical(stats::complete.cases(d[, en_vars]), ok), logical(1))))
lam_grid <- 10^seq(-1, -5, length.out = 33)     # decreasing, as glmnet expects
Y <- zoo::coredata(Re)

tune_both <- function(m) {
  tr   <- which(ok & seq_len(Tn) < m)
  fold <- ceiling(seq_along(tr) / (length(tr) / 5))
  oof  <- array(NA_real_, c(length(tr), N, length(lam_grid)))
  for (j in seq_len(N)) {
    X <- as.matrix(D[[j]][tr, en_vars]); y <- D[[j]]$y[tr]
    for (f in 1:5) {
      i   <- fold != f
      fit <- glmnet::glmnet(X[i, ], y[i], alpha = 0.5, lambda = lam_grid)
      oof[!i, j, ] <- as.matrix(stats::predict(fit, newx = X[!i, , drop = FALSE], s = lam_grid))
    }
  }
  mse <- vapply(seq_along(lam_grid), function(k) mean((oof[, , k] - Y[tr, ])^2), numeric(1))
  ce  <- vapply(seq_along(lam_grid), function(k) {
    F <- oof[, , k]; colnames(F) <- colnames(Re)
    ce_annual(rowSums(tilt_from(F, tr) * Y[tr, ]))
  }, numeric(1))
  list(lam_mse = lam_grid[which.min(mse)], lam_ce = lam_grid[which.max(ce)],
       null_mse = which.min(mse) == 1L)     # TRUE when the null model minimizes the CV error
}

The certainty-equivalent criterion selects a smaller penalty in 17 of the 21 re-tunings and wanders over 4 orders of magnitude from one year to the next, which is what a criterion estimated with the noise of a Sharpe ratio looks like when the surface it searches is flat.

The forecast tuned on the certainty equivalent has the worse mean squared error, an out-of-sample \(R^2\) of -7.7% against the expanding mean where the squared-error forecast has -0.5%, and the better portfolio: a Sharpe ratio of 0.717 against 0.597 and a certainty equivalent net of costs of 4.41% against 3.53%, 88 basis points a year. Two qualifications belong beside the number: the decision-trained net turns the portfolio over 87% a year, 7 times the 13% of the squared-error net, because the penalty it chooses lets the forecasts move; and the Sharpe-ratio advantage of 0.120 is inside the standard error of 0.22. The prediction is confirmed in sign and not established in significance, which on 252 months is as much as any forecast comparison in this book can say.

27.3 What Each Answer Buys

Figure 27.1 puts the three answers on one axis with the register of Section 25.3. In the Sharpe ratio added to the rule it corrects, the first answer buys most: the corrected mean lifts the sixty-month tangency portfolio by 0.18, from the register’s 0.546 to 0.728, at 120 months by 0.20, and the corrected tangency portfolio is the best forecast-based rule in the figure. The third answer buys 0.12 over the same forecaster tuned on squared error, which makes the decision-trained net the best of the tilts at 0.717, 67 basis points of net certainty equivalent above equal weighting. The second answer buys the 0.03 that the term spread was already worth in Section 16.3 and nothing beyond it. In certainty equivalent the first two change places: the training loop adds 88 basis points net to its forecaster where the corrected mean adds 43 to a fully invested tangency rule whose length is not its own to choose.

Each fails somewhere. The corrected mean does not beat the rules that use no mean: the register’s minimum variance stands at 0.829 and its best line, Minimum CVaR, at 0.893, above every forecast-based rule in the figure, and on 27% of the sixty-month windows the correction concedes the point by collapsing onto minimum variance itself; the covariance channel of Equation 8.6 it leaves untouched is where that rule’s advantage lives. Granularity fails to matter: four ways of acting on the term spread land within 0.013 of one another, the rule’s choice of 4 to 5 groups rests on an information coefficient measured in sample that the realized forecasts do not reach, and with sixteen assets and a band of 3.1 percentage points there is too little cross-section for Equation 27.7 to decide anything. And the training loop, the answer that generalizes furthest, is the one whose own prediction is only partially borne out: it delivers a forecast with a worse squared error and a better portfolio, but it chooses its penalty from a criterion so noisy that the choice moves by orders of magnitude between years, trades 7 times as much, and leaves a Sharpe-ratio gain the sample cannot distinguish from zero. The decision loss is the right teacher and a far noisier one than squared error.

For the plug-in rules of Section 9.3 and Section 8.3 the reading is short. The sixty-month tangency portfolio’s 0.546 was never the price of using an estimated mean; it was the price of using the sample mean, and Equation 27.4 recovers 64% of the distance to minimum variance without touching the covariance matrix, with a constant that needed no tuning. What remains is the covariance matrix’s share, which Section 26.1 separates from the mean’s, and the part no estimator can recover: on this market the level direction that minimum variance trades carries most of what there is to earn, and the decision-aware forecaster changes which loss that arithmetic is done in, not the arithmetic. Section 28.1 asks what to do when the model behind the loss is itself in doubt, and the resampled frontiers of Section 28.3 put the same estimation error back on the picture of Section 9.3.

Key Takeaways

  • A mean-variance investor pays for forecast error in the metric \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\), not the identity (Equation 27.2): on the sixteen classes the same squared error costs \(10^{4.1}\) times more in the least volatile direction than in the most (Salcher et al. 2026b; Elmachtoub and Grigas 2022).
  • Shrinking the sample mean toward the grand mean in estimated risk units has an exact risk; the best constant is \((N - 3)(T - 1)/(T - N + 2)\), a function of \(N\) and \(T\) alone, and the corrected mean dominates the sample mean whenever \(T > N\) (Stöckl and Hanke 2026; James and Stein 1961; Stein 1981). On sixty-month windows it lifts the tangency portfolio’s Sharpe ratio from 0.55 to 0.73 and collapses onto minimum variance, which stays ahead, in 27% of the windows.
  • Every dimension of a forecast acted on costs \(1/T\) whatever it carries, so the familiar rules are cells of a granularity grid and the number of rank groups worth forming is \((2NT\rho^2/(1 - \rho^2))^{1/3}\) from the information coefficient (Salcher et al. 2026a; Cattaneo et al. 2020). On the term spread every granularity earns the same few basis points: the band of the tilt, not its resolution, sets the value.
  • Tuning the elastic net’s penalty on the certainty equivalent of the tilt it implies instead of on squared error gives a forecast with a worse \(R^2_{\text{OS}}\) and a better portfolio, 88 basis points net a year, inside the sampling error of the Sharpe ratio and at 7 times the turnover (Brandt et al. 2009; Bertsimas and Kallus 2020).
  • None of the three answers beats the register’s rules that use no mean. The correction prices the mean channel; the covariance channel of Equation 8.6 is where the rest of the plug-in rule’s loss lives.
