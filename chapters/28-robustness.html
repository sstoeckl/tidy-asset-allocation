28  Robustness and Model Uncertainty

What should an investor do when the doubt is not about the values of \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) but about whether the model that produced them is the right one? Garlappi et al. (2007) posed that question for the mean-variance investor and answered it with the decision rule of Gilboa and Schmeidler (1989): hold on to every model the data cannot rule out, and choose the portfolio whose worst case over that set is best. The answer turns out to be a shrinkage toward the minimum-variance portfolio, which places the whole of this chapter on the strategic axis of Section 3.1: a robust policy is still a policy, set once from unconditional beliefs and held, with the ambiguity about those beliefs built into the weights rather than into a signal.

We first separate parameter uncertainty, a distribution over the inputs that Section 8.1 and Section 26.1 price, from model uncertainty, a set of models with no distribution over it, and derive the multi-prior portfolio, the robust worst case over a Kullback–Leibler ball, and the resampled frontier; we then implement all four devices on the sixteen classes, including random portfolios as a picture of the feasible set rather than as the solver of Section 13.1; and we run the robust portfolios out of sample against the register.

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)   # 16 risky classes
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return                                  # the bill
Re <- R - rf                                                       # excess returns
stopifnot(ncol(Re) == 16L, !anyNA(Re))
N  <- ncol(Re); Tn <- nrow(Re); d <- zoo::index(R)
mu <- colMeans(Re); S <- stats::cov(Re)

28.1 Parameter Uncertainty and Model Uncertainty

28.1.1 Two kinds of not knowing

Every chapter since Section 6.1 has treated the inputs of Markowitz (1952) as estimates. A sample mean carries the standard error of Equation 6.1, a sample covariance the error Section 7.1 measures, and the plug-in rule of Equation 8.2 pays for both. Section 26.1 turns that error into a cost in the investor’s units and Section 27.1 corrects the forecast for the decision it feeds. All of this is parameter uncertainty: the model is taken as true, and what is unknown is a vector of numbers inside it, about which the investor holds a distribution — the sampling distribution of the estimator, or a posterior. The natural tool is then to average. The Bayesian investor maximizes expected utility under the predictive distribution, and for the mean-variance problem that is a shrinkage of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) toward a prior center with an intensity set by the posterior precision (Jorion 1986; Black and Litterman 1992), or, once the estimation risk in \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) is admitted, a scaled-down position in the risky assets (Kan and Zhou 2007).

Model uncertainty is a different object. The investor entertains a set of models and has no distribution over the set: not because the prior is diffuse, but because there is no prior. Knight (1921) separated the two as risk and uncertainty, and Ellsberg (1961) showed experimentally that people treat them differently, preferring a bet with known odds to one with unknown odds even when no probability assignment makes the second worse. Averaging over the set requires exactly the weights the investor does not have. The rule Gilboa and Schmeidler (1989) derived from axioms replaces the average with the worst case: evaluate each portfolio by the lowest expected utility any model in the set assigns to it, and pick the portfolio that does best under that evaluation.

The two need different tools because they produce different objects: a distribution over the inputs has a mean, and a set of models has a boundary, on which the worst case sits. What follows derives what the boundary does to the weights.

28.1.2 The multi-prior portfolio

Garlappi et al. (2007) keep the covariance matrix and put the ambiguity in the mean. The set of models is a confidence ellipsoid around the sample mean,

\[ \max_{\boldsymbol{w}} \; \min_{\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)} \; \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}, \qquad \mathcal{E}(\varepsilon) = \left\{\boldsymbol{\mu} : (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \leq \varepsilon \right\}, \tag{28.1}\]

with \(\gamma\) the risk aversion of Equation 3.12. The inner problem has a closed form. Its Lagrangian in \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) has first-order condition \(\boldsymbol{w} = 2\lambda\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\), so the worst mean lies in the direction of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\), and substituting into the constraint, which binds, fixes the distance:

\[ \boldsymbol{\mu}^{*}(\boldsymbol{w}) = \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}}}\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}, \qquad \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*}(\boldsymbol{w}) = \boldsymbol{w}'\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}} . \tag{28.2}\]

Nature marks the portfolio’s expected return down by \(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\) standard deviations, and the outer problem becomes

\[ \max_{\boldsymbol{w}} \; \boldsymbol{w}'\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}} - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}} , \tag{28.3}\]

a mean-variance objective with a second, linear penalty on volatility. With a risk-free asset and no budget constraint the solution is proportional to \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\), as in Equation 9.5. Writing \(\boldsymbol{w} = k\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) and \(C = \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\), the objective is \(kC - \sqrt{\varepsilon C}\,k - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}k^2 C\), and the first-order condition gives

\[ \boldsymbol{w}^{*} = \frac{1}{\gamma} \left(1 - \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{C}}\right)^{+} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} . \tag{28.4}\]

Since \(\sqrt{C}\) is the maximum attainable Sharpe ratio of Equation 9.6, the ambiguity-averse investor holds the same risky portfolio as the plug-in investor and scales it down by one minus the ratio of \(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\) to that Sharpe ratio, holding nothing risky once the ellipsoid is wide enough to contain a mean at which the best Sharpe ratio is zero.

Fully invested, with \(\boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{w} = 1\), the budget constraint enters with a multiplier and the first-order condition reads \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - (\gamma + \sqrt{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} = \lambda\boldsymbol{\iota}\). Define the effective risk aversion \(\gamma^{*} = \gamma + \sqrt{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}}\). Solving for \(\boldsymbol{w}\) and eliminating \(\lambda\) with the budget gives, with \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) the scalars of Equation 9.6,

\[ \boldsymbol{w}^{*} = \boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mv}} + \frac{1}{\gamma^{*}}\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\!\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \frac{B}{A}\boldsymbol{\iota}\right), \qquad \gamma^{*} = \gamma + \frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}^{*}}} . \tag{28.5}\]

The robust portfolio is the ordinary mean-variance portfolio of an investor whose risk aversion has been raised from \(\gamma\) to \(\gamma^{*}\). The second term is the speculative component, which carries no net weight because \(\boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} - \tfrac{B}{A}\boldsymbol{\iota}) = B - B = 0\), and its shrinkage factor relative to the plug-in investor is \(\gamma/\gamma^{*} = 1 / (1 + \sqrt{\varepsilon}/(\gamma\sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}^{*}}))\). As \(\varepsilon \to \infty\) the factor goes to zero and \(\boldsymbol{w}^{*} \to \boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mv}}\): the investor who trusts nothing about the means holds the one portfolio of Equation 9.3 that needs none. Because \(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}^{*}}\) depends on the answer, Equation 28.5 is a fixed point. The two components are \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\)-orthogonal, so \(\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}^{*}} = 1/A + D/\gamma^{*2}\) with \(D = C - B^2/A\), and \(\gamma^{*}\) solves one scalar equation; under a long-only constraint the same iteration runs with a quadratic program in place of the closed form.

The size of the ellipsoid is a confidence level. Under normality, \(T(T-N)/((T-1)N)\) times the quadratic form in Equation 28.1 with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) replaced by \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) follows an \(F\) distribution with \(N\) and \(T-N\) degrees of freedom, so

\[ \varepsilon(p) = \frac{N\,(T-1)}{T\,(T-N)}\; F^{-1}_{N,\,T-N}(p) \tag{28.6}\]

is the radius of the ellipsoid that contains the true mean with probability \(p\). The set of models is a confidence region, which is a sampling object, and the radius grows with \(N\) and shrinks with \(T\) exactly as the standard error of Equation 6.1 does. The multi-prior portfolio is a parameter-uncertainty device that uses a worst case where the Bayesian uses an average, and the comparison with Equation 6.4 is direct: Bayes–Stein moves the estimate toward a common mean by a factor set by the posterior precision; Equation 28.5 moves the portfolio toward minimum variance by a factor set by the radius. Both end in the same place as the sample shrinks. The decision-aware corrections of Section 27.1 change the forecast so that the decision it feeds costs less; the multi-prior rule leaves the forecast alone and changes the decision.

28.1.3 Robust optimization over a set

The inner problem of Equation 28.1 is one instance of robust optimization: specify an uncertainty set for the inputs and optimize the worst case over it. Goldfarb and Iyengar (2003) define the set through a factor model, with intervals for the factor loadings and for the covariance of the factors, and show that the worst-case mean-variance problem is a second-order cone program, convex and solvable with the machinery of Section 13.1. Tütüncü and Koenig (2004) take box sets for \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) and solve the saddle point directly. The set does the work: a box is a per-asset haircut on every long position, an ellipsoid is the volatility penalty of Equation 28.3. The scenario appendix of Appendix H is the same idea applied to the factors of the modeled market rather than to its moments, and Appendix C records which of these formulations the package’s solvers can carry.

28.1.4 The Kullback–Leibler ball

A worst case over means leaves every other feature of the model intact. The robust control approach of Hansen and Sargent (2008) widens the set to every distribution within a fixed relative entropy of a reference model \(p\),

\[ \mathcal{Q}(\eta) = \left\{ q : \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p) = \sum_t q_t \log\frac{q_t}{p_t} \leq \eta \right\}, \tag{28.7}\]

so that an alternative model may move tails, correlations and skewness as well as means. For a portfolio \(\boldsymbol{w}\) with realized returns \(x_t = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{r}_t\) under \(p\), the worst case for the expected return is again a Lagrangian problem, now in the probabilities: minimizing \(\sum_t q_t x_t\) subject to the entropy constraint gives the first-order condition \(x_t + \tfrac{1}{\theta}(\log(q_t/p_t) + 1) = \lambda\), whose solution is the exponential tilt

\[ q_t^{*} = \frac{p_t\, e^{-\theta x_t}}{\sum_s p_s\, e^{-\theta x_s}}, \qquad \theta = \theta(\eta) \;\text{ such that }\; \mathrm{KL}(q^{*}\,\|\,p) = \eta . \tag{28.8}\]

Nature reweights the sample toward the months in which the portfolio did badly, with an intensity \(\theta\) that rises with the radius, and the worst-case mean vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_q = \sum_t q^{*}_t \boldsymbol{r}_t\) marks down most the assets whose bad months coincide with the portfolio’s. The same tilt expressed through the multiplier gives the multiplier preference of Anderson et al. (2003),

\[ \min_{q \in \mathcal{Q}(\eta)} \mathbb{E}_q[x] = \max_{\theta > 0}\left\{ -\frac{1}{\theta}\log \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[e^{-\theta x}\right] - \frac{\eta}{\theta} \right\}, \tag{28.9}\]

whose first term is the certainty equivalent of an investor with exponential utility and absolute risk aversion \(\theta\). Robustness and risk aversion are therefore nearly indistinguishable from outside, and Maenhout (2004) derives robust portfolio rules in which the preference for robustness enters exactly as Equation 28.5 found: as an increase in effective risk aversion. The two sets are related exactly when the reference model is Gaussian: the relative entropy between \(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) and \(\mathcal{N}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) is \(\tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})\), so a ball of radius \(\eta\) restricted to shifts in the mean is the ellipsoid of Equation 28.1 with \(\varepsilon = 2\eta\). On the empirical distribution the ball is larger, and Section 28.2 shows what the extra room buys. Anderson et al. (2003) calibrate \(\eta\) by the probability that the sample would be mistaken for the worst-case model, the counterpart of Equation 28.6.

Between the average and the worst case lies the smooth ambiguity model of Klibanoff et al. (2005), in which the investor holds a second-order distribution \(\pi\) over the models and evaluates a portfolio as \(\mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[\phi\!\left(\mathbb{E}_q[u(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{r})]\right)\right]\) with \(\phi\) concave. A linear \(\phi\) recovers the Bayesian average, and the limit of infinite concavity recovers the maxmin rule, so ambiguity aversion becomes a parameter rather than a switch; this chapter traces that parameter through Equation 28.6.

28.1.5 Resampling, and what kind of uncertainty it treats

Michaud (1989) proposed to replace the frontier by its average over resampled inputs: draw \((\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_b, \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_b)\) from the sampling distribution, solve the frontier on each draw, and average the weights rank by rank,

\[ \bar{\boldsymbol{w}}_k = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \boldsymbol{w}_k\!\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_b, \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_b\right), \qquad k = 1, \dots, K , \tag{28.10}\]

where \(k\) indexes the frontier portfolios from minimum variance to maximum return (Michaud and Michaud 2008). The result is a frontier of more diversified portfolios that move less when the sample changes, which is the property the block bootstrap of Section 9.3 showed the tangency portfolio lacks. It is an average over parameter draws and therefore a parameter-uncertainty device: every draw comes from the same model, and no draw asks what happens if that model is wrong. Scherer (2002) adds the critique that it is not the Bayesian answer either, since the expectation of an optimizer is not the optimizer of an expectation, and that averaging long-only weights pulls every portfolio toward the center of the simplex regardless of what the data say. Measuring it beside the maxmin rules shows the difference between averaging and guarding.

28.2 Four Robustifications on the Sixteen Classes

The quadratic programs below are those of Section 8.2, written out so that the loops of this chapter run in seconds; the PortfolioAnalytics specification is kept beside them as the check. Risk aversion is \(\gamma = 3\) throughout, the default of Stöckl (2020), and every number is annualized from monthly moments.

qp_simplex <- function(Dmat, dvec, nm) {       # max d'w - w'Dw/2 on the simplex
  n <- length(dvec)
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = Dmat * 1e4, dvec = dvec * 1e4,
                            Amat = cbind(rep(1, n), diag(n)),
                            bvec = c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)
  w <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  setNames(w / sum(w), nm)
}
gmv_qp <- function(S) qp_simplex(2 * S, rep(0, ncol(S)), colnames(S))
mv_qp  <- function(mu, S, gamma) qp_simplex(gamma * S, mu, names(mu))   # eq. 03 utility
tan_qp <- function(mu, S) {                     # min y'Sy s.t. y'mu = 1, y >= 0; w = y / sum(y)
  n <- length(mu)
  if (max(mu) <= 0) return(gmv_qp(S))
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S * 1e4, dvec = rep(0, n),
                            Amat = cbind(mu * 1e2, diag(n)), bvec = c(1, rep(0, n)), meq = 1)
  y <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  setNames(y / sum(y), names(mu))
}
target_qp <- function(mu, S, target) {          # one frontier point
  n <- length(mu)
  sol <- quadprog::solve.QP(Dmat = 2 * S * 1e4, dvec = rep(0, n),
                            Amat = cbind(rep(1, n), mu * 1e2, diag(n)),
                            bvec = c(1, target * 1e2, rep(0, n)), meq = 2)
  w <- pmax(sol$solution, 0)
  setNames(w / sum(w), names(mu))
}
p_vol <- function(w, V = S) sqrt(as.numeric(t(w) %*% V %*% w)) * sqrt(12)
p_ret <- function(w, m = mu) sum(m * w) * 12
p_sr  <- function(w) p_ret(w) / p_vol(w)
turnover <- function(w1, w2) sum(abs(w1[names(w2)] - w2)) / 2

w_gmv <- gmv_qp(S)
w_tan <- tan_qp(mu, S)
base_spec <- portfolio.spec(assets = colnames(Re)) |>
  add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
  add.constraint(type = "long_only")
w_pa <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(
  Re, add.objective(base_spec, type = "risk", name = "var"), optimize_method = "ROI"))
stopifnot(max(abs(w_pa[colnames(Re)] - w_gmv)) < 1e-4)

28.2.1 The resampled frontier

Each draw resamples the 372 months in twelve-month blocks, as Section 9.3 does, so that the serial dependence a naive bootstrap would destroy is kept (Künsch 1989; Politis and Romano 1994). On each draw we solve the long-only frontier at 25 equally spaced targets between the draw’s own minimum-variance return and its highest mean, and average the weights by rank as Equation 28.10 prescribes. The resampled tangency portfolio is the average of the draws’ tangency portfolios.

set.seed(1602)
block_index <- function(n, block = 12L) {
  starts <- sample.int(n - block + 1L, ceiling(n / block), replace = TRUE)
  as.vector(outer(0:(block - 1L), starts, "+"))[seq_len(n)]
}
frontier_of <- function(m, V, K = 25L) {
  lo <- sum(gmv_qp(V) * m); hi <- max(m)
  targets <- lo + (hi - lo) * seq(0, 0.995, length.out = K)   # the vertex itself is degenerate
  t(vapply(targets, function(tg) target_qp(m, V, tg), numeric(length(m))))
}

K <- 25L; B <- 200L
fr_sample <- frontier_of(mu, S, K)
boot <- lapply(seq_len(B), function(b) {
  X <- zoo::coredata(Re)[block_index(Tn), ]
  m <- colMeans(X); V <- stats::cov(X)
  list(W = frontier_of(m, V, K), tan = tan_qp(m, V))
})
W_res     <- Reduce(`+`, lapply(boot, `[[`, "W")) / B
tan_draws <- t(vapply(boot, function(b) b$tan, numeric(N)))
w_res_tan <- colMeans(tan_draws)[colnames(Re)]
stopifnot(!anyNA(W_res), identical(colnames(W_res), colnames(Re)),
          abs(sum(w_res_tan) - 1) < 1e-8)

The resampled tangency holds 9 classes where the sample tangency holds 4, and the two are 13% of the portfolio apart. The block bootstrap of Section 9.3 measured how far a draw moves the tangency portfolio; here the mean distance of a draw’s tangency from the sample tangency is 21%, and from the resampled tangency 23%. The resampled portfolio is the arithmetic mean of the draws, which minimizes their squared distance and not the trading distance, so it is not a portfolio the draws cluster around any more tightly. What it does is diversify: it pays 2.1% of the in-sample Sharpe ratio for that, and no point on the resampled frontier reaches the sample tangency’s ratio, which is Scherer (2002)’s point that the averaged frontier is dominated at the moments it was averaged under. Whether it is dominated at the moments that follow is the question of Section 28.3.

28.2.2 Random portfolios as a picture of the feasible set

Section 13.3 used random_portfolios() as a solver and measured it at a fraction of the attainable optimum. Here it is a different instrument: a sample from the feasible set, drawn three ways, so that the frontier can be seen against the set it is the edge of. The sample engine of rp_sample() perturbs weights one at a time under the full constraint list; the simplex engine of rp_simplex() draws uniformly on the simplex and then biases the draw toward its faces, edges and vertices through the fev argument, with \(q = 2^{\text{fev}}\) concentrating the weights into fewer assets as it grows; rp_grid() lays a grid over the box and normalizes.

set.seed(1602)
rp_s <- rp_sample(base_spec, permutations = 2000)
rp_x <- rp_simplex(base_spec, permutations = 2000, fev = 0:5)
rp_g <- rp_grid(base_spec, permutations = 2000, normalize = TRUE)
c(sample = nrow(rp_s), simplex = nrow(rp_x), grid = nrow(rp_g))
#>  sample simplex    grid 
#>    1999    2004   65535
cloud <- function(W, kind, extra = NA_integer_) {
  stopifnot(ncol(W) == N)
  colnames(W) <- names(base_spec$assets); W <- W[, colnames(Re)]
  tibble::tibble(kind = kind, layer = "cloud", fev = extra,
                 vol  = sqrt(rowSums((W %*% S) * W)) * sqrt(12),
                 ret  = as.numeric(W %*% mu) * 12,
                 dist = rowSums(abs(W - rep(w_tan, each = nrow(W)))) / 2,
                 n_pos = rowSums(W > 1e-6))
}
clouds <- dplyr::bind_rows(cloud(rp_s, "sample"),
                           cloud(rp_x, "simplex", rep(0:5, each = nrow(rp_x) / 6L)),
                           cloud(rp_g, "grid"))
stopifnot(nrow(rp_x) %% 6L == 0L)

Nothing a cloud contains comes within 15% of the tangency portfolio in trading distance, and the best member of the best cloud reaches 92% of its Sharpe ratio: the frontier is the edge of the set, and a sample of the set does not reach its edge. The three engines sample it differently. The simplex draws spread across the interior at fev = 0, where every portfolio holds all 16 classes, and move to the faces as fev rises, reaching a median of 2 positions at fev = 5. The grid engine in sixteen dimensions has two levels per asset, so its 65,535 portfolios are exactly the equal-weighted subsets of the classes, which is why its cloud is a lattice. That is a useful picture of the feasible set and a poor search of it, which is the finding of Section 13.3 seen from the other side.

Noterandom_portfolios() ignores a factor-exposure constraint

A factor-exposure constraint bounds \(\boldsymbol{B}'\boldsymbol{w}\), the portfolio’s loading on a set of factors. Take the betas of the sixteen classes on their equal-weighted average, the single factor of Section 5.2, and cap the portfolio beta below the tangency portfolio’s.

mkt  <- rowMeans(zoo::coredata(Re))
beta <- apply(zoo::coredata(Re), 2, function(x) stats::cov(x, mkt) / stats::var(mkt))
beta_tan <- sum(w_tan * beta)
spec_fe <- base_spec |>
  add.constraint(type = "factor_exposure", B = beta, lower = 0.2, upper = 0.4)

set.seed(1602)
rp_fe   <- random_portfolios(spec_fe, permutations = 2000, rp_method = "sample")
rp_fe_x <- rp_simplex(spec_fe, permutations = 2000, fev = 0:5)
violates <- function(W) { e <- as.numeric(W %*% beta); mean(e < 0.2 | e > 0.4) }
c(generated = nrow(rp_fe), sample_violating = violates(rp_fe), simplex_violating = violates(rp_fe_x))
#>         generated  sample_violating simplex_violating 
#>           158.000             1.000             0.992
fe_spec <- spec_fe |>
  add.objective(type = "return", name = "mean") |>
  add.objective(type = "risk",   name = "StdDev")
w_fe_random <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(Re, fe_spec, optimize_method = "random",
                                                 rp = rp_fe, maxSR = TRUE, trace = FALSE))
w_fe_roi    <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(Re, fe_spec, optimize_method = "ROI", maxSR = TRUE))
c(tangency = beta_tan,
  random = sum(w_fe_random[colnames(Re)] * beta), ROI = sum(w_fe_roi[colnames(Re)] * beta))
#> tangency   random      ROI 
#>   0.4884   0.4993   0.4000

The documentation says which constraints the generators check — leverage, box, group, position limit and leverage exposure — and factor exposure is not among them. The audit confirms it: of the 158 portfolios the default call keeps after the budget elimination described in Section 13.2, 100% breach the bound, as do 99% of the simplex draws, and the grid engine honors only the box by its own account. Nor does the objective catch it: constrained_objective() penalizes budget, box, group, position, diversification and turnover violations and has no term for factor exposure, so optimize_method = "random" returns a portfolio with a beta of 0.50 against a cap of 0.4, without a warning, while ROI returns one at the cap exactly. A factor-exposure constraint is a linear constraint and belongs on the convex path of Appendix C.

28.2.3 The multi-prior portfolio

Equation 28.5 and Equation 28.4 are closed forms, and glPF() in Stöckl (2020) implements the same problem. We write the derivation out, check it against a direct maximization of Equation 28.3, and compare it with the package.

gamma <- 3
A <- as.numeric(t(rep(1, N)) %*% solve(S, rep(1, N)))
Bs <- as.numeric(t(rep(1, N)) %*% solve(S, mu))
C <- as.numeric(t(mu) %*% solve(S, mu))
D <- C - Bs^2 / A
eps_of <- function(p, T_ = Tn, N_ = N) N_ * (T_ - 1) / (T_ * (T_ - N_)) * stats::qf(p, N_, T_ - N_)

mp_closed <- function(eps, gamma) {           # eq. 28-fully-invested, weights of any sign
  w_mv <- solve(S, rep(1, N)) / A
  spec_dir <- solve(S, mu - (Bs / A) * rep(1, N))
  phi <- if (eps == 0) 1 / gamma else
    stats::uniroot(function(phi) phi * (gamma + sqrt(eps) / sqrt(1 / A + phi^2 * D)) - 1,
                   c(0, 1 / gamma), tol = 1e-12)$root
  list(w = setNames(as.numeric(w_mv + phi * spec_dir), names(mu)), shrink = phi * gamma)
}
robust_objective <- function(w, eps, gamma) {
  sg <- sqrt(as.numeric(t(w) %*% S %*% w))
  sum(w * mu) - sqrt(eps) * sg - gamma / 2 * sg^2
}

eps_half <- eps_of(0.5)

               method = "BFGS", control = list(reltol = 1e-14, maxit = 2000))

At the median radius \(\varepsilon(0.5) = 0.043\) the shrinkage factor \(\gamma/\gamma^{*}\) is 0.22: the speculative component of Equation 28.5 is cut to about a fifth before the ellipsoid has reached even odds of containing the true mean. The derived weights agree with the direct maximization to 1e-12. glPF() returns the same weights at \(\varepsilon = 0\) and, at this radius, weights that differ by up to 0.077 and attain a lower value of Equation 28.3, so the chapter keeps the derived solution.

Under the long-only constraint of every portfolio in this book the closed form is gone and the fixed point remains: solve the quadratic program of Equation 3.12 at \(\gamma^{*}\), read off \(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}}\), update \(\gamma^{*}\), repeat.

mp_long_only <- function(mu, S, eps, gamma, tol = 1e-8, maxit = 200L) {
  w <- mv_qp(mu, S, gamma); g_star <- gamma
  for (i in seq_len(maxit)) {
    g_star <- gamma + sqrt(eps) / sqrt(as.numeric(t(w) %*% S %*% w))
    w_new  <- mv_qp(mu, S, g_star)
    if (max(abs(w_new - w)) < tol) { w <- w_new; break }
    w <- w_new
  }
  structure(w, gamma_star = g_star, iterations = i)
}

Stöckl (2022) turns Equation 28.6 into a time series by replacing the confidence level with the \(p\)-value of the turbulence of Equation 5.6: a month far from the estimated mean, in the metric of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\), is evidence that the mean is poorly known, and the ellipsoid widens. The radius is a monotone transform of the month’s turbulence, with a rank correlation of 1.00, and 50% of the variation in its logarithm is the logarithm of the month’s cross-sectional dispersion alone. In 32% of the months the radius exceeds the squared Sharpe ratio and Equation 28.4 holds nothing risky. A time-varying ambiguity of this kind is therefore largely a volatility-timing rule: it widens the set of models when the month was volatile, and Kritzman and Li (2010) show what such a rule buys as a risk signal. Whether it also measures something volatility does not is the question Stöckl (2022) asks of the equity premium, and it cannot be settled on a modeled market.

28.2.4 The Kullback–Leibler ball

The reference model is the empirical distribution of the 372 months. For a portfolio, Equation 28.8 is a one-dimensional root in \(\theta\); the robust portfolio maximizes the tilted mean less the variance penalty, a concave problem, by Newton steps in which the curvature of the worst-case mean is \(\theta\) times the covariance under the tilt.

Xm <- zoo::coredata(Re)
kl_tilt <- function(w, eta, X = Xm) {
  x <- as.numeric(X %*% w)
  q_of  <- function(th) { a <- -th * x; a <- a - max(a); q <- exp(a); q / sum(q) }
  kl_of <- function(th) { q <- q_of(th); q <- q[q > 0]; sum(q * log(q * length(x))) }
  theta <- if (eta <= 0) 0 else
    stats::uniroot(function(th) kl_of(th) - eta, c(0, 5000), tol = 1e-10)$root
  q <- q_of(theta)
  list(q = q, theta = theta, mu_q = setNames(as.numeric(t(X) %*% q), colnames(X)))
}

kl_robust <- function(eta, gamma, X = Xm, tol = 1e-8, maxit = 100L) {
  m0 <- colMeans(X); V <- stats::cov(X); n <- length(m0)
  J <- function(w) sum(kl_tilt(w, eta, X)$q * as.numeric(X %*% w)) -
    gamma / 2 * as.numeric(t(w) %*% V %*% w)
  w <- mv_qp(m0, V, gamma); j_old <- J(w)
  for (i in seq_len(maxit)) {
    tl <- kl_tilt(w, eta, X)
    Xc <- sweep(X, 2, tl$mu_q) * sqrt(tl$q)
    Cq <- crossprod(Xc)                                  # covariance under the tilt
    w_new <- qp_simplex(gamma * V + tl$theta * Cq,
                        tl$mu_q + tl$theta * as.numeric(Cq %*% w), names(m0))
    step <- 1; j_new <- J(w_new)
    while (j_new < j_old - 1e-12 && step > 1e-4) {      # damp a step that overshoots
      step <- step / 2; w_new <- w + step * (w_new - w); j_new <- J(w_new)
    }
    if (max(abs(w_new - w)) < tol) { w <- w_new; break }
    w <- w_new; j_old <- j_new
  }
  tl <- kl_tilt(w, eta, X)
  structure(w, theta = tl$theta, mu_q = tl$mu_q, iterations = i)
}

etas <- c(0.01, 0.05, 0.2)
kl   <- lapply(etas, kl_robust, gamma = gamma)
w_plugin <- mv_qp(mu, S, gamma)

The ball does to the portfolio what the ellipsoid did: the distance to minimum variance falls as the radius grows, to 7% at \(\eta = 0.2\). The route is different. Nature’s mark-down is not proportional to \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\) as in Equation 28.2 but to each class’s covariance with the months in which the portfolio lost most. EU_equity, a class the plug-in portfolio holds, is marked down by 13 percentage points a year at \(\eta = 0.05\), and the classes with the longest left tails of Section 4.3 are marked down beyond what their covariance implies, which the fourth exercise measures. Even the smallest radius moves the portfolio by more than half its weight, because the plug-in portfolio at \(\gamma = 3\) is the all-equity corner that Equation 28.5 also abandoned first. For the application the radius is set by the Gaussian correspondence \(\eta = \varepsilon/2\), so that the two robust portfolios guard against sets of the same size and differ only in their shape.

28.3 Robustness Out of Sample

The six portfolios are re-estimated on every sixty-month window and held for the month that follows, from January of model year 11, the first month the register of Section 25.3 scores, with the metrics computed as that register computes them. The resampled tangency uses 100 draws per window with a small ridge on each draw’s covariance, since five twelve-month blocks can coincide; the multi-prior portfolio uses \(\varepsilon(0.5)\) of Equation 28.6 at \(T = 60\), and the ball \(\eta = \varepsilon(0.5)/2\). The certainty equivalent is \(\bar{r} - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\hat{\sigma}^2\) at \(\gamma = 3\), annualized, in excess of the bill.

score <- function(rule) {
  eop <- NULL; gross <- turn <- numeric(length(ends))
  for (j in seq_along(ends)) {
    w  <- as.numeric(rules[[j]][[rule]])
    rt <- Rm[ends[j] + 1L, ]
    turn[j]  <- if (is.null(eop)) NA_real_ else 0.5 * sum(abs(w - eop))
    gross[j] <- sum(w * rt)
    eop <- w * (1 + rt) / (1 + gross[j])                 # drift to the next rebalance
  }
  rfo <- rf[ends + 1L]; n <- length(gross)
  ann_return <- prod(1 + gross)^(12 / n) - 1
  ann_rf     <- prod(1 + rfo)^(12 / n) - 1
  x <- xts::xts(gross, order.by = d[ends + 1L])
  tibble::tibble(strategy = rule,
                 sharpe = (ann_return - ann_rf) / (stats::sd(gross) * sqrt(12)),
                 ce = 12 * mean(gross - rfo) - gamma / 2 * 12 * stats::var(gross),
                 ann_turnover = mean(turn, na.rm = TRUE) * 12,
                 max_drawdown = as.numeric(PerformanceAnalytics::maxDrawdown(x)),
                 ann_vol = stats::sd(gross) * sqrt(12))
}

The register rows reproduce to within 5e-05 for the two strategies fitted on excess returns and within 0.001 for minimum variance, which the register fits on total returns. By Sharpe ratio the order is Minimum variance, Multi-prior, p = 0.5, Equal weight, KL ball, eta = eps/2, Tangency, Resampled tangency.

Each robustification buys something different. The two maxmin portfolios move the tangency portfolio part of the way to minimum variance, and the data pay for the distance traveled: the multi-prior portfolio raises the Sharpe ratio from 0.55 to 0.65, cuts turnover from 0.82 to 0.50 of the portfolio a year and the drawdown from 20% to 16%, and the ball of the same size does slightly less of each. Neither reaches minimum variance, which needs no mean at all and finishes first of the six here, as it finishes ahead of every rule that estimates a mean in every cell of the register Section 8.3 reads. The resampled tangency buys nothing here: averaging over draws moves weight away from US_government, which anchors the tangency portfolio, from 57% on average to 47%, and the result is more volatile, has a lower Sharpe ratio than the plug-in portfolio it was meant to stabilize, a larger drawdown, and a turnover of 0.73, because the average of a hundred unstable answers is itself re-estimated every month. Equal weighting has the highest certainty equivalent at \(\gamma = 3\), as DeMiguel et al. (2009) would predict, and the lowest turnover; it is the one rule here that guards against model uncertainty by holding no model.

The lesson is the one Equation 28.5 states: a worst case over means is an increase in risk aversion, and what it buys is what a higher risk aversion buys, in proportion to the size of the set. The set is a confidence region, and on sixty months of sixteen classes the region is already wide enough at even odds to move most of the way to the portfolio that ignores the means.

Key Takeaways

  • Parameter uncertainty is a distribution over the inputs and is treated by averaging; model uncertainty is a set of models with no distribution over it and is treated by a worst case (Gilboa and Schmeidler 1989). The first produces shrinkage of the estimate, the second shrinkage of the portfolio.
  • The multi-prior investor of Garlappi et al. (2007) holds the mean-variance portfolio of an investor with effective risk aversion \(\gamma^{*} = \gamma + \sqrt{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{\boldsymbol{w}}\), and converges to minimum variance as the ellipsoid grows. Its radius Equation 28.6 is a confidence level, which makes it a parameter-uncertainty device in a model-uncertainty frame.
  • A Kullback–Leibler ball around the empirical distribution reweights the sample toward the portfolio’s bad months (Hansen and Sargent 2008); with a Gaussian reference it is the ellipsoid with \(\varepsilon = 2\eta\). The worst case marks down the classes with the worst tails rather than the most volatile.
  • Resampling averages over parameter draws. On this market the resampled tangency portfolio is more diversified in sample and dominated out of sample; it is not a robust portfolio in the sense of this chapter (Scherer 2002).
  • random_portfolios() is a picture of the feasible set and not a search of it, and it ignores a factor-exposure constraint at generation and in the objective; a bound on factor exposure belongs on the convex path.
