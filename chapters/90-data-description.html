Appendix A — Data Description

This appendix documents the modeled market in full. Chapter 2 (Section 2.2) introduces it and says why the book runs on it; chapter 4 (Section 4.2) measures its properties. Here we record what each shipped object contains, how it was constructed, and what it cannot support. Everything below is computed from the objects as they ship in tidypm, so the appendix cannot drift from the data.

The dataset is modeled and contains no real data. Factor models for government bonds, credit, equities and commodities were estimated by historical simulation on market data from 1992 to 2022; the factors were replayed, the idiosyncratic components drawn afresh, and the asset paths reassembled, so that no series is the history of a real security. The market therefore runs on its own calendar, model years 1 to 31, read with the tools of Section 2.2.1. Its stress episodes are named for what happens in them and ship as sim_episodes. And because every series is the output of a factor model, the market can be re-run with its factors shocked, which a history cannot, and Appendix H does so.

objects <- c("sim_assets",

  x <- get(o)

}

The return tables and the exchange rates cover model years 1 to 31 exactly. The weekly bill and the curve start one week before the first weekly return, and the capitalizations extend past both ends of the sample, so that a lagged capitalization exists for the first return.

A.1 Class Series

The allocation universe is the sixteen risky class series in sim_class_returns_monthly: five currencies, each with a government bond, a credit and an equity series, plus one global commodity index. The five money-market series ship beside them in sim_money_market_monthly and are not part of the universe, for the reason given in Section 2.4. Both tables carry date, class, return (a simple total return in decimal) and index (a total-return index with base 100 one period before the first observation).

R  <- as_xts_pm(sim_class_returns_monthly, date, class, return)
M  <- as_xts_pm(sim_money_market_monthly, date, class, return)
rf <- sim_riskfree_monthly$return
d  <- zoo::index(R)
stopifnot(ncol(R) == 16L, ncol(M) == 5L,
          identical(d, zoo::index(M)),
          all(d == sim_riskfree_monthly$date))

# The index is the compounded return series, in both tables.
rebuilt <- dplyr::bind_rows(sim_class_returns_monthly, sim_money_market_monthly) |>
  dplyr::group_by(class) |>
  dplyr::arrange(date, .by_group = TRUE) |>
  dplyr::mutate(rebuilt = 100 * cumprod(1 + return)) |>
  dplyr::ungroup()
stopifnot(max(abs(rebuilt$rebuilt - rebuilt$index)) < 1e-8)

c(months = nrow(R), first_model_year = min(model_year(d)),
  last_model_year = max(model_year(d)))
#>           months first_model_year  last_model_year 
#>              372                1               31

Weighting. Equity class series are value-weighted on the market capitalization of the previous weekly observation, so no portfolio is formed with information it could not have had; government, credit, money-market and commodity series are equally weighted across their instruments, because no capitalization exists for them. The portfolio is rebalanced weekly, and the monthly return is the compounded weekly one, so the monthly series is a weekly-rebalanced portfolio observed at month ends rather than a monthly-rebalanced one.

The money market is a duration asset. Each *_money_market series is the 0.25-year point on a Nelson–Siegel curve fitted to 2-, 5- and 10-year yields, marked to market. The US series tracks the bill closely but is not the bill:

mm <- as.numeric(M[, "US_money_market"])
c(mm_negative_months   = mean(mm < 0),
  bill_negative_months = mean(rf < 0),
  correlation          = stats::cor(mm, rf))
#>   mm_negative_months bill_negative_months          correlation 
#>             0.188172             0.002688             0.930009

Abroad, a three-month instrument has almost no duration, so unhedged in dollars it is close to a pure currency position:

vapply(c("EU", "UK", "CH", "JP"), function(rg) {
  fx <- sim_fx_monthly[sim_fx_monthly$region == rg, ]
  stats::cor(as.numeric(M[, paste0(rg, "_money_market")]),
             fx$fx_return[match(zoo::index(M), fx$date)])
}, numeric(1))
#>     EU     UK     CH     JP 
#> 0.9970 0.9953 0.9984 0.9994

Episodes. Four stress episodes are kept apart throughout the book because they are economically different. They ship as sim_episodes, each named for what happens in it, and in_episode() selects the dates of one, so that no chapter types a window (Section 2.2.1).

Table A.3 gives the cumulative return of every class through each episode, compounded over the months in_episode() selects.

Every figure is in dollars and unhedged, so a foreign class carries its currency’s move with it. In the equity bear market all five equity classes fall, the worst by 55%, while four of the five government classes gain. In the credit crisis all five equity classes and four of the five credit classes fall, and US_government gains 8%. The liquidity shock lasts three months, in which every equity and credit class and fourteen of the sixteen classes in all fall. In the rates shock every government and credit class falls, together with four of the five equity classes, and the commodity index gains 12%.

A.2 Instrument Series

sim_asset_returns_monthly and sim_asset_returns_weekly hold the same market at instrument level, one column per asset, and sim_assets describes each instrument.

Ra <- as_xts_pm(sim_asset_returns_monthly, date, asset, return)
stopifnot(ncol(Ra) == nrow(sim_assets), !anyNA(Ra),
          setequal(colnames(Ra), sim_assets$asset_id))
dim(Ra)
#> [1] 372 181

Identifiers are anonymized (US_EQU_01, EU_CRE_03, GLOBAL_CMD_02): a region, an asset class and a running number. Government bonds are zero-coupon instruments held at constant maturities of 2, 5 and 10 years; credit is the same three maturities in two grades (credit_grade is IG or HY); the money-market instrument is the 0.25-year point on the regional curve; the eight commodities are a broad index plus gold, energy, precious metals, silver, agriculture, industrial metals and a crypto series. Equities carry a sector.

Conditioning. Three maturities per region are priced off a three-factor curve, and the two credit grades at each maturity share one credit factor, so some bond combinations are near-exact replicas of others. The instrument-level covariance is therefore close to singular, which is why every instrument-level optimization in Part VI needs a constraint or a shrinkage estimator (Section 7.1, Section 21.1).

ev  <- eigen(stats::cov(Ra), symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
ev16 <- eigen(stats::cov(R), symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
condition <- c(instruments_log10_condition = log10(max(ev) / min(ev)),
               classes_log10_condition     = log10(max(ev16) / min(ev16)))
stopifnot(min(ev) > 0)                # positive definite, but only just
condition
#> instruments_log10_condition     classes_log10_condition 
#>                      12.196                       4.105

A.3 Risk-Free Rate and Exchange Rates

sim_riskfree_monthly and sim_riskfree_weekly are a three-month Treasury bill carried onto the model’s grid: rate_annual is the quoted annual rate and return the simple return over one period. The bill enters the model as an input, and it is the risk-free rate everywhere in the book (Section 3.1).

c(annualized = ann_ret(rf), negative_months = sum(rf < 0),
  min_rate = min(sim_riskfree_monthly$rate_annual),
  max_rate = max(sim_riskfree_monthly$rate_annual))
#>      annualized negative_months        min_rate        max_rate 
#>         0.02262         1.00000        -0.00010         0.06190

sim_fx_monthly and sim_fx_weekly hold, for the four non-US regions, the value of one unit of local currency in dollars (rate, weekly only) and its return fx_return. Like the bill, the exchange rates enter the model as inputs. The quote direction matters: a positive fx_return means the local currency appreciated against the dollar, which is a gain for an unhedged dollar investor holding the local asset. Subtracting fx_return from an unhedged local return gives the hedged return, which is how the book hedges (Section 4.3).

sim_fx_monthly |>
  dplyr::group_by(region) |>
  dplyr::summarise(ann_vol = ann_vol(fx_return),
                   ann_return = ann_ret(fx_return), .groups = "drop")

#> 2 EU      0.0953   -0.00907

sim_curve_weekly holds the drivers of the bond block: Nelson–Siegel level, slope and curvature for each region, plus an investment-grade and a high-yield credit spread per region. Chapter 22 (Section 22.1) decomposes bond returns on them.

sim_marketcap_weekly holds the market capitalization of the 123 equities on the weekly grid, which is what a capitalization-weighted benchmark needs (Section 24.3, Section 21.2).

sim_esg_changes_quarterly holds changes in an ESG rating of the equities: the quarter end (date), the instrument (asset) and an integer esg_change. It carries changes only, no starting level, and the changes have no effect on the returns.

A.4 Known Limitations

The dataset is frozen at version 6.2.0. The idiosyncratic components of every instrument are one draw, so a regenerated export is a different realized path rather than an update, and every number in this book is pinned to this one. The limitations below are properties of the model and are stated rather than repaired.

equity_ids <- sim_assets$asset_id[sim_assets$asset_class == "equity"]
equity_vol <- stats::quantile(apply(Ra[, equity_ids], 2, ann_vol), c(0.25, 0.5, 0.75))

overlay    <- 0.03
overlay_se <- overlay / (ann_vol(as.numeric(R[, "EU_equity"])) / sqrt(nrow(R) / 12))

# Share of variance the currency hedge removes from each foreign bond class.
foreign_bonds <- grep("^(UK|EU|CH|JP)_(government|credit)$", colnames(R), value = TRUE)
hedge_share <- vapply(foreign_bonds, function(s) {
  fx <- sim_fx_monthly[sim_fx_monthly$region == sub("_.*$", "", s), ]
  stopifnot(all(fx$date == d))
  u <- as.numeric(R[, s])
  1 - stats::var(u - fx$fx_return) / stats::var(u)
}, numeric(1))
stopifnot(overlay_se < 1, length(foreign_bonds) == 8L, all(hedge_share > 0.5))

round(c(equity_vol, overlay_in_se = overlay_se,
        hedge_share_min = min(hedge_share), hedge_share_max = max(hedge_share)), 2)
#>             25%             50%             75%   overlay_in_se hedge_share_min 
#>            0.26            0.31            0.37            0.79            0.63 
#> hedge_share_max 
#>            0.92
  • One realized path. The factor paths are fixed; only the idiosyncratic components are drawn. The market as shipped is one path, not a distribution over paths: its factors can be shocked (Appendix H), but the data hold no second draw of them.
  • The euro-area equity overlay. Every euro-area equity carries an expected-return overlay of three percentage points a year, added as a constant weekly drift. It is a stated capital-market assumption, 0.8 standard errors of the sample mean, made so that the class is not excluded by every mean-variance optimizer on the sample. No other series carries one.
  • Single securities. The equity series are individual securities with a median volatility of 31 percent, half of them between 26 and 37 percent, and their idiosyncratic components are drawn independently. A portfolio of them diversifies more readily than a real index would.
  • Currency. Everything is in dollars, unhedged. For a non-US bond the currency is the larger part of the risk, between 63 and 92 percent of the variance of the eight foreign bond classes, and sim_fx_* exists so that the hedged position can always be formed.
  • Near rank deficiency. The bond block is nearly rank-deficient at instrument level: the covariance matrix of the 181 instruments has a condition number of \(10^{12.2}\). Unconstrained mean-variance on all of them produces nonsense, which is worth demonstrating rather than preventing.
  • Derived Swiss credit. No Swiss credit series with usable history exists, so the Swiss credit loadings are derived from the other regions rather than measured.
  • No fundamentals, no ESG levels. The dataset carries no firm fundamentals, and of ESG ratings only the 1113 changes of sim_esg_changes_quarterly, not the levels, so cumulating them gives a rating up to an unknown constant. Chapter 20 (Section 20.1) and the value-factor material of Part VI therefore work with what returns, capitalizations and rating changes can support.
  • Nominal only. There is no inflation series; the book works in nominal dollars throughout (Section 3.1).
