Appendix B — Proofs

Draft|To comment, select a passage and annotate it; see how reviews work.

Parts II to IV state results and show what they do to a portfolio. The derivations behind them are collected here. Part VIII derives in place and contributes nothing to this appendix.

Throughout, \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) is the vector of expected excess returns, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) the covariance matrix of those excess returns, assumed positive definite, \(\boldsymbol{\iota}\) a vector of ones, and \(\boldsymbol{w}\) the vector of portfolio weights. Three scalars recur:

\[ A = \boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}, \qquad B = \boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}, \qquad C = \boldsymbol{\mu}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}, \qquad D = AC - B^2 . \tag{B.1}\]

\(A\) and \(C\) are positive because \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\) is positive definite, and \(D > 0\) whenever \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) is not proportional to \(\boldsymbol{\iota}\), by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied in the inner product induced by \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\). If every asset has the same expected return then \(D = 0\), the frontier collapses to a single point, and the problem below has no interior solution. These results are due to Merton (1972).

B.1 The Minimum-Variance Portfolio

Claim. The fully invested portfolio of least variance is

\[ \boldsymbol{w}_{\text{mv}} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}}{A}, \qquad \text{with variance} \qquad \sigma^2_{\text{mv}} = \frac{1}{A}. \]

Proof. Minimize \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\) subject to \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\iota} = 1\). With multiplier \(2\lambda\) the Lagrangian is

\[ \mathcal{L} = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} - 2\lambda\left(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\iota} - 1\right), \]

and the first-order condition is \(2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} - 2\lambda\boldsymbol{\iota} = \boldsymbol{0}\), so \(\boldsymbol{w} = \lambda\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}\). Imposing the budget constraint gives \(\lambda\,\boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota} = \lambda A = 1\), hence \(\lambda = 1/A\) and the stated weights. The objective is strictly convex, so the stationary point is the unique minimum. Its variance is \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} = \lambda^2 A = 1/A\). \(\square\)

The vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) does not appear. This is the property that makes the minimum-variance portfolio the most stable point on the frontier in practice, since it is exactly the input that is estimated worst.

B.2 The Frontier

Claim. The least variance attainable at expected excess return \(\bar{\mu}\) is

\[ \sigma^2(\bar{\mu}) = \frac{A\bar{\mu}^2 - 2B\bar{\mu} + C}{D}. \]

Proof. Minimize \(\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\) subject to \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} = \bar{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\iota} = 1\). The first-order condition is \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} = \lambda\boldsymbol{\mu} + \gamma\boldsymbol{\iota}\), so that

\[ \boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \left(\lambda\boldsymbol{\mu} + \gamma\boldsymbol{\iota}\right). \tag{B.2}\]

Substituting into the two constraints gives the linear system

\[ \begin{pmatrix} C & B \\ B & A \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \\ \gamma \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \bar{\mu} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \lambda = \frac{A\bar{\mu} - B}{D}, \quad \gamma = \frac{C - B\bar{\mu}}{D}. \]

The variance follows from Equation B.2:

\[ \sigma^2 = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{w}'\left(\lambda\boldsymbol{\mu} + \gamma\boldsymbol{\iota}\right) = \lambda\bar{\mu} + \gamma = \frac{A\bar{\mu}^2 - B\bar{\mu} + C - B\bar{\mu}}{D} = \frac{A\bar{\mu}^2 - 2B\bar{\mu} + C}{D}. \qquad \square \]

The expression is a parabola in \((\sigma^2, \bar{\mu})\) space and a hyperbola in \((\sigma, \bar{\mu})\) space. Differentiating, \(\sigma^2\) is minimized at \(\bar{\mu} = B/A\), where it equals

\[ \frac{A(B/A)^2 - 2B(B/A) + C}{D} = \frac{C - B^2/A}{D} = \frac{1}{A}, \]

recovering the minimum-variance portfolio of the previous section as the vertex.

B.3 The Tangency Portfolio

Claim. With returns measured in excess of the risk-free rate, the fully invested portfolio of maximum Sharpe ratio is

\[ \boldsymbol{w}_{\text{tan}} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}}{B}, \qquad \text{attaining} \qquad \max_{\boldsymbol{w}} \frac{\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu}} {\sqrt{\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}}} = \sqrt{C}. \]

Proof. The Sharpe ratio \(S(\boldsymbol{w}) = \boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} / (\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w})^{1/2}\) is homogeneous of degree zero: \(S(c\boldsymbol{w}) = S(\boldsymbol{w})\) for any \(c > 0\). The budget constraint therefore fixes only the scale, not the direction, and we may maximize without it and rescale afterwards. Differentiating,

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial \boldsymbol{w}} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w})^{1/2}} - \frac{(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu})\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}} {(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w})^{3/2}} = \boldsymbol{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol{\mu} = \frac{\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu}} {\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}}\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}, \]

so \(\boldsymbol{w} \propto \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\). Rescaling so that the weights sum to one divides by \(\boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu} = B\). Evaluating the ratio at this point gives \(S^2 = (\boldsymbol{\mu}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu})^2 / (\boldsymbol{\mu}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}) = C\). \(\square\)

Two consequences are worth noting. The solution requires \(B \neq 0\); if \(\boldsymbol{\iota}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu} = 0\) no fully invested tangency portfolio exists, and the ray from the risk-free rate is asymptotic to the frontier rather than touching it. And \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) enters premultiplied by \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\), which is the algebraic source of the instability documented by Best and Grauer (1991): when \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is close to singular, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\) is large, and small changes in estimated means produce large changes in weights.

B.4 Two-Fund Separation

Claim. Every frontier portfolio is a linear combination of the minimum-variance portfolio and the tangency portfolio. Consequently any two distinct frontier portfolios span the entire frontier.

Proof. From Equation B.2, every frontier portfolio has the form \(\boldsymbol{w} = \lambda\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu} + \gamma\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}\), a linear combination of the two vectors \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\iota}\). These are, up to scale, the tangency and minimum-variance portfolios. The frontier is therefore a two-dimensional affine family, and any two distinct members with different expected returns form a basis for it. \(\square\)

When a risk-free asset is available, Tobin (1958) sharpens this. An investor maximizing \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\) with the remainder held in cash has first-order condition \(\boldsymbol{\mu} = \gamma\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\), so \(\boldsymbol{w} = \gamma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\). Risk aversion changes only the scale of the risky holding, never its composition. Every investor holds the same risky portfolio, which is what allows Sharpe (1964) to aggregate individual choices into a market equilibrium.

B.5 Quadratic Utility and the Mean-Variance Problem

Claim. Maximizing \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\mu} - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\) over \(\boldsymbol{w}\) traces the same frontier as Equation 9.2 as \(\gamma\) varies over \((0, \infty)\).

Proof. The first-order condition of the unconstrained problem is \(\boldsymbol{\mu} - \gamma\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{0}\), giving \(\boldsymbol{w} = \gamma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\). Under the budget constraint the same Lagrangian argument as in the previous section yields \(\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\gamma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu} + \gamma_0\boldsymbol{\iota})\) for a multiplier \(\gamma_0\), which is Equation B.2 with \(\lambda = \gamma^{-1}\). Each \(\gamma\) therefore selects one frontier point, and the map from \(\gamma\) to \(\bar{\mu}\) is monotone. \(\square\)

WarningThe risk-aversion argument in PortfolioAnalytics is \(\gamma/2\), not \(\gamma\)

This matters whenever a result is compared against a textbook formula. The package’s quadratic utility objective builds \(\boldsymbol{Q} = 2\lambda\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) and hands it to ROI, whose quadratic program is stated as \(\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}'\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{x}\). The factors do not cancel: the effective coefficient on \(\boldsymbol{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{w}\) is \(\lambda\), whereas the convention above puts \(\gamma/2\) there. Setting risk_aversion = 2 therefore solves the problem a textbook would write with \(\gamma = 4\). Verified by numerical fit, where the ratio is exactly 2.000 at every level tested. The behavior is undocumented upstream and is recorded in Appendix D.
