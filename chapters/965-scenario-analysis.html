Appendix H — Scenario Analysis

What happens to a portfolio if yields rise by a hundred basis points, or if the dollar appreciates by ten percent against every currency? A scenario is a statement about a few factors, and the question is what it implies for every asset that loads on them. Meucci (2008) frames a scenario as a change of probabilities on a panel of outcomes; Kupiec (1998) places stress scenarios inside the same distributional framework as value at risk, so that a scenario has a probability and a loss rather than only a loss; Breuer et al. (2009) ask which scenarios are at once plausible, severe and useful, and answer with the worst loss inside an ellipsoid of plausible factor moves; Glasserman et al. (2015) select scenarios by empirical likelihood; Rebonato (2010) argues that the factor moves of a scenario must be coherent with one another, which a model of their joint behavior supplies and a list of shocks does not; and Alexander and Sheedy (2008) show how a market risk model supplies that coherence. Every one of these starts from the same object: a mapping from factor moves to asset returns. In the modeled market the mapping exists by construction, because every series is the output of a factor model (Section 2.2), so a shock to a factor moves every series that loads on it.

The generator itself is not shipped. What ships are the factor layers: the level, slope and curvature of each region’s curve and its two credit spreads in sim_curve_weekly, the exchange rates in sim_fx_weekly, the five regional equity classes and the commodity index. From them this appendix estimates a mapping \(\boldsymbol{B}\) from factor shocks to the return of every instrument and every class, and then shocks it. Three things should be said plainly. The mapping is estimated from the shipped data, by regression, and checked against the analytical durations of Section 22.1 where those exist. It is linear and one-period: a scenario is a vector of simultaneous factor moves, and the response is \(\boldsymbol{B}\) times that vector. And it is a tool for asking what moves if, not a re-run of the generator: the idiosyncratic draws, the convexity of the bonds and the correlation between factors are outside it, and Section H.3 measures what that costs by pushing the four episodes of sim_episodes through the mapping.

H.1 Factor Exposures

The factor set has thirty-five members. For each region there are the weekly changes of the level, the slope and the curvature of its curve and of its investment-grade and high-yield spreads; for each non-dollar region the return of its currency; for each region the return of its equity market in local currency, the class return less the currency’s move by Equation 2.4, so that the equity factor and the currency factor say different things; and the return of the commodity index. The curve series are read in the units Section 22.2 established: the level, slope and curvature are in percentage points and the spreads in decimals, so the first three are divided by a hundred and a loading is a duration in years.

lsc <- grep("_(Level|Slope|Curvature)$", names(cv), value = TRUE)

Which factors an instrument may load on is a design decision, not a search: a government bond loads on its region’s three curve factors, a credit bond also on the spread of its grade, a money-market instrument on the curve, an equity on its local market, a commodity on the commodity index, and every non-dollar instrument on its currency. The sixteen class series are treated the same way, except that a credit class loads on both spreads of its region. The exposures are the coefficients of a regression of weekly returns on those factor changes, with Newey–West standard errors as in Section 5.2, and everything is indexed by name.

meta <- dplyr::bind_rows(

factors_of <- function(region, kind, grade) {
  curve <- paste0(region, c("_Level", "_Slope", "_Curvature"))
  ccy   <- if (region %in% foreign) paste0(region, "_fx")
  spread <- if (is.na(grade)) paste0(region, c("_IG_Spread", "_HY_Spread")) else paste0(region, "_", grade, "_Spread")
  switch(kind, government = c(curve, ccy), money_market = c(curve, ccy),
         credit = c(curve, spread, ccy), equity = c(paste0(region, "_equity"), ccy),
         commodity = "GLOBAL_commodity")
}
expose <- function(y, cols) {
  m  <- stats::lm(y ~ ., data = data.frame(y = y, F[, cols, drop = FALSE]))
  nw <- sandwich::NeweyWest(m, lag = 6, prewhite = FALSE)
  b  <- stats::coef(m)
  list(alpha = b[[1]], beta = b[-1], t = (b / sqrt(diag(nw)))[-1],
       r2 = suppressWarnings(summary(m)$r.squared))          # an identity row fits exactly
}

For the bonds the exposures are known before any regression is run. A zero-coupon bond of maturity \(\tau\) has, by Equation 22.2, a loading of minus its duration on the yield at its maturity, and the shipped factors are the rotation of the two-, five- and ten-year yields that Section 22.2 identified, the observable level, slope and curvature of Diebold and Li (2006): the level is the ten-year yield, the slope the two-year less the ten-year, and the curvature twice the five-year less the two ends. The loadings of the three yields on the three factors are therefore

B_emp <- rbind(`2` = c(level = 1, slope = 1, curvature = 0),
               `5` = c(1, 0.5, 0.5), `10` = c(1, 0, 0))
B_emp
#>    level slope curvature
#> 2      1   1.0       0.0
#> 5      1   0.5       0.5
#> 10     1   0.0       0.0

and the analytical exposure of a bond of maturity \(\tau\) is \(-D(\tau)\) times its row, with \(D(\tau) = \tau / (1 + \bar y(\tau))\) the modified duration under annual compounding at the mean yield; a credit bond adds a spread duration of the same size (Merton 1974), and a non-dollar bond a loading of one on its currency. The latent loadings of Nelson and Siegel (1987) at the Diebold–Li decay are a different rotation of the same three yields, shown in Section 22.2; the mapping uses the rotation the data ship. The maturities are inferred as in that chapter, from the level loading, and the regression is required to recover the analytical level loading within ten percent.

bonds <- meta |>
  dplyr::filter(level == "instrument", kind %in% c("government", "credit")) |>
  dplyr::mutate(b_level = B[cbind(name, paste0(region, "_Level"))]) |>
  dplyr::group_by(region, kind, grade) |>
  dplyr::mutate(maturity = c(10, 5, 2)[rank(b_level)]) |>          # most negative: the ten-year
  dplyr::ungroup()
stopifnot(all(table(bonds$region, bonds$maturity) == 3L))

  mean(as.matrix(cv[, paste0(rg, c("_Level", "_Slope", "_Curvature"))]) %*% B_emp[as.character(tau), ])
bonds <- bonds |>
  dplyr::mutate(D = maturity / (1 + mapply(ybar, region, maturity)),
                a_level = -D * B_emp[as.character(maturity), "level"],

                a_curv  = -D * B_emp[as.character(maturity), "curvature"],
                a_spread = ifelse(kind == "credit", -D, NA_real_),
                b_slope = B[cbind(name, paste0(region, "_Slope"))],
                b_curv  = B[cbind(name, paste0(region, "_Curvature"))],
                b_spread = ifelse(kind == "credit",
                                  B[cbind(name, paste0(region, "_", ifelse(is.na(grade), "IG", grade), "_Spread"))],

stopifnot(all(abs(bonds$b_level / bonds$a_level - 1) < 0.10))

In Table H.1 the two columns agree within 4 percent on the level for every bond of every region, and within 0.5 years on the slope, the curvature and the spread: the regression recovers the durations the theory states, the near rank deficiency of Section 7.1 seen one bond at a time. Across all 15 government and 30 credit instruments the lowest \(R^2\) is 0.970, and every non-dollar bond loads on its currency within five percent of one. The 123 equities are the other extreme: a median beta on the local market of 0.95 and a median \(R^2\) of 0.32, the rest being the idiosyncratic draw that no factor carries (Figure H.1).

At class level the rows of \(\boldsymbol{B}\) are what a scenario needs, and Table H.2 collects them. The equity classes and the commodity index are themselves factor layers, so their rows are identities by construction: a foreign equity class is one times its local market plus one times its currency. What the mapping adds is the bond block, where a class is an equally weighted ladder of three maturities and its row is, by Equation 22.3, the average of its instruments’ rows, which the chunk above asserts on the returns themselves. The credit rows are built that way for a second reason: the two spreads of a region share one credit factor (Section 22.1), their weekly changes correlate at 0.96 or more, and a regression of the class on both cannot tell their loadings apart, whereas each credit instrument loads on the spread of its own grade. The regression at class level is kept as the check, and its \(R^2\) is the last column.

H.2 Shocks and Their Propagation

A scenario is a named vector of factor shocks, zero wherever it is silent, and its propagation is one matrix product,

\[ \boldsymbol{r}^{\text{scenario}} = \boldsymbol{B}\,\boldsymbol{s}, \tag{H.1}\]

with \(\boldsymbol{s}\) the shock vector in the factors’ own units: a yield or spread change in decimals, a currency, equity or commodity move as a return. The function refuses a shock whose names do not match the columns of \(\boldsymbol{B}\), for the reason Section 9.2 gives.

shock <- function(...) {
  s <- setNames(rep(0, ncol(F)), colnames(F)); v <- c(...)
  stopifnot(all(names(v) %in% names(s)))
  s[names(v)] <- v; s
}
propagate <- function(shock, B) {
  stopifnot(identical(names(shock), colnames(B)))
  drop(B %*% shock)
}
scenarios <- list(
  "curves +100 bp" = shock(setNames(rep(0.01, 5), paste0(regions, "_Level"))),
  "spreads +200 bp, equities -15%" = shock(
    setNames(rep(0.02, 10), paste0(rep(regions, each = 2), c("_IG_Spread", "_HY_Spread"))),
    setNames(rep(-0.15, 5), paste0(regions, "_equity"))),
  "dollar +10%" = shock(setNames(rep(-0.10, 4), paste0(foreign, "_fx"))),
  "equities -25%, curves -50 bp" = shock(
    setNames(rep(-0.25, 5), paste0(regions, "_equity")),
    setNames(rep(-0.005, 5), paste0(regions, "_Level"))))
S_class <- sapply(scenarios, propagate, B = B[colnames(R), ])
stopifnot(identical(rownames(S_class), colnames(R)))

The four scenarios are a parallel rise of one hundred basis points in every curve, which moves the level alone because the slope and the curvature are differences of yields; a widening of every credit spread by two hundred basis points with every equity market down fifteen percent in local currency; an appreciation of the dollar that takes ten percent off the dollar value of every other currency; and an equity sell-off of twenty-five percent with every curve rallying fifty basis points. They are received by the policy portfolio of Section 15.3, recomputed as that chapter builds it, a tangency portfolio across sleeves that are a tangency portfolio for equity and minimum variance for government and credit, and by three strategies of the register of Section 25.3 at full-sample weights: long-only minimum variance, long-only tangency and equal weight.

Table H.3 and Figure H.2 read the mapping across the four scenarios, one factor layer at a time. A parallel rise of one hundred basis points costs each of the ten bond classes its duration times one percent, between 5.3 and 5.5 percent, and moves nothing else. The minimum-variance portfolio, which holds 82 percent of US_government, loses 4.5 percent and is the worst of the four portfolios, against 3.4 for equal weight: the least volatile portfolio of Section 9.3 is a concentrated position in the one factor this scenario shocks. The spread widening with equities down fifteen percent is the credit crisis in miniature. Each credit class loses its spread duration times two percent, between 10.5 and 10.9 percent, every equity class its fifteen, and the ranking reverses: equal weight loses 8.0 percent and minimum variance 1.9. The dollar scenario touches no US series and the commodity index and takes ten percent from every other class, so it is a test of home bias: equal weight, with twelve of its sixteen series abroad, loses 7.5 percent, the policy portfolio 1.5 and minimum variance 0.8. The sell-off with a rally is the only scenario in which the bond block gains, 2.6 to 2.8 percent on fifty basis points, and the 29 percent the policy portfolio holds in equity costs it 5.4 percent against 1.0 for minimum variance. In three of the four scenarios minimum variance loses least and equal weight most; in the parallel rise it is the other way round. Every number in the table is a weighted sum of a row of Table H.2, and that is the point: a scenario return can be traced to the loading that produced it.

What a linear one-period mapping leaves out is listed rather than hidden. It has no convexity, so a large yield move is priced by the first-order term of Equation 22.2 alone and the loss on a rise is overstated, the gain on a fall understated. It holds the exposures fixed, whereas Longin and Solnik (2001) and Ang and Chen (2002) show that correlations rise in falling markets and Chow et al. (1999) build the scenario portfolio on exactly that distinction between good and bad regimes. It has no second round: a spread widening that forces selling, or a currency move that changes a central bank’s policy, is a path and not a vector. And it carries no idiosyncratic draw, so an instrument’s return under a scenario is its systematic return only, which for the median equity is 32 percent of its variance. The last two limits are what the turbulence measure of Kritzman and Li (2010) and the coherent scenarios of Rebonato (2010) are built to address, and the next section measures the first and the last.

H.3 Scenarios Against the Episodes

The validation treats each episode of sim_episodes as a scenario the market actually ran. The realized factor moves over an episode’s weeks are the sum of the weekly changes for the curve factors and the spreads and the compounded return for the currencies, the equity markets and the commodity index; pushed through \(\boldsymbol{B}\), with each regression’s constant times the number of weeks added, which for a bond is mostly its carry, they give the cumulative return the mapping predicts. The realized returns are the ones Appendix A tables for the classes, which the chunk asserts, and their counterparts for the instruments.

lv <- c(lsc, grep("_Spread$", names(cv), value = TRUE))        # factors that are changes
rt <- setdiff(colnames(F), lv)                                  # factors that are returns
episode_shock <- function(e) {
  i <- which(in_episode(wk, e))
  shock(colSums(F[i, lv]), apply(1 + F[i, rt, drop = FALSE], 2, prod) - 1)
}
fit <- dplyr::bind_rows(lapply(sim_episodes$episode, function(e) {
  i <- which(in_episode(wk, e)); m <- in_episode(zoo::index(R), e)
  realized <- apply(1 + Y[i, ], 2, prod) - 1
  stopifnot(max(abs(realized[colnames(R)] - (apply(1 + R[m, ], 2, prod) - 1))) < 1e-8)
  tibble::tibble(episode = sim_episodes$label[sim_episodes$episode == e], name = meta$name,
                 level = meta$level, kind = meta$kind,
                 predicted = propagate(episode_shock(e), B) + alpha * length(i),
                 realized = realized[meta$name])
})) |> dplyr::mutate(episode = factor(episode, sim_episodes$label))
r2_line <- function(y, yhat) 1 - sum((y - yhat)^2) / sum((y - mean(y))^2)   # against the 45-degree line
fit_tab <- fit |>
  dplyr::group_by(episode) |>
  dplyr::summarise(
    r2_classes     = r2_line(realized[level == "class"], predicted[level == "class"]),
    r2_instruments = r2_line(realized[level == "instrument"], predicted[level == "instrument"]),
    miss_class     = name[level == "class"][which.max(abs(realized - predicted)[level == "class"])],
    miss_instrument = name[level == "instrument"][which.max(abs(realized - predicted)[level == "instrument"])],
    .groups = "drop")

Table H.4 and Figure H.3 give the answer. Across the sixteen classes the mapping explains between 97.4 and 99.7 percent of the variation in cumulative returns, measured against the 45-degree line without refitting, and the misses have names. In the equity bear market the class the mapping misses by most is JP_equity, whose realized return lies 7.3 percentage points above the prediction over the 30 months of the episode: a foreign class is its local market times its currency and the mapping adds them, so the longer the window and the larger the moves, the larger the cross term it leaves out. In the credit crisis it is UK_credit, which lost 28.0 percent against a predicted 34.5: the UK high-yield spread widened by 737 basis points, the largest move of the four episodes, and a spread duration alone overstates the loss on a move of that size by the convexity term of Equation 22.2, which a spread has as much as a yield. In the rates shock the five government classes sit just below the line, by 0.7 percentage points on average over the year: the mapping credits each bond with its sample-average carry, and the episode starts from a US level of 1.5 percent against a sample mean of 4.0. Across the instruments the \(R^2\) falls to between 0.14 and 0.61, and the largest miss in every episode is a single equity, by as much as 165 percentage points for one name over the equity bear market: the idiosyncratic draw, which carries the larger part of a single equity’s variance in Figure H.1 and which no scenario can reach. In that episode the mean absolute miss of the 123 equities is 30.4 percentage points against 4.4 for the bonds. The mapping is, then, what it was said to be: exact for the factor layers, exact for the bonds up to convexity, carry and compounding, and silent on the draw.

The stress test of the mandate of Section 12.3 closes the appendix. The mandate is minimum variance under seven linear lines, fully invested, no series above 35 percent, equities between 40 and 60 percent, government at least 10, credit at most 40, commodities at most 15 percent, and an expected excess return of at least six percent a year, with the position limit of eight imposed afterwards by the screen of that chapter. The lines are added one at a time from the long-only minimum-variance portfolio of Section 9.3, so that the line that accounts for a difference can be read off.

grp   <- split(seq_along(colnames(Re)), sleeve_of(colnames(Re)))

min_var <- function(spec, X = Re) {
  w <- extractWeights(optimize.portfolio(X, spec |> add.objective(type = "risk", name = "var"),
                                         optimize_method = "ROI"))
  stopifnot(!anyNA(w)); w
}
lines <- function(assets, with_group = TRUE, with_floor = TRUE) {
  g <- split(seq_along(assets), sleeve_of(assets))
  s <- portfolio.spec(assets = assets) |>
    add.constraint(type = "full_investment") |>
    add.constraint(type = "box", min = 0, max = 0.35)
  if (with_group) s <- s |> add.constraint(type = "group", groups = g,
                                           group_min = unname(m_min[names(g)]),
                                           group_max = unname(m_max[names(g)]))
  if (with_floor) s <- s |> add.constraint(type = "return", return_target = floor_ann / 12)
  s
}

Table H.5 answers the question the mandate was written for. In one of the four scenarios the mandate loses less than the long-only minimum-variance portfolio: the parallel rise, 3.0 against 4.5 percent, and the rung that accounts for it is the class limits. The cap alone changes nothing in that scenario, because the weight it releases from US_government goes to other bonds of the same duration; the class limits require forty percent in equities and so cut the bond block from 83 to 58 percent of the portfolio, and with it the duration. The same lines are what make the mandate lose more in the other three. In the spread widening the cap is the largest step, from 1.9 to 4.5 percent, because the weight it releases goes in part to US_credit, 27 percent of the portfolio at that rung against none before, which carries the spread duration the scenario shocks. In the sell-off the class limits are, taking the loss from 0.5 to 8.4 percent, which is what a forty percent equity floor means in a sell-off of twenty-five percent. And in the dollar scenario the return floor runs the other way, reducing the loss by 1.8 percentage points, because the expected return it demands is bought with US_equity, which rises from 17 to 25 percent at the expense of the foreign series the scenario hits. A mandate is a set of exposures the committee has chosen without naming them, and the table names them: the equity floor is a duration ceiling and a sell-off exposure, the cap is a decision to hold the spread duration the minimum-variance portfolio would not, and the return floor is a dollar position.

That is what a reader can do with this appendix. A scenario is a vector, shock() names its entries, \(\boldsymbol{B}\) carries one row per instrument and per class, and propagate() is one matrix product: any scenario a committee can write as factor moves, any portfolio written as weights by name, and any exposure matrix, the regression betas of Section 5.2 or the durations of Section 22.2, can be substituted in a line. The book’s data make both sides inspectable, and the four episodes say how far to trust the result: to within a few percentage points for the factor layers and the bonds, and not at all for the draw.
